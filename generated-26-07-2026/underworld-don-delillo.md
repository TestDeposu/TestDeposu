# Underworld

**Author:** Don DeLillo  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

**Underworld by Don DeLillo: A Grand Sweep Through America's Heart of Darkness**

Don DeLillo's magnum opus, "Underworld," is a literary tour de force that encapsulates the spirit of America from the 1950s to the 1990s. This sprawling, ambitious novel is not just a story; it's a symphony, a tapestry, a grand opera of American history, told through the lives of its characters and the objects that bind them.

At its heart, "Underworld" is a meditation on the American experience, a deep dive into the nation's psyche as it grapples with the Cold War, the Civil Rights Movement, Vietnam, and the cultural shifts that define each decade. DeLillo masterfully weaves these historical events into the fabric of his narrative, not as mere backdrop, but as integral elements that shape his characters' lives and decisions.

The novel begins with a moment of legend: the "Shot Heard Round the World" baseball game between the New York Giants and the Brooklyn Dodgers, which coincides with news of the Soviet Union's first hydrogen bomb test. This moment, charged with both triumph and dread, sets the tone for the rest of the novel. From here, DeLillo follows the trajectory of two main characters, Nick Shay and Klara Sax, as their lives intersect and diverge against the backdrop of a changing America.

DeLillo's prose is dense, poetic, and often breathtaking. He has a knack for capturing the essence of a moment, a place, a feeling, in a way that is both precise and evocative. His sentences are long, complex, and often run-on, reflecting the sprawling, interconnected nature of the world he's describing. Yet, despite their length, they are never confusing; rather, they draw the reader in, inviting us to linger in the moment, to savor the detail, to feel the weight of history.

One of the novel's most striking features is its use of objects - a baseball, a photograph, a painting - as symbols that connect the characters and their stories. These objects, passed from hand to hand, become repositories of memory, history, and meaning. They are, in many ways, the heart of the novel, the threads that bind the tapestry together.

"Underworld" is not an easy read. It's long, complex, and often challenging. But it's also deeply rewarding. DeLillo's ability to capture the essence of a moment, a place, a feeling, is unparalleled. He doesn't just tell a story; he creates a world, a universe, a history. And he invites us to step inside, to experience it for ourselves.

In the end, "Underworld" is a novel about America, about the American experience, about the American dream. It's a novel about the things we create, the things we destroy, and the things we leave behind. It's a novel about the underworld, the hidden histories, the dark hearts of men and nations. And it's a novel that will stay with you long after you've turned the last page.
