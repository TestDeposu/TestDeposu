# The Painted Girls

**Author:** Cathy Marie Buchanan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

**The Painted Girls: A Tale of Sisterhood and Survival in Belle Époque Paris**

Cathy Marie Buchanan's "The Painted Girls" is a captivating historical novel that weaves a tale of two sisters navigating the treacherous landscape of late 19th-century Paris. The story, set against the backdrop of the Paris Opéra and the burgeoning art scene, explores the themes of sisterhood, vulnerability, and the exploitation of women in a society that values their beauty above all else.

The novel opens with the sudden death of the van Goethem sisters' father, leaving them and their laundress mother in dire financial straits. In a desperate bid to keep their family afloat, Marie is sent to train at the Paris Opéra, while Antoinette finds work as an extra in a stage adaptation of Émile Zola's "L'Assommoir." Buchanan masterfully captures the harsh realities of their lives, painting a vivid picture of the poverty and hardship that shaped the sisters' experiences.

At the heart of the novel lies the complex and tender bond between Marie and Antoinette. Buchanan's prose beautifully captures the sisters' love and loyalty, as well as the inevitable tensions that arise from their differing paths. Marie's passion for dance and her rise as a muse for Edgar Degas contrast sharply with Antoinette's descent into the Parisian demimonde, yet neither sister judges the other. Instead, they cling to each other, forming a bulwark against the storms that threaten to tear their lives apart.

Buchanan's exploration of the darker side of the Belle Époque is both compelling and unsettling. She delves into the exploitation of young women, both by the ballet and the art world, and the limited options available to those who dared to defy societal norms. Through Marie's experiences as a model for Degas, Buchanan highlights the objectification of women, even as they are elevated to the status of muses. Meanwhile, Antoinette's story serves as a stark reminder of the precarious nature of women's lives in a society that values their virtue above all else.

"The Painted Girls" is not only a compelling narrative but also a meticulously researched historical novel. Buchanan's attention to detail brings the world of late 19th-century Paris to life, from the grimy backstage of the Paris Opéra to the bohemian studios of the Impressionists. Her exploration of the art world, and her use of real-life figures such as Degas and Zola, adds depth and authenticity to the story.

Buchanan's prose is elegant and evocative, her characters complex and fully realized. She writes with a keen eye for detail and a deep understanding of the historical context, creating a novel that is both engaging and thought-provoking. "The Painted Girls" is a powerful exploration of sisterhood, survival, and the price of beauty in a society that demands it.
