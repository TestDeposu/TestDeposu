# Doing Justice: A Prosecutor's Thoughts on Crime, Punishment, and the Rule of Law

**Author:** Preet Bharara  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

**Title: "Doing Justice": A Compelling Insight into the Legal System by Preet Bharara**

In "Doing Justice", Preet Bharara, the former US Attorney for the Southern District of New York, offers a rare, insider's perspective on the American legal system. Bharara's debut book is not just a memoir, but a profound exploration of the complexities, challenges, and triumphs of the rule of law. It's a must-read for anyone interested in understanding the intricate dance of justice in the world's most powerful democracy.

Bharara's style is engaging and accessible, making complex legal concepts palatable to the layperson. He weaves personal anecdotes from his career with broader discussions on crime, punishment, and the rule of law. The narrative is peppered with cases he prosecuted, from high-profile white-collar crimes to street-level drug trafficking, each serving as a microcosm to explore larger themes.

The book's main theme is the delicate balance of power and justice in a society. Bharara argues that the rule of law is not a given, but a fragile construct that requires constant vigilance and protection. He doesn't shy away from the system's flaws, acknowledging that it's human-made and thus imperfect. However, he also emphasizes the importance of faith in the system, urging readers to accept its imperfections and work towards improvement.

Bharara's prose is clear and concise, yet rich with nuance. He has a knack for explaining complex legal principles in simple, everyday language. His writing is also marked by a dry wit, making the book an enjoyable read despite its heavy subject matter.

One of the book's strengths is Bharara's ability to humanize the legal system. He doesn't just discuss cases in abstract terms, but paints vivid portraits of the people involved - victims, perpetrators, and everyone in between. This personal touch makes the book's themes resonate on a deeper level.

However, the book is not without its criticisms. Some may find Bharara's perspective too prosecutorial, lacking in defense or alternative viewpoints. Others might argue that the book could delve deeper into the systemic issues plaguing the American legal system.

Despite these minor criticisms, "Doing Justice" is a compelling read that offers a unique insight into the American legal system. It's a call to action, urging readers to engage with the system, understand its complexities, and work towards its improvement.
