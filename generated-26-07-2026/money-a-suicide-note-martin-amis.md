# Money: A Suicide Note

**Author:** Martin Amis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2025

In the scathing and darkly comedic world of Martin Amis's "Money," the pursuit of wealth and status is a never-ending, self-destructive cycle. Originally published in 1984, this classic novel has been re-released with a new introduction by Lorrie Moore, serving as a poignant reminder of the timelessness of its themes. Amis's razor-sharp prose cuts through the illusions of success, laying bare the trappings of materialism and the corrupting influence of late-stage capitalism.

At the center of this maelstrom is John Self, a London-based commercial director with a penchant for booze, pornography, and prostitutes. His life is a hedonistic blur, driven by the relentless pursuit of pleasure and the accumulation of wealth. But when he receives an invitation from the enigmatic filmmaker Fielding Goodney to collaborate on his first feature film, "Good Money," John sees an opportunity to transcend his mundane existence and make a name for himself in the cutthroat world of Hollywood.

As John navigates the surreal landscape of 1980s America, he is forced to confront the darker aspects of his own nature. His degenerative proclivities threaten to destroy the life he's built, and he must choose between his baser instincts and the possibility of redemption. Through John's struggles, Amis masterfully skewers the excesses of capitalist culture, revealing the empty, loveless lives of those who worship at the altar of wealth and status.

Amis's writing is a marvel of linguistic precision, his prose slicing through the façade of respectability to expose the rot beneath. His characters are flawed, often despicable, and yet, somehow, they are also strangely relatable. This is a testament to Amis's skill as a storyteller, his ability to craft a narrative that is both repellent and fascinating.

One of the most striking aspects of "Money" is its relevance to contemporary society. Despite being written over three decades ago, the novel's themes of capitalist excess, social dislocation, and the corrupting influence of wealth remain eerily prescient. In an era where income inequality, social media obsession, and the cult of celebrity have become endemic, Amis's critique of late-stage capitalism feels more urgent than ever.

If you're looking for a novel that will challenge your assumptions, make you laugh, and leave you feeling uneasy, then "Money" is the perfect choice. With its new introduction by Lorrie Moore, this re-release is a timely reminder of the power of great literature to disturb, provoke, and ultimately, transform us.
