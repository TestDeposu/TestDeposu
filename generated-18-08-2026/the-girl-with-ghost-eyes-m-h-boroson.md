# The Girl with Ghost Eyes

**Author:** M.H. Boroson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

M.H. Boroson’s *The Girl with Ghost Eyes* drops readers straight into the neon‑lit alleys of 1890s San Francisco Chinatown, where incense smoke curls around paper talismans and the clatter of kung‑fu practice echoes from hidden courtyards. At its heart, the novel is a quiet yet fierce meditation on belonging—how a young widow named Li‑lin negotiates the weight of her father’s legacy, the stigma of her yin eyes, and the expectations of a community caught between old‑world Daoist rites and the rush of a modernizing America. Boroson makes Li‑lin’s inner conflict the engine of the plot: every spirit she banishes, every gangster she out‑fights, is also a step toward reclaiming agency in a world that would rather see her silent and subservient.

What sets the novel apart is Boroson’s seamless weaving of martial‑arts choreography with authentic Chinese magic. The peachwood sword, the burning talismans, and the wisecracking eyeball spirit that rides in Li‑lin’s pocket are not mere genre props; they feel like extensions of Daoist cosmology, rendered with respect and a dash of playful irreverence. The author’s prose is crisp and kinetic, mirroring the rapid footwork of Li‑lin’s kung‑fu while still pausing for lyrical moments—descriptions of moonlit rooftops, the scent of sandalwood incense, or the sting of shame that lingers after a failed exorcism. This balance keeps the narrative brisk without sacrificing emotional depth; the reader feels the pulse of each fight and the ache of each personal loss.

Boroson also excels at world‑building through detail rather than exposition. The novel’s Chinatown feels lived‑in: crowded markets, clandestine gambling halls, the uneasy coexistence of traditional herbalists and newly arrived telegraph operators. By grounding the supernatural in this specific historical slice, the author avoids the pitfall of generic “Asian‑flavored” fantasy and instead offers a texture that is both exotic and intimately familiar. The humor—often delivered by the eyeball spirit’s sarcastic asides—acts as a pressure valve, preventing the darker themes of widowhood, familial duty, and looming apocalypse from overwhelming the story.

Ultimately, *The Girl with Ghost Eyes* succeeds because it treats its protagonist not as a token “strong female lead” but as a fully realized person navigating intersecting pressures: cultural expectation, personal grief, and a mystical destiny that refuses to be ignored. Boroson’s style—energetic, respectful of tradition, yet unafraid to inject wit—makes the novel a standout entry in urban fantasy that feels both fresh and deeply rooted.
