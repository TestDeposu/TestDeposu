# Ishmael (Star Trek: The Original Series, #23)

**Author:** Barbara Hambly  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2000

**Ishmael: A Cosmic Whodunit That Redefines Time Itself**

Barbara Hambly’s *Ishmael*—the second *Star Trek* novel to feature the *Enterprise* crew—isn’t just another space adventure. It’s a masterclass in speculative fiction, weaving together the cerebral intrigue of a time-travel mystery with the moral complexity of a Klingon conspiracy. At its heart, this novel asks: *What if the future could be rewritten by a single act of violence?* And in doing so, Hambly crafts a story that lingers long after the last page, challenging readers to question the very fabric of causality.

The premise is audacious. Spock, the logical and unshakable Vulcan, vanishes mid-mission aboard a Klingon vessel, his last transmission a chilling whisper: *"Ishmael."* The Klingons, usually portrayed as ruthless warriors, are now hunters of history itself, convinced that killing a man from the past will alter the Federation’s destiny. The *Enterprise* crew—Captain Kirk, Dr. McCoy, and the ever-resourceful Uhura—must unravel a conspiracy that spans centuries, where the line between cause and effect blurs into something almost metaphysical. Hambly doesn’t just tell a story; she *plays* with time, bending it like a living thing to serve her narrative.

What makes *Ishmael* truly compelling is Hambly’s ability to balance *Star Trek*’s signature optimism with the grim, philosophical weight of her own creation. The Klingons aren’t just villains; they’re tragic figures, bound by a belief system that treats history as a game of dominoes—one wrong push, and everything falls. This isn’t just a race against time; it’s a race against the inevitability of fate. And yet, Hambly never lets the story become overly bleak. There’s a spark of hope, a reminder that even in the face of cosmic forces, human (and Vulcan) ingenuity can find a way.

Her prose is sharp, precise, and rich with detail—whether describing the cold efficiency of a Klingon starship or the quiet desperation of a man trapped in a loop of his own past. Hambly’s writing is never showy, but it’s always *felt*. She understands that the best science fiction isn’t about flashy effects; it’s about the ideas that stick with you. And *Ishmael* is packed with them: the ethics of altering history, the cost of absolute certainty, and the fragile line between heroism and hubris.

The novel’s strength lies in its characters. Kirk, ever the charismatic leader, is forced to confront a threat that defies conventional logic. McCoy’s medical expertise becomes a tool for survival in ways he never anticipated. And Spock, of course, remains the emotional core—his disappearance isn’t just a plot device; it’s a catalyst for the crew to question everything they thought they knew. Hambly doesn’t shy away from making Spock vulnerable, and in doing so, she deepens his character in a way that feels both true to the original series and entirely original.

The only minor quibble is that Hambly occasionally leans into the *Star Trek* lore a bit too heavily, which can feel like a crutch rather than an extension of the story. But this is a minor flaw in an otherwise brilliant execution. *Ishmael* isn’t just a *Star Trek* novel; it’s a standalone work of genius, one that stands alongside the best of its genre.

In the end, *Ishmael* is a reminder that the greatest mysteries aren’t always the ones we can solve—they’re the ones we’re forced to *live* with. And Hambly, with her deft storytelling and unflinching moral clarity, makes sure we do.
