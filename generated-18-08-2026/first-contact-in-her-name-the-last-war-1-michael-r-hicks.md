# First Contact (In Her Name: The Last War, #1)

**Author:** Michael R. Hicks  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

First Contact (In Her Name: The Last War, #1) by Michael R. Hicks drops readers straight into the thrill and terror of humanity’s inaugural brush with an alien civilization that is both awe‑inspiring and terrifyingly alien. The premise is deceptively simple: a survey ship discovers two habitable worlds, only to realize it has trespassed into the domain of the ancient Kreelan Empire—a matriarchal, sword‑wielding star‑spanning realm whose warriors view combat as sacred ritual. What follows is a brutal, high‑stakes trial by combat that leaves only one human survivor, Midshipman Ichiro Sato, to carry back a dire warning: war is inevitable.

Hicks’ greatest strength lies in his ability to marry the grand sweep of space opera with the intimate, visceral intensity of a gladiatorial arena. The opening chapters pulse with the optimism of exploration—bright ship corridors, eager crew banter, the tantalizing promise of new colonies—before the mood snaps into a cold, metallic dread as the Kreelan flagship looms. This tonal shift is handled with a sure hand; the author never lingers too long on exposition, instead letting the world‑building emerge through action, dialogue, and the stark contrast between human technology and Kreelan barbaric elegance. The alien society feels lived‑in: its hierarchy, rites, and the almost mythic reverence for Tesh‑Dar, the high warrior priestess, are revealed in snippets that feel both exotic and internally consistent.

Stylistically, Hicks writes in a lean, propulsive prose that favors short, punchy sentences during combat scenes, giving the reader a heartbeat‑matching rhythm. When he steps back to explore Ichiro’s inner turmoil—his grief, his dawning respect for his captors, and the forbidden spark of romance that flickers amidst the carnage—his language softens, allowing moments of introspection that deepen the emotional core without slowing the pace. The blend of military science fiction rigor with a dash of fantasy (the sword‑centric culture, the priestess’s quasi‑divine aura) creates a hybrid feel that will appeal to fans of both David Weber’s Honor Harrington series and the more mythic undertones of Frank Herbert’s Dune.

The central theme—first contact as a catalyst for inevitable conflict—resonates beyond the page. Hicks uses the encounter to comment on hubris, the peril of assuming benevolence in the unknown, and the ways cultural misunderstandings can ignite wars that neither side truly wants. Yet he avoids didacticism; the moral questions emerge organically from Ichiro’s struggle to reconcile his duty to Earth with the unexpected honor he finds among his foes. This tension fuels the narrative’s drive, making the book not just a romp through laser‑filled corridors but a meditation on the cost of survival when two civilizations clash on fundamentally different values.

In terms of pacing, the novel never feels stagnant. After the initial capture and arena sequence, the story expands into political intrigue aboard the Kreelan flagship, hints at larger galactic machinations, and seeds future installments of the In Her Name saga. Readers who crave relentless action will find plenty to satisfy, while those who appreciate layered world‑building and character growth will not be disappointed.

Overall, First Contact delivers a punchy, emotionally charged opening to a saga that promises epic scale, fierce alien cultures, and a human protagonist forced to become a messenger of doom. Hicks balances spectacle with substance, delivering a space opera that feels both fresh and reminiscent of the genre’s best traditions.
