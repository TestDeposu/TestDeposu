# Fluke: Or, I Know Why the Winged Whale Sings

**Author:** Christopher Moore  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Fluke: Or, I Know Why the Winged Whale Sings is Christopher Moore at his most delightfully off‑kilter, taking a genuine scientific mystery—why humpback whales sing—and turning it into a riotous, sun‑soaked romp that feels like a beach‑side cocktail party for the intellectually curious. The novel follows Nathan Quinn, a earnest Ph.D. in behavioral biology who has spent two decades chasing the elusive purpose of the whales’ haunting melodies alongside his laid‑back partner, Clay Demolocus. Their routine of recording songs off Maui is shattered when Nathan swears he sees the phrase “Bite Me” scrawled across a fluke, a hallucination (or perhaps a message) that propels the pair into a surreal investigation blending marine biology, government conspiracies, and outright absurdity.

At its heart, Fluke wrestles with the tension between wonder and the human need to explain the inexplicable. Moore uses the whale song as a metaphor for the mysteries that elude reductionist science—beauty that resists purpose, communication that may be more art than signal. Yet rather than delivering a solemn treatise, he lets the question dissolve into laughter, reminding readers that the pursuit of knowledge can be as much about the joy of the chase as the certainty of the answer. The theme echoes throughout the narrative: the characters’ quirky obsessions, the bizarre encounters with a rogue whale‑watching tour, a clandestine navy project, and a fateful encounter with a talking, winged whale all serve to underline that meaning often emerges in the unexpected, the ridiculous, and the profoundly human moments between data points.

Moore’s style is unmistakable: a laid‑back, Margaritaville‑infused voice that reads like a witty friend narrating over margaritas at sunset. He marries precise scientific jargon—bioacoustics, spectrograms, cetacean anatomy—with slapstick dialogue, pop‑culture asides, and a keen ear for the absurd. The prose flows with a breezy rhythm, punctuated by sudden, sharp bursts of insight that feel earned rather than forced. His humor is never merely decorative; it underscores the novel’s philosophical undercurrents, allowing the reader to linger on the awe of the ocean while simultaneously chuckling at the human propensity to overthink, over‑equip, and over‑dress a simple quest for understanding.

What makes Fluke particularly effective is how Moore balances reverence for the natural world with a healthy dose of skepticism. The whale’s song remains enigmatic, but the journey to uncover its source becomes a celebration of curiosity itself—a reminder that not every mystery needs a tidy solution to be valuable. Fans of Moore’s earlier work will recognize his signature blend of irreverence and heart, while newcomers will discover an author who can make marine biology feel as accessible and entertaining as a midnight surf session.

In short, Fluke is a clever, humorous, and surprisingly thoughtful romp that asks us to listen closely—not just to the whales, but to the laughter that bubbles up when we admit that some songs may simply be… for the fun of it.
