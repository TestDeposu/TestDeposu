# We3

**Author:** Grant Morrison  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2005

When Grant Morrison and Frank Quitely collaborated on WE3 in 2005, they did not merely create a comic book; they engineered a visceral, heartbreaking assault on the senses. Known best for their monumental work on All-Star Superman, this creative duo pivots from the golden heights of superhero mythology into the grimy, fluorescent-lit underground of military-industrial experimentation. The result is a breathless, devastating masterpiece that uses the language of extreme action to articulate a profound meditation on innocence, loyalty, and what it truly means to call a place home.

The premise is deceptively simple, echoing classic sci-fi creature features yet stripping away any camp. The government has taken three ordinary house pets—a dog named 1, a cat named 3, and a rabbit named 2—and surgically fused them into mechanized killing machines. Equipped with hyper-amplified nervous systems and devastating mechanical exoskeletons, they possess the combat capability of an entire battalion. They are designated as Animal Weapon 3, or WE3. But when their testing phase concludes, the creators decide to decommission them—a sterile word for execution. Terrified and confused, the trio breaks out, embarking on a desperate cross-country journey to find the one thing their altered minds still crave: home.

Morrison’s script is an exercise in stylistic daring. Dialogue is stripped to its absolute barest minimum, rendered in fragmented, halting telepathic bursts that reflect the broken cognitive states of animals forced to process human cruelty. This minimalist approach elevates the emotional resonance tenfold. Instead of sprawling exposition, Morrison relies on pure pacing and raw empathy. The tragedy of WE3 lies not in their formidable firepower, but in their utter vulnerability. They are weapons of mass destruction who still speak in the simple, heartbreaking vernacular of household pets, referring to humans as "HOME" and yearning for basic comfort. 

Yet, the true genius of WE3 lies in the symbiotic friction between Morrison’s narrative economy and Frank Quitely’s revolutionary visual storytelling. Quitely approaches the page not as a static canvas, but as a cinematic storyboard. The action sequences are nothing short of astonishing. Utilizing a relentless, multi-tiered grid system, Quitely mimics the rapid-fire aesthetic of anime and action cinema. Panels fracture, overlap, and explode across the page, capturing the frantic speed of ballistic missiles and the terrifying agility of the animal cyborgs. 

Despite the ultra-violence, Quitely imbues the animals with staggering emotional depth. Through subtle shifts in posture, wide pleading eyes, and the awkward weight of their heavy metallic suits, 1, 1s, and 3 feel painfully alive. You feel the claustrophobia of their armor and the stinging reality of a world that views them as disposable assets. The juxtaposition of hyper-detailed, brutal gore with the innocent, bewildered faces of a dog, cat, and rabbit creates an unbearable tension that lingers long after the final page.

At its core, WE3 is an unflinching critique of callous authority and the instrumentalization of life. The shadowy military agents hunting the trio operate with a chilling bureaucratic detachment, viewing sentient beings as mere prototypes to be replaced by newer models. Against this cold machinery of state violence, Morrison and Quitely pit the fierce, unyielding bond of friendship and survival. 

WE3 is a rare achievement in the medium—a graphic novella that marries high-octane spectacle with profound emotional devastation. It moves at the speed of a freight train, yet manages to break your heart with surgical precision. Morrison and Quitely remind us that even amidst the most monstrous dehumanization, the instinct for love and belonging remains indomitable. This is storytelling at its most daring, beautiful, and devastating.
