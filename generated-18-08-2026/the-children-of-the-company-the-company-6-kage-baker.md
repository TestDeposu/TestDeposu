# The Children of the Company (The Company, #6)

**Author:** Kage Baker  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2026

Kage Baker’s The Children of the Company stands as a masterclass in speculative fiction, a brilliant and biting continuation of a saga that redefines the boundaries of time-travel narratives. In this sixth installment, Baker returns to her richly textured universe where Dr. Lewis's infamous Dr. Zeus Corporation—operating as the clandestine Company—harvests the past not merely for the sake of historical preservation, but for cold, unyielding corporate profit. But what makes this volume particularly striking is how it shifts the lens from the weary heroism of the botanical preservationists and operatives we have come to know, focusing instead on the intoxicating, dangerous allure of institutional decay from the inside out.

At the heart of the novel is Executive Facilitator General Labienus, a character whose lineage of arrogance stretches back to the dust of ancient Sumeria, where he was foolishly worshipped as a deity. Baker uses Labienus to dissect the novel’s central, most provocative theme: the profound contempt of the immortal for the mortal. When you are engineered to live forever, guarding the fleeting, tragic art of creatures who will be dead in a blink, empathy is a luxury easily discarded. Labienus is not just a villain; he is the logical endpoint of a corporate culture built on exploitation and god-complexes. His calculated subversion—ranging from the mysterious shadows of the ADONAI black project to the trembling earth of pre-quake San Francisco, through the mercantile espionage of Amsterdam’s chocolate wars and the mist-shrouded Irish monasteries hiding the remnants of the Fair Folk—paints a panoramic portrait of a millennium-long mutiny. 

Baker’s literary style remains one of the sharpest in modern science fiction, blending hard-edged speculation with a deliciously dark, cynical humor. She possesses an uncanny ability to make historical epochs feel lived-in, vibrant, and tactile. Whether she is describing the sensory chaos of a Dutch trading floor or the eerie, quiet isolation of an Irish cloister, her prose is meticulously researched yet effortlessly readable. The juxtaposition of corporate bureaucracy with immortal ambition creates an absurdist corporate satire wrapped in high-stakes historical fantasy. You are treated to immortal operatives bickering over centuries-old slights while the fate of humanity hangs in the balance, a dynamic that keeps the narrative refreshingly human despite its superhuman cast.

As the timeline inexorably ticks forward toward the ominous and mysterious "Silence of 2355," the tension in The Children of the Company tightens like a noose. Baker handles the sprawling chronology with breathtaking structural agility. We see how seeds planted in the ancient world bloom into crises in the modern, all orchestrated by a man who views time itself as a chessboard. It is a brilliant exploration of power, institutional rot, and the bitter irony of trying to save a species you fundamentally despise. 

Ultimately, this installment proves that Baker’s imaginative well is bottomless. It satisfies longtime devotees of the series while offering a compelling, darkly comedic entry point for newcomers who appreciate history with a twist of dystopian philosophy. For anyone fascinated by the mechanics of time travel, the flaws of capitalism writ large across millennia, and characters who are delightfully, unapologetically ruthless, The Children of the Company is an essential, spellbinding read.
