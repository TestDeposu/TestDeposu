# The (sort of) Dark Mage (Waldo Rabbit #1)

**Author:** Nelson Chereta  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

The (sort of) Dark Mage by Nelson Chereta opens with an unassuming premise that quickly reveals itself as a clever subversion of fantasy conventions. Waldo Rabbit, the titular “sort of” dark mage, is neither a brooding necromancer nor a gleeful villain; he is a reluctant practitioner of shadow magic who spends as much time worrying about his overdue library fees as he does contemplating the moral weight of his spells. This tension between the mundane and the macabre forms the book’s central theme: the gray area where power, responsibility, and everyday life intersect. Chereta invites readers to consider what it truly means to wield darkness—not as a symbol of evil, but as a tool that can be used for protection, mischief, or simply to light a candle in a world that often demands stark moral choices.

From the first page, Chereta’s style is immediately recognizable: a breezy, almost conversational tone laced with dry wit and a keen eye for the absurd. He balances lyrical description with snappy dialogue, allowing the world to feel both richly imagined and instantly accessible. When Waldo trudges through the fog‑shrouded outskirts of his village, the prose shifts to a lush, atmospheric register, evoking the chill of unseen spells. Moments later, a banter‑filled exchange with his sarcastic familiar—a talking raven who insists on being called “Sir Caws‑alot”—snaps the narrative back into a light‑hearted rhythm. This oscillation between gravitas and levity is not a tonal misstep; it is the engine that drives the novel’s exploration of duality. The darkness Waldo commands is never presented as monolithic; it is shaded, nuanced, and often ridiculous, mirroring the protagonist’s own internal conflict.

The author’s world‑building thrives on specificity without becoming bogged down in exposition. Rather than delivering a textbook‑style primer on the mechanics of shadow magic, Chereta lets the rules emerge through Waldo’s mishaps—failed attempts to conceal a stray shadow, accidental summonings of mischievous imps, and the occasional inconvenient side‑effect of turning a teacup into a miniature black hole. These moments serve dual purposes: they entertain while quietly instructing the reader about the limits and costs of Waldo’s power. By grounding fantastical elements in everyday inconveniences—late rent, noisy neighbors, the perennial quest for a decent cup of coffee—Chereta makes the supernatural feel intimately human.

Characterization is another strength. Waldo is a refreshingly imperfect hero. His motivations are rarely grandiose; he seeks acceptance, a quiet life, and perhaps a bit of respect from the townsfolk who eye his lingering aura with suspicion. His supporting cast—ranging from the earnest apprentice who idolizes him despite his own clumsy attempts at magic, to the pragmatic mayor who sees Waldo as both a potential asset and a liability—each reflect different societal responses to ambiguity. The novel avoids painting any character as purely heroic or villainous; instead, it presents a spectrum where choices are motivated by fear, love, pragmatism, or simple boredom. This nuanced approach reinforces the central idea that darkness, like any tool, derives its moral weight from the hands that wield it.

Chereta’s humor is subtle but pervasive, often arising from the juxtaposition of epic fantasy tropes with modern sensibilities. A scene where Waldo attempts to brood dramatically on a cliffside, only to be interrupted by a flock of overly enthusiastic pigeons demanding breadcrumbs, exemplifies this blend. The comedy never undermines the stakes; rather, it highlights the absurdity of taking oneself too seriously when the world itself refuses to adhere to neat binaries. Readers who enjoy works that flirt with parody while maintaining genuine emotional stakes—think of the tonal balance found in series like Terry Pratchett’s Discworld or Patrick Rothfuss’s The Kingkiller Chronicle—will find a kindred spirit here.

The pacing, while generally brisk, occasionally lingers on introspective passages that allow Waldo to confront his own fears of becoming the very thing he tries to avoid. These quieter moments are where the novel’s thematic depth shines brightest. Chereta uses Waldo’s internal monologue to question whether embracing a “sort of” darkness is a compromise or a strength, ultimately suggesting that authenticity lies in owning one’s contradictions rather than denying them. This philosophical undercurrent gives the book a resonance that extends beyond its humorous façade.

In terms of audience, The (sort of) Dark Mage will appeal to readers who enjoy fantasy that does not take itself too seriously but still offers thoughtful commentary on power and identity. Its accessible prose makes it suitable for both seasoned genre fans and newcomers looking for an entry point that feels fresh rather than derivative. The novel’s brevity—though exact page count is unknown—ensures that the narrative remains tight, with each scene contributing to the overarching exploration of moral ambiguity.

Overall, Nelson Chereta delivers a debut that is as charming as it is thought‑provoking. By marrying a whimsical narrative voice with a genuine examination of what it means to dwell in shadows, he creates a story that lingers in the mind long after the final laugh. The (sort of) Dark Mage reminds us that heroism need not be clad in shining armor; sometimes it wears a slightly crooked hat, carries a rabbit‑foot charm, and casts spells that are just a shade too dark for comfort—yet undeniably human.
