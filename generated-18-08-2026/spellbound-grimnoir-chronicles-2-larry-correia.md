# Spellbound (Grimnoir Chronicles, #2)

**Author:** Larry Correia  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

When Larry Correia unleashes a sequel, he doesn’t just raise the stakes; he straps them to a rocket, sets them on fire, and fires them directly into an alternate-history 1930s where magic collides with the gritty reality of hardboiled detective fiction. Spellbound, the second installment of the Grimnoir Chronicles, is an absolute masterclass in urban fantasy pacing, blending the snappy, fedora-tipping dialogue of a classic noir novel with the jaw-dropping, world-ending destruction of modern cosmic horror. 

The central theme of Spellbound pulses through every frantic page: the burden of legacy and the terrifying cost of survival. Correia takes the classic tropes of secret societies and hidden magical subcultures and grounds them in the sweat, blood, and economic desperation of the Great Depression. The Grimnoir Knights are not pristine, cape-wearing paragons of virtue; they are rough-around-the-edges bruisers, weary veterans, and beleaguered working-class folks who just happen to be able to manipulate gravity, throw lightning, or heal bullet wounds. When an assassination attempt on President Franklin Roosevelt is framed on the society, these magical defenders are forced into the shadows. The theme deepens from mere protection to systemic persecution, forcing the protagonists to question whether a world that fears and hunts them is truly worth saving.

Yet, Correia pivots the narrative from a political manhunt into a terrifying philosophical abyss. The re-emergence of a supposedly dead man plunges former private investigator Jake Sullivan and his comrades into a realization far more horrifying than any federal conspiracy. Magic is not a localized evolutionary quirk of the nineteenth century; it is an interstellar refugee fleeing from an apex predator—a cosmic entity that consumes magic and leaves hollowed-out, lifeless realities in its wake. This escalation transforms Spellbound from a period-piece superhero romp into a desperate race against existential annihilation. 

Correia’s signature style is defined by an unrelenting kinetic energy and a distinctly blue-collar charm. His prose moves with the speed of a Tommy gun. There is no stylistic pretension here; Correia is a master of efficient, punchy storytelling that prioritizes momentum, visceral action choreography, and unapologetic entertainment. Every action sequence is meticulously detailed, utilizing the unique physics of the characters' magical abilities in clever, tactical ways. Yet, beneath the booming cacophony of magical duels, smoking tommy guns, and zeppelin battles, there is a surprising warmth. The found-family dynamic among the Grimnoir provides a steady emotional anchor, ensuring that the characters remain deeply human even as they punch through concrete walls and battle cosmic horrors.

Spellbound succeeds because it never takes a breath, yet it never loses its focus. It is a thrilling, cinematic collision of genres that proves Larry Correia is one of the most inventive architects of modern speculative fiction. For readers who crave high-octane action, rich alternate-history world-building, and characters you would gladly follow into the jaws of a cosmic apocalypse, this book is an unputdownable triumph.
