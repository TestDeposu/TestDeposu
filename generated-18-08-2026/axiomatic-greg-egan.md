# Axiomatic

**Author:** Greg Egan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1995

To read Greg Egan’s 1995 collection *Axiomatic* is to subject your own consciousness to a series of philosophical stress tests. In an era where science fiction often relies on sweeping space operas or nostalgic retro-futurism, Egan carved out a distinct, uncompromising niche as the genre's premier hard-science intellectual. This landmark collection of eighteen short stories does not merely entertain; it interrogates the very bedrock of what it means to be human, utilizing speculative fiction as a scalpel to dissect identity, memory, free will, and morality.

Egan’s style is uniquely clinical and relentlessly logical. A former computer programmer and mathematician, he approaches fiction with the precision of a theoretical physicist. Yet, his cold academic rigor is invariably anchored by intense emotional stakes. Where lesser hard-SF writers hide behind technobabble, Egan commits fully to the terrifying, beautiful consequences of his premises. If you can implant a new personality into your brain, what happens to the original "you"? If you could buy an "axiom"—a neural implant that rewrites your core ethical code—are you still the author of your own actions? 

The title story, “Axiomatic,” sets the tone for the entire volume, exploring a world where neurological implants can alter fundamental beliefs, such as the prohibition against murder, framed through a grieving husband desperate for a psychological realignment to cope with tragedy. Across the collection, Egan transforms abstract philosophical paradoxes into visceral, tangible realities. In “Learning to Be Me,” he examines the "jewel"—a neural backup that gradually learns to mimic its human host until the biological brain can be discarded—forcing readers to confront the agonizing transition of selfhood. In “The Hundred Light-Year Diary,” time travel is inverted into an observational burden, where parents read their children's future histories before they are even born, locking them into predetermined trajectories.

What makes *Axiomatic* enduringly striking is Egan's profound skepticism toward the soul, replaced instead by a fierce reverence for the complexity of the physical brain. His characters are rarely heroes in the traditional sense; they are scientists, sufferers, and everyday people trapped in the gears of radical technological paradigm shifts. They grapple with body horror of the highest order, where flesh and silicon merge, and where identity is proven to be as fragile as a line of faulty code. 

Egan’s prose lacks superfluous ornamentation because it does not need it. The power emanates entirely from the sheer velocity of his concepts. He dares the reader to keep up, trusting our intellect while simultaneously dismantling our most cherished dogmas. *Axiomatic* remains a towering achievement in modern speculative fiction—a dazzling, chilling, and essential masterpiece that redefines the boundaries of mind and machine.
