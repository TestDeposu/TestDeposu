# Re: Colonised Planet 5, Shikasta (Canopus in Argos, #1)

**Author:** Doris Lessing  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

Doris Lessing’s *Re: Colonised Planet 5, Shikasta* is not merely a work of science fiction; it is a monumental, disquieting interrogation of humanity, framed as a cosmic bureaucratic archive. As the inaugural volume of her ambitious *Canopus in Argos* sequence, the novel abandons the traditional constraints of terrestrial fiction to examine Earth—renamed Shikasta, meaning "the broken one"—through the cold, analytical lens of a superior galactic empire. 

The narrative unfolds primarily through the reports, journals, and case files of Johor, an emissary from the advanced planetary empire of Canopus. His mission is to chronicle and intervene in the tragic, downward spiral of Shikasta, a planet that has fallen out of alignment with the cosmic harmony due to a rupture with Canopus, leaving it vulnerable to the malicious and exploitative machinations of a rival empire, Sirius. Through Johor’s eyes, human history is recast not as a grand epic of self-determination, but as a pitiful, repeating cycle of violence, spiritual amnesia, and systemic collapse.

Lessing’s central theme is the tragic alienation of humanity from its higher spiritual origins. She posits that human beings were once interconnected with the harmonious flow of the universe—a state she terms the "Sowf"—but lost this connection through a combination of cosmic catastrophe and native vanity. Shikasta is humanity stripped of its romantic illusions. Lessing uses the framing device of an imperial dossier to hold up an unforgiving mirror to the twentieth century, equating our real-world history of colonialism, world wars, and ecological devastation with a planetary sickness. The book suggests that humanity’s greatest flaw is our stubborn amnesia: we have forgotten who we are, where we came from, and what we are truly capable of achieving.

Stylistically, Lessing employs a daring, collage-like approach that defies conventional genre categorization. Eschewing standard linear plotting and deep character development in the traditional novelistic sense, she adopts a dry, documentary tone. The prose is deliberately clinical, mimicking the cadence of administrative reports, ethnographic studies, and personal diaries. This stylistic choice is both jarring and brilliant. By distancing the reader from emotional immersion, Lessing forces an intellectual reckoning. We are asked to read the history of our own species the way a distant, benevolent, yet exhausted bureaucrat might read the chart of a terminally ill patient. 

Yet, beneath the bureaucratic veneer lies Lessing’s signature moral urgency. Her prose vibrates with a prophetic intensity, warning us of our impending self-destruction while stubbornly holding onto the faint ember of hope. The inclusion of documents from the native Shikastans—particularly the poignant diaries of Rachel Sherban, a dying woman living through the collapse of a twentieth-century society—ground the cosmic scale of the narrative in raw, heartbreaking human experience. 

*Shikasta* is undeniably a demanding read. It requires patience and a willingness to sit with discomfort. It lacks the slick world-building and easy escapism of traditional space opera. Instead, it offers something far more valuable: a profound, philosophical myth-making that challenges our understanding of history, evolution, and morality. Lessing dismantles our anthropocentric worldview, forcing us to realize that we are part of a much larger, and far more fragile, galactic ecosystem. 

Ultimately, *Re: Colonised Planet 5, Shikasta* stands as a towering achievement of speculative literature. It is a visionary, sobering, and fiercely intelligent critique of the human condition that remains as urgently relevant today as it was upon its creation. Lessing does not comfort her readers; she awakens them.
