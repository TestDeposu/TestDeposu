# Gun, With Occasional Music

**Author:** Jonathan Lethem  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2003

**A Noir Future Where the Only Thing More Unsettling Than Crime Is Civility**

Jonathan Lethem’s *Gun, With Occasional Music* is a novel that feels like it was written in a fever dream—one where the rules of detective fiction, sci-fi, and absurdist satire collide in a neon-lit Oakland of the near future. The year is unspecified, but the world is unmistakably Lethem’s: a place where animals have evolved into sentient, sometimes sinister beings, where the police enforce moral purity through "karma levels," and where the line between detective and romantic lead blurs with the kind of melancholic charm that only a master of understated melancholy could pull off. This is a book that wears its influences—Dashiell Hammett, Philip K. Dick, and the surrealist undercurrents of *Motherless Brooklyn*—like a well-worn trench coat, its edges frayed with wit and its pockets stuffed with existential dread.

At its core, *Gun, With Occasional Music* is a hard-boiled mystery, but not the kind you’d find in a 1940s pulp magazine. Here, the detective, Conrad Metcalf, is a man adrift in a world that’s both more advanced and more morally bankrupt than his own. The novel opens with a rabbit sitting in his waiting room—a detail that, on its own, could be dismissed as whimsical, but Lethem uses it as a metaphor for the chaos of modern life. The rabbit isn’t just an animal; it’s a symbol of the absurdity that lurks beneath the surface of even the most "civilized" societies. And if that weren’t enough, there’s a kangaroo with a gun, a wife who might be more than she seems, and a murder that implicates everyone from the Inquisitor’s Office (a shadowy arm of the state) to the gangsters running the Fickle Muse, a bar that doubles as a front for something darker.

What makes *Gun, With Occasional Music* so compelling isn’t just its premise—though it’s rich enough to sustain a dozen lesser novels—but Lethem’s ability to balance tone. The novel oscillates between noir cynicism and dark humor, between the gritty realism of a detective unraveling a case and the surrealism of a world where animals have rights, drugs erase memory, and morality is measured in numerical values. The result is a story that feels both grounded and wildly imaginative, a testament to Lethem’s skill in making the bizarre feel plausible and the plausible feel strange.

The prose is lean, precise, and dripping with the kind of dry wit that makes Lethem’s work so distinctive. His descriptions are sharp, his dialogue crackles with life, and his characters—even the secondary ones—feel real, flawed, and deeply human (or in some cases, deeply *other*). Conrad Metcalf is a detective who’s seen too much, loved too little, and is just trying to keep his head above water in a world that’s drowning in its own contradictions. His relationship with Celeste, the wife of the murdered doctor, is one of the novel’s most tender and tragic elements—a love that’s doomed from the start, not because of external forces, but because of the way the world has shaped them both.

The novel’s satire is subtle but devastating. Lethem doesn’t just mock the idea of a future where animals have rights; he explores the hypocrisy of a society that claims to be progressive but enforces its own brand of moral purity through surveillance and punishment. The "karma levels" system is a chilling metaphor for how easily authority can disguise itself as justice, and the drugs Forgettol and Acceptol—one that erases memory, the other that numbs dissent—serve as a dark commentary on how easily people can be controlled. Yet, for all its cynicism, the novel never loses its humanity. Lethem’s world is flawed, but it’s also full of hope, of resistance, of the quiet defiance of characters who refuse to be broken.

The title itself is a clue to the novel’s duality: the "gun" is the obvious symbol of violence and danger, but the "occasional music" suggests that there’s still beauty to be found in this broken world. It’s a reminder that even in the darkest of places, there’s always a glimmer of something brighter. And that, perhaps, is Lethem’s most enduring message: that the future isn’t just something to fear, but something to fight for, even when the fight seems hopeless.

*Gun, With Occasional Music* is a novel that demands to be read, not just for its cleverness or its originality, but for its heart. It’s a story about love and loss, about the struggle to find meaning in a world that’s falling apart, and about the quiet courage of those who refuse to give up. It’s a book that feels like it was written yesterday, even though it was published over two decades ago—a testament to Lethem’s ability to capture the essence of our own times in a world that’s just slightly off-kilter. In the end, it’s a novel that lingers, not just because of its twists or its satire, but because of the way it makes you see the world around you in a new light.
