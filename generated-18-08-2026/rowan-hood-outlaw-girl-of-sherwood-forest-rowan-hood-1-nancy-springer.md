# Rowan Hood: Outlaw Girl of Sherwood Forest (Rowan Hood, #1)

**Author:** Nancy Springer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2003

**Rowan Hood: Outlaw Girl of Sherwood Forest – A Bold Reimagining of Legend and Rebellion**

Nancy Springer’s *Rowan Hood: Outlaw Girl of Sherwood Forest* is more than a sequel—it’s a fiery reinvention of Robin Hood’s mythos, stripped of its medieval trappings and reimagined as a sharp, feminist fable for the modern age. The third installment in the *Rowan Hood* series (though it stands well on its own) doesn’t just expand the world of its outlaw protagonist; it deepens the stakes, sharpens the moral edges, and proves that rebellion isn’t just about arrows and bandits—it’s about survival, sisterhood, and the cost of power.

At its heart, this book is a battle cry for agency. Etty—once Princess Ettarde, now a reluctant heir to a kingdom built on betrayal—finds herself torn between the freedom she’s carved out with Rowan Hood’s ragtag band and the suffocating expectations of her father, King Solon. Her abduction isn’t just a plot device; it’s a brutal metaphor for how women are commodified, traded, and weaponized in a world that sees them as pawns. When Solon uses Etty’s mother as bait—a living cage in the heart of Sherwood—Springer doesn’t shy away from the horror of it. The winter setting isn’t just atmospheric; it’s a mirror for the emotional freeze Etty and her mother endure, their bodies and spirits held hostage. Rowan Hood, ever the reluctant leader, must decide whether to play by the rules of kings or burn them all down.

Springer’s prose is lean, precise, and laced with the kind of dry wit that makes her work so engaging. Her dialogue crackles with tension, whether it’s the snapping retorts between Rowan and her band or the chilling negotiations between Etty and her father. The author excels at balancing action with introspection, ensuring that the physical battles are as gripping as the psychological ones. Etty’s internal conflict—her love for her mother, her resentment of her father, her fear of the unknown—is rendered with raw honesty, making her a protagonist who feels deeply human, not just heroic.

What elevates this book beyond a typical adventure tale is its unflinching exploration of power dynamics. Solon isn’t just a villain; he’s a study in how tyranny justifies itself. His willingness to sacrifice his own wife to secure peace is a chilling indictment of the lengths despots will go to maintain control. Rowan Hood, meanwhile, embodies the quiet defiance of those who refuse to be broken. Her leadership isn’t about charisma or brute strength—it’s about listening, adapting, and trusting the people around her. The band’s dynamics, with their clashing personalities and shared history, add depth to the narrative, making their battles feel personal as well as political.

Springer also plays with genre conventions, blending the mythic with the modern. The forest isn’t just a backdrop; it’s a character in its own right, a place of both refuge and danger. The winter setting amplifies the sense of isolation and desperation, turning the story into a survival tale as much as a rebellion. And while the Robin Hood legend is familiar, Springer’s take is refreshingly original. There’s no merry band of archers here—just a group of misfits, each with their own reasons for being outlaws, united by necessity rather than nostalgia.

The emotional core of the book lies in its mother-daughter relationships. Etty’s bond with her mother is tested in ways that feel both heartbreaking and necessary, forcing her to confront the legacy of her bloodline. Meanwhile, Rowan’s own complicated history with her mother (hinted at in previous books) adds layers to her character, making her more than just a fearless leader—she’s a woman grappling with her own past. These relationships give the story its emotional weight, ensuring that the battles aren’t just about arrows and swords but about love, loyalty, and the choices that define us.

Springer’s writing is never preachy, but the themes she explores—consent, survival, the cost of peace—are undeniable. She doesn’t offer easy answers, and that’s what makes the story so compelling. The reader is left to grapple with the same questions Etty does: How far would you go to protect what’s yours? And when the world tries to cage you, what does it mean to be free?

*Rowan Hood: Outlaw Girl of Sherwood Forest* is a book for anyone who’s ever felt trapped by expectations, who’s ever had to choose between safety and defiance. It’s a tale of outlaws, yes, but also of outcasts—those who refuse to be defined by the rules of the world around them. Springer’s prose is sharp, her characters are unforgettable, and her story is a reminder that rebellion isn’t always about taking over a kingdom. Sometimes, it’s about refusing to be a prisoner in your own life.
