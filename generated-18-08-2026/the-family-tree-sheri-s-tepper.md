# The Family Tree

**Author:** Sheri S. Tepper  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

The Family Tree by Sheri S. Tepper arrives as a vivid, unsettling meditation on what happens when humanity’s drive to manipulate life collides with the planet’s quiet insistence on reclaiming its own narrative. Set in a near‑future where the line between genetic engineering and wilderness blurs, the novel follows Dora Henry, a weary but sharp‑eyed police officer tasked with solving the murders of three prominent geneticists. As Dora pursues the killer, the city around her begins to sprout—weeds unfurl into towering trees, concrete cracks under the pressure of emergent roots, and an inexplicable verdancy spreads with alarming speed. Simultaneously, Dora discovers she can hear, and even converse, with the burgeoning flora, a gift that pulls her deeper into a mystery that stretches from the present murder scene to a distant, possibly inevitable, ecological reckoning.

At its core, The Family Tree is a feminist eco‑speculative work that interrogates power, responsibility, and the ways in which bodies—both human and vegetal—are coded, controlled, and liberated. Tepper uses Dora’s investigation as a conduit to explore how scientific hubris, particularly when wielded by predominantly male institutions, can precipitate catastrophes that disproportionately affect women and marginalized communities. The flora’s uprising is not merely a backdrop; it becomes a voice of resistance, echoing the novel’s insistence that nature possesses its own agency and intelligence, often silenced by patriarchal narratives of conquest. Through Dora’s evolving relationship with the plants—shifting from fear to cautious collaboration—Tepper suggests that true survival hinges on listening to, rather than commanding, the living world.

Stylistically, Tepper blends lyrical prose with a crisp, detective‑novel rhythm, allowing the narrative to swing between moments of poetic wonder and hard‑boiled pragmatism. Her descriptions are lush without becoming overwrought: a single sentence can evoke the scent of damp earth after rain, the metallic tang of blood on a crime scene, and the faint hum of chlorophyll‑laden air all at once. This sensory richness grounds the speculative elements in a tangible reality, making the fantastical feel inevitable rather than gratuitous. The dialogue, meanwhile, carries Tepper’s trademark wit—sharp, sardonic exchanges that reveal character motivations while offering occasional satirical asides about bureaucracy, media sensationalism, and the commodification of science. These flashes of humor prevent the novel from tipping into outright dystopia; instead, they keep the tone buoyant, inviting readers to contemplate grave themes without succumbing to despair.

The mystery plot itself is tightly woven. Each clue Dora uncovers—whether a strange genetic marker, a cryptic note left at a murder site, or an anomalous growth pattern in a city park—feels purposeful, driving both the external investigation and her internal transformation. Tepper excels at layering: the seemingly discrete events of murder, botanical proliferation, and temporal dislocation gradually reveal themselves as facets of a single, sprawling conspiracy. As Dora draws nearer to the truth, the narrative tightens, the pacing quickens, and the stakes expand from a personal quest for justice to a planetary ultimatum. The novel’s climax, which intertwines a revelation about future human evolution with an immediate, vegetative uprising, feels both surprising and inevitable—a hallmark of Tepper’s skill at delivering “delightful surprises” that nonetheless resonate with the thematic groundwork laid earlier.

If there is a note of caution, it lies in occasional expository passages where Tepper pauses to explain the scientific or sociopolitical underpinnings of her world‑building. While these interludes are generally concise and informative, a few readers might find them slightly disruptive to the novel’s otherwise fluid momentum. Nevertheless, they serve an important function: they anchor the speculative extrapolation in credible research, reinforcing the author’s social conscience and preventing the narrative from drifting into pure fantasy.

Overall, The Family Tree stands as a testament to Sheri S. Tepper’s ability to marry genre entertainment with pressing ethical inquiry. Her feminist lens does not merely overlay the story; it reshapes the very mechanics of plot and character, insisting that the salvation of humankind depends on redefining relationships—not only between people but between humanity and the living world that sustains it. Readers who appreciate richly textured world‑building, strong female protagonists, and stories that ask as much about our ecological future as they do about our present will find much to admire in this “beautifully realized” work.
