"The Living Blood" is the second installment in Tananarive Due’s African Immortals trilogy, a novel that fuses the intimate dread of a supernatural thriller with the urgent urgency of a contemporary pandemic narrative. Set against the backdrop of a world gripped by AIDS and other virulent illnesses, the story follows Fana Wolde, the sole immortal born with the Living Blood, a gift that grants her instantaneous healing and the uncanny ability to read minds. Yet this blessing is also a burden, tethering her to a secretive Blood Colony that harvests her essence to produce Glow—a miraculous drug promised to cure nearly any disease. Through Fana’s eyes, Due explores the moral quagmire of exploiting a rare biological treasure for the greater good, asking whether salvation can ever justify the commodification of a single life, especially when that life is entwined with an ancient prophecy that threatens to unleash devastation.

From the opening pages, Due’s prose shimmers with a lyrical intensity that feels both grounded and otherworldly. She opens with a quiet scene in the Pacific Northwest—a protected home where Fana’s family shields her from the world—then thrusts the reader into a frantic escape alongside her mortal best friend, whose imprisonment by the Colony sparks a desperate flight. The narrative pacing mirrors the heartbeat of its protagonist: steady, almost meditative in moments of introspection, then accelerating into breath‑less chase scenes as Fana and her friend join the Underground Railroad of Glow peddlers. This dual rhythm allows Due to linger on the psychological weight of immortality while simultaneously delivering the kinetic thrill of a conspiracy thriller.

What distinguishes Due’s handling of the supernatural is her refusal to treat immortality as a mere fantasy trope. Instead, she roots it in African diasporic history, hinting at a lineage that stretches back over a thousand years, a clan that has survived slavery, colonialism, and now a modern health crisis. The Living Blood becomes a metaphor for resilience: the blood that heals also carries the memory of ancestral endurance. By tying the Colony’s secret to a Vatican‑linked sect that has hunted Glow peddlers for a century, Due weaves a tapestry where religious power, scientific ambition, and ethnic persecution intersect. The sect’s violence is not random; it is motivated by an ancient blood prophecy that positions Fana as either the savior or the harbinger of a cataclysm that could claim countless lives. This prophecy injects the novel with a sense of inevitable fate, forcing Fana to confront not only external enemies but also the internal question of whether she can rewrite destiny.

The theme of sacrifice permeates the narrative. Fana’s willingness to risk her own safety to free her friend underscores a moral core that elevates the story beyond a simple action plot. Her dual nature—immortal yet emotionally mortal—creates a tension that fuels her growth. Due’s style is marked by a seamless blend of genres. By framing the drug’s source as a marginalized, hidden community, Due critiques the exploitation of minority bodies for global benefit—a commentary that resonates powerfully in an era of vaccine nationalism and pharmaceutical profiteering. Yet she never lets the message overwhelm the story; instead, the thematic concerns emerge organically from Fana’s personal journey, making the critique feel earned rather than didactic.
