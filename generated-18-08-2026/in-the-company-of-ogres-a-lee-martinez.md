# In the Company of Ogres

**Author:** A. Lee Martinez  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

If Terry Pratchett and Robert Asprin had ever collaborated over a tankard of suspiciously cloudy ale in a dim tavern at the edge of the world, the result might look something like A. Lee Martinez’s 2007 comic fantasy, In the Company of Ogres. Following up on his delightfully twisted horror-comedy debut, Gil's All Fright Diner, Martinez returns with a sharp, irreverent, and utterly chaotic romp that takes standard high fantasy tropes, strips them of their self-importance, and cheerfully tosses them into a meat grinder. The result is a brisk, highly entertaining satire that pokes fun at military bureaucracy, immortal angst, and the glorious concept of the underdog squad.

At the narrative heart of In the Company of Ogres is the central theme of finding purpose among the castoffs. The military machine depicted in the novel cares little for honor or glory; it cares about paperwork, quotas, and getting rid of liabilities. Enter Never Dead Ned, a soldier whose primary tactical advantage is that death is merely a temporary inconvenience. Ned has mastered the art of perishing and popping back up, a skill that lands him the ultimate poisoned chalice: the command of Ogre Company. This is the legion's undisputed dumping ground, a ragtag unit of misfits, monsters, and walking health hazards. 

Martinez uses this classic "bad news bears" setup to explore identity and redemption. Ogre Company’s members—featuring a polite two-headed ogre, a lovesick Amazon, a blind oracle with a nose for tomorrow, and a suicidal goblin pilot—are defined by society as monsters and failures. Yet, under Ned’s relentlessly pragmatic leadership, they are given a chance to define themselves. The absurdity of their existence highlights a core Martinez motif: that heroism isn't about being noble or unblemished; it is about surviving the daily grind, looking out for your weird friends, and laughing in the face of annihilation. 

Stylistically, Martinez writes with the breathless pace and kinetic energy of a Saturday morning cartoon crossed with a fast-talking noir detective novel. His comedic timing is razor-sharp, relying heavily on deadpan dialogue, escalating slapstick, and an effortless subversion of expectations. Where other fantasy authors might bog down a military assignment with endless pages of world-building and tactical maps, Martinez keeps the focus squarely on character chemistry and verbal sparring. The humor is self-aware without ever collapsing into pure cynicism; there is a genuine warmth at the core of Ogre Company’s dysfunction. Even as Ned uncovers a deeper, darker mystery regarding the true nature of his immortality and the suspicious mortality rates of his predecessors, the narrative never loses its buoyant, irreverent spirit. 

In the Company of Ogres is a fast, funny, and deeply satisfying read that proves fantasy doesn't always need to take itself seriously to tell a compelling story. Martinez manages the difficult feat of making readers care deeply about a company of monsters who are, fundamentally, just trying to survive their boss. For fans of witty, lighthearted speculative fiction, this book is an absolute must-read deployment.
