# Brass Man (Agent Cormac, #3)

**Author:** Neal Asher  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

**Brass Man: A Cosmic Horror Masterpiece Where the Past Never Dies**

Neal Asher’s *Brass Man*—the third installment in his *Agent Cormac* series—is a masterclass in blending hard science fiction, cosmic horror, and high-stakes adventure into a narrative that feels both timeless and urgently relevant. Set against the backdrop of a dying world and a universe teeming with forgotten horrors, this novel doesn’t just deliver a gripping story; it immerses the reader in a world where technology, biology, and malevolent intelligence collide in ways that are as chilling as they are intellectually stimulating.

At its core, *Brass Man* is a tale of resurrection and retribution. The brass killing machine, Mr. Crane, was once a weapon of war, a relic of a forgotten conflict that should have stayed buried. But here he is, back from the dead—or at least, back from whatever hellish state he was in—and now he’s hunting again. Meanwhile, on the primitive world of Cull, a knight named Anderson embarks on his own quest to slay a dragon, unaware that his hunt is being mirrored by Crane’s relentless pursuit. This duality isn’t just narrative trickery; it’s a thematic mirror, reflecting how history repeats itself, how the past refuses to stay dead, and how even the most advanced civilizations can be undone by their own creations.

Asher’s genius lies in his ability to weave these threads together seamlessly. The world of Cull is a pressure cooker of survival, where the inhabitants struggle against both the natural horrors of their planet—ferocious insectile monsters and a planet itself seemingly waking from a long slumber—and the unnatural threats lurking in the shadows. The "Dragon," an entity with ambiguous motives, plays a particularly unsettling role, acting as a kind of cosmic trickster or perhaps a guardian, manipulating events with genetic experiments that blur the line between human and monster. It’s a character study in ambiguity, leaving the reader to question whether this entity is a savior, a tyrant, or something far more sinister.

But the real horror of *Brass Man* isn’t just the monsters or the dragon—it’s the revelation of an ancient alien technology that Mika, the scientist in the story, begins to uncover. Asher doesn’t shy away from the grim possibilities of what humanity (or what’s left of it) might become when confronted with forces beyond its understanding. The idea that a planet could be "awakening" in a way that defies natural law, that a brass machine could be resurrected by forces unknown, and that genetic experimentation could lead to something beyond human control—these are themes that resonate deeply in an era where science and ethics are constantly pushing boundaries.

Asher’s writing style is as sharp as it is immersive. His prose is lean, efficient, and packed with detail, allowing the reader to feel the grit of Cull’s surface, the cold precision of Crane’s movements, and the desperation of those trying to survive. He balances action with introspection, ensuring that even as the plot races forward—with chases, battles, and revelations—there’s always room for the philosophical undercurrents. The dialogue crackles with tension, and the characters, while not always fully fleshed out, are compelling enough to keep the reader invested in their fates.

What makes *Brass Man* particularly compelling is its sense of inevitability. The events feel like they’re unfolding as they should, driven by forces larger than any single character. Crane’s return isn’t just a plot device; it’s a reminder that some enemies never truly die, that some technologies are too dangerous to be contained, and that some worlds are doomed from the moment they’re born. The earthquakes on Cull, the genetic experiments, the dragon’s interference—all of it feels like part of a larger, inescapable cycle.

For fans of hard science fiction, *Brass Man* is a treat. Asher’s worldbuilding is meticulous, his concepts are bold, and his storytelling is relentless. For those who enjoy cosmic horror, the novel delivers a sense of dread that lingers long after the last page. And for anyone who loves a good, high-stakes adventure, the book doesn’t disappoint, packing in enough twists, turns, and revelations to keep the reader on the edge of their seat.

In the end, *Brass Man* is more than just another entry in the *Agent Cormac* series—it’s a standout example of what science fiction can achieve when it dares to explore the darkest corners of the universe. It’s a story about the past refusing to stay buried, about the dangers of playing with forces we don’t understand, and about the fragile line between survival and extinction. And in Asher’s hands, it’s all delivered with a level of craftsmanship that makes it impossible to put down.
