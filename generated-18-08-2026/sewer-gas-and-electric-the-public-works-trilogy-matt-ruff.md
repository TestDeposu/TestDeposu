# Sewer, Gas and Electric: The Public Works Trilogy

**Author:** Matt Ruff  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

Sewer, Gas and Electric: The Public Works Trilogy by Matt Ruff is a wildly inventive and darkly comedic satire that delves into the very heart of a surreal technocratic future. Set in the year 2023, the novel is a dizzying romp that careens through the streets of Manhattan, plunging into the depths of its festering sewers and soaring to the heights of its skyscrapers.

At its core, the novel is a scathing critique of the excesses of capitalism and the dehumanizing effects of technology. Ruff's vision of the future is a bleak one, where androids and humans coexist in a world that is both eerily familiar and yet utterly alien. The billionaire Harry Gant, who is constructing a new Tower of Babel, serves as a symbol of the hubris and ambition that drives humanity's relentless pursuit of progress.

But Ruff's satire is not just limited to the world of high finance and technology. He also takes aim at the cultural icons of our time, resurrecting Ayn Rand and bottling her in a hurricane lamp, where she serves as a wisecracking assistant to the crusading Joan Fine. The inclusion of eco-terrorists, Civil War veterans, and possessed Volkswagen Beetles adds to the sense of chaos and disorder that pervades the novel.

One of the most striking aspects of Ruff's style is his ability to blend the surreal with the mundane. His prose is breezy and humorous, making the novel a joy to read, even as it tackles some of the darker aspects of human nature. The characters are vividly drawn and often hilarious, with Ayn Rand's sardonic observations providing some of the novel's funniest moments.

But beneath the humor and satire, Ruff is tackling some serious themes. He is concerned with the ways in which technology is changing us, and the ways in which we are losing touch with our humanity. The novel is a warning about the dangers of unchecked progress and the importance of holding on to our values in the face of overwhelming change.

In many ways, Sewer, Gas and Electric is a novel that defies easy categorization. It is a work of science fiction, but it is also a satire, a comedy, and a social commentary. It is a novel that will appeal to fans of Thomas Pynchon, Steve Erickson, and David Foster Wallace, and yet it is also a novel that will appeal to anyone who is looking for a wildly inventive and thought-provoking read.

In short, Sewer, Gas and Electric is a novel that is not to be missed. It is a turbocharged neo-Dickensian hot rod that will take you on a wild ride through the streets of Manhattan and the depths of the human condition.
