# A Plague of Angels (Plague of Angels, #1)

**Author:** Sheri S. Tepper  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1994

Sheri S. Tepper has long held a reputation as one of speculative fiction’s most fiercely visionary and uncompromising voices. In her 1994 masterwork, A Plague of Angels, Tepper weaves a deceptively traditional tapestry of fantasy tropes—witches in mountaintop fortresses, enchanted villages, runaway farm boys, and ancient prophecies—only to subvert them with the razor-sharp teeth of ecological urgency and biting social critique. This is not merely a tale of heroes and monsters; it is an intricate, cautionary examination of civilization, gender, technology, and the relentless human drive to dominate the natural world.

The novel’s narrative architecture relies on a brilliant juxtaposition. On the surface, the synopsis hints at a classic romantic quest. We are introduced to classic archetypes: the solitary witch lurking in her canyon stronghold to keep out dragons and ogres, the orphaned beauty maturing in an idyllic enchanted enclave, and the restless youth seeking destiny far from the agrarian monotony of his origins. Yet, under Tepper’s deft guidance, these familiar elements quickly mutate into something far stranger and more resonant. The magical veneer peels away to reveal a post-apocalyptic Earth, scarred by centuries of human hubris, genetic manipulation, and corporate exploitation. 

Tepper’s prose style is characterized by its lyrical precision, rich world-building, and unflinching moral clarity. She does not coddle her readers with easy answers or comfortable resolutions. Instead, she plunges us into a complex, sprawling landscape where sentient constructs, remnants of lost technologies, and mutated ecosystems coexist with human survivors who are all too eager to repeat the mistakes of their ancestors. The central theme of A Plague of Angels revolves around the consequences of ecological disenfranchisement and the commodification of life itself. The titular angels are not benign messengers of divine grace; they represent a terrifying, overwhelming force of systemic reckoning, embodying the unintended consequences of human dominion over nature.

What elevates the novel above standard science fantasy is Tepper's profound focus on agency—particularly female agency—in the face of systemic patriarchal and technological oppression. The young women in her narratives are never passive damsels waiting for rescue; they are active architects of their own survival, forced to navigate treacherous political and environmental landscapes shaped by the arrogance of men and machines. Through sharp dialogue, layered plotting, and a palpable sense of atmospheric dread, Tepper constructs a world that feels both ancient and chillingly futuristic. 

For readers willing to look beyond the comforting facade of traditional fantasy, A Plague of Angels offers a deeply rewarding, intellectually stimulating journey. Tepper challenges her audience to question the very foundations of progress and to consider what truly remains of humanity when the earth is forced to settle its debts. It is a striking, beautiful, and profoundly unsettling piece of speculative fiction that solidifies Tepper’s legacy as a literary ecofeminist pioneer.
