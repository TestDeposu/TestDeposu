# Daughter of Regals and Other Tales

**Author:** Stephen R. Donaldson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

**A Realm of Wonder and Shadow: Stephen R. Donaldson’s *Daughter of Regals and Other Tales***

Stephen R. Donaldson, the visionary behind *The Chronicles of Thomas Covenant* and *The Illearth War*, has long been a master of grand, mythic storytelling—where the weight of destiny collides with the fragility of human (or near-human) resolve. In *Daughter of Regals and Other Tales*, his first short story collection to appear in trade paperback, Donaldson proves that his genius extends beyond sprawling epics. Here, in eight luminous tales, he weaves tales of mystics and unicorns, angels and kings, each imbued with the same dazzling prose and philosophical depth that defined his novels. What emerges is a collection that feels both intimate and vast, a meditation on power, sacrifice, and the unseen forces that shape existence.

At its core, *Daughter of Regals* explores the tension between the divine and the mortal, the sacred and the profane. The titular story, "Daughter of Regals," introduces a princess who discovers her lineage is not what it seems—a revelation that forces her to confront the cost of royal duty and the illusions of power. Similarly, the famous outtake from *The Illearth War*, "Gilden-Fire," resurfaces here, a tale of a man who bargains with a demonic entity, only to learn that true strength lies not in domination but in understanding. These stories often revolve around figures who are outsiders—mystics, exiles, or those cursed with knowledge—characters who must navigate worlds where magic is as real as politics, and where every choice carries eternal consequences.

Donaldson’s style in these tales is as distinctive as ever. His prose is rich with lyrical beauty, yet it never descends into melodrama. Instead, he balances poetic grandeur with sharp, psychological insight. A passage describing a unicorn’s gaze might read like a sonnet, but the next sentence could dissect the fragility of human ambition with surgical precision. His worldbuilding, even in short form, is immersive—whether it’s the mythic kingdoms of "The Daughter of Regals" or the eerie, otherworldly landscapes of "The Angel of the Lord." Yet what truly sets these stories apart is Donaldson’s ability to infuse them with philosophical weight. Many of the tales grapple with themes of free will, divine justice, and the nature of evil, echoing the existential questions that underpin his novels.

One of the collection’s strengths is its diversity of tone. Some stories, like "The Angel of the Lord," feel like fables—moral parables wrapped in mythic imagery. Others, such as "The Unicorn and the Rose," lean into darker, more psychological territory, where the line between hero and villain blurs. There’s a sense that Donaldson is playing with different genres within the same universe, each tale offering a unique lens into his expansive imagination. Yet despite their variety, the stories share a unifying thread: a fascination with the unseen. Whether it’s angels, unicorns, or the remnants of forgotten gods, Donaldson’s tales suggest that the world is far stranger—and far more dangerous—than it appears.

For longtime fans of Donaldson’s work, this collection will feel like a treasure trove, offering fresh perspectives on familiar themes while introducing new characters and settings. For readers new to his writing, it’s a perfect introduction—a chance to experience his signature blend of fantasy and philosophy without the commitment of a full-length novel. The stories are self-contained, yet they leave an impression that lingers, much like the afterimage of a dream.

Ultimately, *Daughter of Regals and Other Tales* is a testament to Donaldson’s enduring brilliance. It proves that even in shorter form, his imagination remains boundless, his prose unforgettable, and his themes as profound as ever. Whether you’re drawn to the mysticism of "The Daughter of Regals" or the moral ambiguity of "The Unicorn and the Rose," this collection is a reminder that some stories—like the best fantasies—are meant to be savored, not rushed. It’s a book that invites reflection, wonder, and the occasional gasp of recognition for those who’ve long admired Donaldson’s work.
