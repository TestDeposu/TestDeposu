# Orphanage (Jason Wander, #1)

**Author:** Robert Buettner  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

War is a young person’s game, but Robert Buettner’s gripping military science fiction debut, Orphanage, strips that brutal truth down to its rawest, most chilling components. Set against the backdrop of a near-future Earth facing an annihilating extraterrestrial threat, the novel introduces us to Jason Wander, a young man whose life is utterly erased by global catastrophe. When an alien probe devastates a major American city, the governments of the world realize they are hopelessly outmatched. Humanity’s only hope lies in a desperate counter-offensive, requiring an army built not of career soldiers, but of the disposable. 

Jason, along with thousands of other institutionalized youths and societal castaways, is drafted into a secretive, expedited military program. With no families to mourn them and no futures to speak of, these orphans become the ultimate expendable asset. Buettner’s central theme is immediately established: the commodification of human life in the machinery of survival. The book explores the profound alienation of a generation that has nothing left to lose, marching willingly into the meat grinder of interstellar warfare simply because the military offers them a bed, a purpose, and a chance to strike back at the cosmos.

Buettner’s writing style is unapologetically direct, gritty, and fiercely authentic. Drawing heavily on his own military background, the author avoids the glossy, overly sanitized tech-fantasy often found in space opera. Instead, he grounds the narrative in the tedious, exhausting, and often dehumanizing reality of basic training, contrasted sharply against the sheer, incomprehensible terror of combat. The prose is lean and muscular, moving with the relentless cadence of a forced march. Buettner does not waste time on flowery world-building; he drops the reader directly into the boots of frightened teenagers learning how to operate powered armor and heavy ordnance, trusting that the immersive realism will speak for itself.

What makes Orphanage resonate long after the final page is its refusal to rely on easy heroism. Jason Wander is not a born commander or a chosen one; he is a survivor navigating a labyrinth of institutional coldness and existential dread. The enemies in the sky are terrifyingly alien, but the antagonist that truly haunts the novel is the calculating bureaucracy of a desperate planet willing to spend its youth to buy tomorrow. 

In a crowded genre of interstellar conflicts, Buettner’s novel stands out as a visceral meditation on duty, grief, and the ultimate cost of humanity's ticket to the stars. It is a striking, fast-paced critique of militarism wrapped in a high-octane sci-fi shell—a remarkable opening salvo for a defining military SF series.
