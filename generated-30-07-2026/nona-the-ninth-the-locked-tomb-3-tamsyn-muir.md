# Nona the Ninth (The Locked Tomb, #3)

**Author:** Tamsyn Muir  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

To step into Tamsyn Muir’s universe is to accept a thrilling, disorienting descent into a literary fever dream where necromancy meets teenage angst, ancient cosmic trauma, and profound emotional devastation. With Nona the Ninth, the third installment in the wildly acclaimed Locked Tomb series, Muir pulls off a narrative pivot so daring, so fundamentally unexpected, it leaves the reader entirely breathless. This is not the snarky, sword-swinging gothic space opera of Gideon the Ninth, nor the labyrinthine, grief-drenched political thriller of Harrow the Ninth. Instead, Nona the Ninth is an intimate, aching, and deeply surreal portrait of humanity at the edge of the apocalypse, filtered through the consciousness of a protagonist who simply wants a birthday party, some hot garlic noodles, and a chance to pet every dog in the city.

The central theme of Nona the Ninth is the agonizing fragility of ordinary life when the universe is collapsing around it. While an apocalyptic blue sphere looms on the horizon, threatening to obliterate the planet, and a desperate war wages between the Blood of Eden and the remnants of the Cohort, Muir directs her lens downward. She focuses on the micro rather than the macro: the cramped apartments, the daily walks, the chaotic love of a found family holding fast against the dark. Nona herself is the embodiment of this theme. Waking up six months prior in a borrowed body with no memories of her true origins, Nona is a creature of pure, unfiltered empathy. She loves dogs, she loves her teachers and students, and above all, she loves her caretakers—Camilla, Pyrrha, and the lingering, spectral presence of Palamedes. In a series historically defined by death, undeath, and anatomical obsession, Nona represents a startlingly vibrant celebration of living. 

Yet, Muir’s trademark signature style remains the driving force beneath this domestic tranquility. Muir writes with a ferocious, chaotic brilliance that seamlessly melds contemporary internet slang, meme culture, and modern colloquialisms with archaic, high-fantasy grandeur and visceral, body-horror poetry. This linguistic whiplash could feel grating in lesser hands, but Muir wields it with absolute mastery. Her prose is rhythmic, sharp-witted, and intoxicatingly atmospheric. Every sentence feels charged with a kinetic energy, oscillating on a dime between laugh-out-loud absurdity and heart-wrenching melancholy. 

As the novel progresses, the cozy, slice-of-life facade begins to fracture, and the true weight of the overarching narrative bleeds through. Nona’s nightly dreams of a woman with a skull-painted face serve as a haunting reminder that Nona is not who—or what—she pretends to be. The tension builds quietly beneath the surface of the mundane, creating a pervasive sense of impending doom. Muir expertly manipulates dramatic irony, allowing us to fall hopelessly in love with Nona while simultaneously dreading the inevitable moment when the borrowed body must be returned, and the illusion must end. 

Nona the Ninth is a masterclass in tonal shifting and character-driven storytelling. It challenges the conventions of epic fantasy and science fiction by daring to slow down the relentless march of plot in order to examine the emotional architecture of its characters. It asks what we are willing to fight for when the world is ending, and answers that salvation is often found not in grand weapons or divine power, but in the simple, radiant act of loving the people right in front of you. Tamsyn Muir has crafted something truly sui generis—a tender, weird, and utterly devastating triumph that cements her status as one of the most innovative voices in modern speculative fiction.
