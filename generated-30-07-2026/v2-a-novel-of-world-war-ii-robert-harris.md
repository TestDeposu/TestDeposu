# V2: A Novel of World War II

**Author:** Robert Harris  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

Robert Harris has carved out a formidable literary reputation as a master of historical suspense, brilliantly transforming the grand geopolitical chess matches of the twentieth century into propulsive, deeply human thrillers. In his 2020 bestselling novel, V2, Harris turns his discerning eye toward the waning months of the Second World War, focusing on the harrowing technological terror of Hitler’s vengeance weapons. Eschewing the traditional battlefields of the European theater, Harris sets his sights on the grim intersection of science, morality, and desperation, delivering a taut narrative that examines the chilling cost of innovation when placed in the service of tyranny.

The novel unfolds over a frantic few days in November 1944, structured around a dual-perspective narrative that brings together two individuals standing on opposite sides of an unforgiving technological divide. On the German side is Rudi Graf, an idealistic engineer whose boyhood dreams of space exploration and lunar travel have been brutally hijacked by the Third Reich. Alongside the infamous Wernher von Braun, Graf finds himself trapped in a bleak seaside town in occupied Holland, tasked with launching V2 rockets—supersonic, unstoppable terrors falling blindly from the stratosphere—upon the civilian populace of London. Graf is no fanatic; he is a pragmatist slowly suffocating under the paranoid gaze of the SS, struggling to survive a project he knows is both scientifically miraculous and morally bankrupt.

Opposite him is Kay Caton-Walsh, a young British intelligence officer and former actress whose life has been upended by the Blitz. Following a devastating V2 strike that shatters her world, Kay volunteers for a perilous mission to newly liberated Belgium. Armed with little more than a slide rule, rudimentary telemetry equations, and fierce determination, she and a small unit of Allied specialists must calculate the trajectory of the incoming rockets, locate the mobile launch sites hidden in the Dutch forests, and direct tactical airstrikes to silence them before London is leveled entirely. 

Harris’s signature stylistic brilliance lies in his ability to make complex historical mechanics utterly riveting. Much like he did with the cryptographic puzzles of Enigma or the political machinations of Munich, Harris treats science and mathematics not as dry background elements, but as characters in their own right. The chapters crackle with the authentic jargon of ballistics, fuel mixtures, and atmospheric pressure, creating a claustrophobic atmosphere of intellectual pursuit married to industrial slaughter. The reader is thrust directly into the muddy bunkers of the Dutch coast and the freezing, calculation-heavy rooms of Allied intelligence, feeling the relentless ticking of the clock.

The central theme of V2 explores the dangerous dissociation of modern warfare. Harris investigates how genius and evil can so easily coexist in the minds of men who look up at the stars while designing instruments of mass annihilation. Wernher von Braun—looming large in the background as a chilling portrait of moral pliability—represents the archetype of the modern technocrat, willing to make a Faustian bargain with the devil just to see his rockets fly. Through Rudi Graf, Harris asks a poignant question: At what point does the pursuit of scientific ambition become complicity in mass murder? 

Meanwhile, Kay’s storyline grounds the macro-level tragedy in human grief and resilience. Her mission is a desperate race against time, heightened by the constant, paralyzing dread of a silent weapon that strikes without warning—you hear the explosion before you ever hear the missile coming. Harris masterfully captures the psychological toll of the V2 campaign, a form of warfare designed not to conquer territory, but to erode the human spirit through random, untraceable devastation.

As the paths of Graf and Kay inevitably hurtle toward a climax, Harris tightens the screws of suspense with a craftsman's precision. There are no easy heroes or melodramatic villains here; instead, the novel presents a nuanced, morally complex landscape where Allied ingenuity meets German desperation, and where trust is a luxury few can afford. 

V2 is a triumph of historical fiction—lean, deeply researched, and relentlessly engaging. Harris strips away the romanticism often associated with the Greatest Generation to reveal the cold, mechanical reality of total war. It is a striking reminder of how easily human intellect can be weaponized, and a gripping testament to the ordinary people who risked everything to stop it.
