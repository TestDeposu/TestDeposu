# Malice (The Faithful and the Fallen, #1)

**Author:** John Gwynne  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

In the realm of epic fantasy, few authors have managed to craft a world as rich, immersive, and unapologetically brutal as John Gwynne's Banished Lands. "Malice," the first installment in The Faithful and the Fallen series, is a masterclass in storytelling, weaving a complex tapestry of blind ambition, ancient monsters, and the inevitability of war.

Gwynne's writing style is akin to a slow-burning fire, gradually building in intensity until the reader is consumed by the sheer scope of the narrative. His prose is economical, yet evocative, conjuring vivid images of a world on the brink of collapse. The Banished Lands, with its dark history and foreboding landscapes, is a character in its own right, exerting a profound influence on the lives of those who inhabit it.

At the heart of "Malice" lies a cast of complex, multidimensional characters, each with their own distinct voice and motivations. Corban, the young warrior, is driven by a desire to prove himself, while Evnis is consumed by an insatiable lust for power. Veradis, the skilled swordsman, struggles to emerge from the shadow of his brother, and Nathair, the High Prince, harbors ambitions that threaten to upend the fragile balance of power in the Banished Lands.

Gwynne's greatest strength, however, lies in his ability to craft a narrative that is both epic in scope and intimate in its characterizations. The world of the Banished Lands is one of ancient monsters, mythical creatures, and forgotten lore, yet it is the human characters, with their frailties and contradictions, who truly drive the story forward.

One of the primary themes of "Malice" is the corrupting influence of power and the devastating consequences of blind ambition. Gwynne explores this theme through the character of Evnis, whose relentless pursuit of power is both fascinating and terrifying to behold. As the story unfolds, it becomes clear that Evnis's actions will have far-reaching consequences, setting in motion a chain of events that will change the course of history.

The world-building in "Malice" is nothing short of phenomenal, with Gwynne drawing inspiration from Celtic and Norse mythologies to create a richly detailed landscape. The Banished Lands are a place of dark forests, snow-capped mountains, and treacherous bogs, where the forces of nature are as much a threat as the monsters that lurk in the shadows.

If there is a criticism to be made, it is that the pacing of the novel can be somewhat uneven, with certain plot threads developing more slowly than others. However, this is a minor quibble in an otherwise outstanding work of fantasy fiction.

In conclusion, "Malice" is a stunning achievement in the world of epic fantasy, a novel that will appeal to fans of George R.R. Martin, Patrick Rothfuss, and Mark Lawrence. With its richly detailed world-building, complex characters, and gripping narrative, it is a must-read for anyone who enjoys a good fantasy novel.
