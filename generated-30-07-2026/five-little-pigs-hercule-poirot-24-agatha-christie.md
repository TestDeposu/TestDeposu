# Five Little Pigs (Hercule Poirot, #24)

**Author:** Agatha Christie  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2023

Agatha Christie is a master of structural ingenuity, but in Five Little Pigs, originally published in 1942 and presented here in a striking modern edition, she achieves something far more profound than a standard locked-room puzzle. While many Golden Age mysteries rely on frantic pacing and physical clues, this chilling masterpiece operates as a psychological post-mortem. It is a brilliant exploration of memory, subjective truth, and the lingering toxicity of a crime committed sixteen years in the past. 

The premise is deceptively elegant and famously framed by a nursery rhyme: beautiful, volatile painter Amyas Crale is poisoned. His wife, Caroline, is convicted and dies in prison, leaving behind a legacy of tragedy. Yet, sixteen years later, their daughter begs Hercule Poirot to uncover the truth. Caroline left behind a written declaration of her innocence, and Poirot, driven by his obsession with human psychology, agrees to re-examine the case. The catch? There are only five other suspects present that fateful day at the family estate—the five little pigs of the rhyme, each neatly compartmentalized by motive, opportunity, and personality.

What sets Five Little Pigs apart in Christie’s vast bibliography is her distinct narrative style. Instead of relying on Poirot’s bustling interrogation scenes in real-time, Christie constructs the novel as a series of Rashomon-style monologues. Poirot interviews the five survivors—the coldly pragmatic stockbroker, the gentle amateur herbalist, the glamorous and vindictive femme fatale, the loyal governess, and the scarred, defensive sister—asking each of them to write down their exact memories of the day of the murder. 

Through this brilliant device, Christie explores the central theme of subjective perception. As Poirot reads the five written accounts, a fascinating psychological portrait emerges. None of the witnesses necessarily lie outright, yet each remembers the events differently, coloring the past through their own biases, jealousies, and desires. Christie dissects how human memory is a deeply flawed instrument, malleable and easily corrupted by emotion. The author’s sharp, observant prose strips away the civilized veneer of the English upper-middle class to reveal the petty rivalries, possessive love, and quiet malice lurking beneath.

Even without the frantic action typical of modern thrillers, the tension in Five Little Pigs is palpable. Poirot remains largely an invisible catalyst, allowing the suspects to condemn or acquit themselves through their own words. When the diminutive Belgian detective finally gathers them together to reveal the killer, the solution is as emotionally devastating as it is logically airtight. 

This reissue serves as a timely reminder of why the Queen of Crime remains unrivaled. Five Little Pigs is not merely a whodunit; it is a timeless study of guilt and human nature that lingers in the mind long after the final page is turned. Essential reading for both seasoned aficionados and a new generation of mystery lovers.
