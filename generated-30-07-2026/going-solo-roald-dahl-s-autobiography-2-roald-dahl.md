# Going Solo (Roald Dahl's Autobiography, #2)

**Author:** Roald Dahl  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1993

Before he was the master of delightfully macabre children's literature, dreaming up giant peaches and chocolate factories, Roald Dahl lived a life more astonishing than any fiction he would later pen. Going Solo, the second volume of his autobiography following the acclaimed Boy, transports readers away from the idyllic, if strict, English boarding schools of his youth and drops them headfirst into the vast, perilous expanse of the Second World War. Published in 1993, this memoir is not merely a historical recounting of wartime exploits; it is a masterclass in narrative tension, capturing the essence of a young man stepping blindly into adulthood and finding himself forged in the fires of global conflict.

The overarching theme of Going Solo is, fittingly, the crucible of independence. The narrative begins in 1938 when a twenty-two-year-old Dahl boards a steamship bound for East Africa to work for the Shell oil company. The lush, unpredictable landscapes of Dar es Salaam and the surrounding territories provide an exotic backdrop for his early professional life, filled with encounters with majestic local wildlife, eccentric colonial characters, and the creeping dread of approaching war. However, the true metamorphosis occurs when Dahl leaves the safety of the oil company to enlist in the Royal Air Force. 

What follows is a breathtaking descent into the theater of war. Dahl’s recounting of his flight training in Iraq and his subsequent deployment with Hurricane fighter squadrons in the Mediterranean and North Africa forms the beating heart of the book. Yet, Going Solo resists the temptation to become a dry, patriotic war chronicle. Instead, Dahl focuses intensely on the psychological weight of isolation—hence the title. Whether he is navigating a biplane over treacherous desert dunes with a rudimentary map, or engaging in heart-stopping dogfights against overwhelming odds in the skies above Greece, Dahl was perpetually alone with his thoughts, his machine, and his mortality. 

The author’s literary style is the true star of the show. Dahl possesses an uncanny ability to translate the high-stakes terror of wartime aviation into prose that feels effortlessly conversational, laced with his trademark deadpan humor and observational sharpness. He does not posture as an infallible war hero; rather, he presents himself as a remarkably ordinary, sometimes bewildered young man thrust into extraordinary circumstances. His descriptions are cinematic and visceral. When he details his catastrophic plane crash in the Western Desert—an accident that left him temporarily blinded and with severe skull fractures—he manages to balance agonizing physical trauma with moments of dark, almost absurd comedy. It is this exact stylistic duality, the seamless blending of the horrific and the whimsical, that defines Dahl’s entire literary legacy. 

Furthermore, Dahl’s interactions with the broader world reveal a profound curiosity about human nature. From recounting the bizarre domestic habits of his fellow colonial expatriates to paying homage to the brave, doomed pilots he flew alongside—many of whom did not survive the campaign—Dahl honors the memory of his fallen comrades without slipping into mawkish sentimentality. He observes the world with the wide-eyed wonder of a storyteller storing away details for later use, making the reader acutely aware that the horrors and triumphs of these years deeply influenced the dark edges of his later children's fiction.

Going Solo is a triumph of autobiographical writing. It proves that Roald Dahl’s own life was every bit as gripping, eccentric, and miraculous as the tales he spun for millions of readers. For anyone who has ever marveled at his imagination, this book offers a humbling and exhilarating look at the man behind the magic, demonstrating how a young pilot learned to navigate not just the treacherous skies of World War II, but the turbulent winds of destiny itself.
