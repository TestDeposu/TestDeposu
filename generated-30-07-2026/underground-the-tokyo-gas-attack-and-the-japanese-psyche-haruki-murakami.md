# Underground: The Tokyo Gas Attack and the Japanese Psyche

**Author:** Haruki Murakami  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2001

On March 20, 1995, the mundane rhythm of Tokyo morning commuters was violently shattered. Members of the doomsday cult Aum Shinrikyo released liquid sarin gas into the enclosed confines of the city subway system, killing twelve people and injuring thousands. It was an atrocity that pierced the heart of Japan’s national myth of safety, order, and invulnerability. Yet, in Underground: The Tokyo Gas Attack and the Japanese Psyche, renowned novelist Haruki Murakami does not focus on the perpetrators, nor does he offer a sensationalized true-crime thriller. Instead, turning away from the fiction that made him famous, Murakami trains his compassionate lens on the victims, the survivors, and the ordinary workers who lived through an ordinary morning turned extraordinary by terror.

The central theme of Underground is the democratization of trauma and the quiet resilience of everyday people. Murakami’s previous novels often explored surreal isolation and the labyrinthine depths of the human subconscious. Here, the labyrinth is terrifyingly real: the concrete tunnels of the Tokyo metro. By interviewing dozens of survivors—salarymen, students, transit workers, and doctors—Murakami rescues these individuals from being mere statistics in a news cycle. He explores how a society built on corporate loyalty, rigid conformity, and a collective assumption of absolute peace suddenly had to confront the irrational, random nature of modern evil. Through their testimonies, the book asks a haunting question: What happens when the invisible social contract that protects you in public spaces is brutally ripped apart?

What makes this work extraordinary is Murakami’s distinctive stylistic approach. Eschewing the detached objectivity of traditional journalism, Murakami acts as a literary conduit. His prose is characteristically clear, hypnotic, and deeply empathetic. He listens with infinite patience, allowing his subjects to meander through their memories, capturing not just the horror of the gas attacks, but the poignant banality of the moments immediately preceding them—the smell of cheap coffee, the worry over a late train, the annoyance of a crowded carriage. This technique grounds the catastrophe in the texture of daily life, making the sudden intrusion of lethal poison all the more jarring. 

Furthermore, Murakami exhibits a rare moral courage by turning his investigative gaze, in the book's second half, toward the cult members themselves. Rather than dismissing them as cartoonish monsters, he attempts to understand what drove alienated, highly educated young Japanese citizens into the totalitarian embrace of Aum Shinrikyo. In doing so, he critiques the pressures of contemporary Japanese society—the relentless corporate grind, the spiritual emptiness of hyper-capitalism, and the desperate yearning for meaning in a disconnected world. He suggests that the subway attack was not an isolated aberration, but a symptom of a deeper, systemic malaise.

Underground is a masterclass in empathetic non-fiction. It transcends its specific historical moment in 1995 to become a universal meditation on vulnerability, resilience, and the fragility of civilization. Murakami strips away the noise of politics and sensationalism, leaving the reader face-to-face with the raw, beating heart of humanity. It is a haunting, essential read that lingers long after the final page is turned, reminding us that the dark tunnels of our world are never truly as safe as we pretend they are.
