# An Ideal Husband

**Author:** Oscar Wilde  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Oscar Wilde’s *An Ideal Husband* arrives in this Apple Books Classic edition as a glittering reminder that wit can be both a scalpel and a mirror. First staged in 1895, the play crackles with the same electric paradoxes that made Wilde’s epigrams famous, and its core concern—how society polishes its morality while tolerating deceit beneath the surface—feels startlingly contemporary.

The plot hinges on a classic blackmail scheme: Mrs. Cheveley threatens to expose Sir Robert Chiltern’s illicit rise to wealth unless he supports a dubious canal project. Lady Chiltern, embodying the Victorian ideal of wifely devotion, refuses to entertain any suspicion of her husband’s honor, setting up a tension between public image and private truth. As the deception spirals, Wilde forces his characters—and the audience—to confront the hollowness of a moral code that values appearances over integrity. The Chilterns’ journey from blind faith to painful self‑examination mirrors the broader Victorian struggle to reconcile progress with propriety withering of respectability could be bought, sold, or blackmailed away.

Wilde’s style is the play’s true engine. His dialogue is a rapid‑fire succession of paradoxes, inversions, and razor‑sharp one‑liners that do more than entertain; they reveal character motivations with surgical precision. Sir Robert’s defense—“I can resist everything except temptation”—is both a confession and a critique of the era’s self‑delusion. Lady Chiltern’s steadfastness, initially presented as virtue, gradually exposes the danger of an unyielding moral absolutism that leaves no room for forgiveness or growth. Even the comic relief, embodied by the ever‑observing Lord Goring, serves as a vehicle for Wilde’s skepticism: his charming cynicism underscores the idea that true wisdom lies in recognizing one’s own flaws rather than condemning others’.

What makes *An Ideal Husband* endure is not merely its period setting but its timeless interrogation of hypocrisy. In an age of political scandals, influencer façades, and cancel culture, Wilde’s accusation that societies often “pretend to be moral while secretly rewarding the immoral” rings louder than ever. The play does not offer easy answers; instead, it leaves us unsettled, questioning whether any husband—or any public figure—can ever truly embody the ideal when the ideal itself is a construct built on deception.

Critics might note that the resolution feels somewhat tidy, with Lord Goring’s machinations restoring order and the Chilterns finding a fragile reconciliation. This neatness can be read as a concession to the theatrical conventions of Wilde’s time, yet it also serves a purpose: it highlights that societal forgiveness often depends on performance rather than genuine repentance. The audience is invited to enjoy the restoration while remaining aware of its artifice—a meta‑commentary that is quintessentially Wildean.

In sum, *An Ideal Husband* is a masterclass in satirical drama, where Wilde’s dazzling style elevates a seemingly straightforward scandal into a profound meditation on morality, marriage, and the masks we wear. It remains a must‑read not only for lovers of classic theatre but for anyone intrigued by the enduring dance between truth and the stories we tell ourselves to look good.
