# The Wrong Play (The Wrong Player, #2)

**Author:** C.R. Jane  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

The Wrong Play (The Wrong Player, #2) by C.R. Jane offers a brisk, scatter‑shot tour through the off‑beat corners of music history, presenting 250 bite‑size anecdotes that range from punk‑rock showdowns to jazz improvisations, from family‑born secret languages to the peculiar marketing quirks of 1990s teen magazines. Rather than a linear narrative or a deep‑dive musicology tome, Jane treats the book as a mixtape: each story is a track, sharply edited, designed to catch the listener’s ear and then move on. The result is a readable, often entertaining sampler that feels less like a scholarly reference and more like a late‑night conversation with a friend who loves to drop obscure trivia.

At its core, the collection celebrates the idea that music lives not only in polished recordings but in the messy, spontaneous moments that surround it. The opening vignette about Black Flag’s near‑empty Oklahoma City set encapsulates this theme perfectly. Henry Rollins’ frustration, mollified by bassist Chuck Dukowski’s reminder that “even if only a few people are there, they came to see us,” distills a punk ethos of authenticity and relentless effort. Jane uses this story to argue that the true measure of a performance lies in the artist’s commitment, not the size of the crowd—a lesson that recurs throughout the book in various guises.

Similarly, the anecdote involving David Amram and Miles Davis highlights jazz’s forgiving philosophy: “there are no wrong notes.” Amram’s recollection of Mingus turning a cash‑register chime into a musical cue illustrates how improvisation can transform interruptions into inspiration. Jane leans on this tale to suggest that creativity thrives when musicians remain open to the unexpected, a notion that resonates with the book’s broader encouragement to listen closely to the world’s ambient soundtrack.

Leonard Bernstein’s playful family language—Rybernian—offers a lighter, more personal counterpoint. By turning everyday speech into a private code, Bernstein and his friends turned communication itself into a form of play, echoing the improvisational spirit found in the earlier jazz story. Nina Bernstein’s description of the language as a way to “mispronounce things” and to bring her father “right down to earth” underscores Jane’s point that music, and the culture surrounding it, often thrives in the realm of the informal and the intimate.

The final vignette about Sassy magazine’s flexidisc of R.E.M.’s cover of Syd Barrett’s “Dark Globe” serves as a cultural footnote that ties the book’s themes together. The image of fans discarding the magazine to keep the flexidisc reveals how music can eclipse its medium, becoming a coveted artifact in its own right. Jane uses this story to comment on the ways fans curate and valorize musical moments, sometimes at the expense of the surrounding context—a subtle reminder that the value we assign to music is often as much about the narrative we build around it as the sound itself.

Stylistically, C.R. Jane adopts a conversational, almost anecdotal tone that makes the book accessible to readers who might be intimidated by more academic music histories. Sentences are short, punchy, and frequently peppered with colloquialisms (“straightened me out on a few things,” “play your *ss off”), which mirrors the raw energy of the punk and jazz scenes she describes. This approach works well for the book’s purpose: to deliver quick, memorable nuggets that can be enjoyed in short bursts, much like scrolling through a playlist of favorite tracks.

However, the very strengths that make the book enjoyable also point to its limitations. The rapid‑fire format leaves little room for deeper analysis or contextualization. Readers seeking insight into why certain moments mattered historically, or how they influenced later movements, may find the treatment superficial. Additionally, the sheer volume of anecdotes—250 in total—can lead to occasional repetitions or a sense of fatigue; after a while, the novelty of each story diminishes, and the book begins to feel more like a compendium of fun facts than a cohesive argument. Minor editorial slips—such as the misspelling of “Ashram” for Amram—also distract from an otherwise lively presentation.

Despite these drawbacks, The Wrong Play succeeds as a source of inspiration and a reminder that music’s magic often resides in its imperfections, its spontaneity, and the communities that form around it. Jane’s enthusiasm is infectious, and her ability to unearth obscure yet resonant stories makes the volume a worthwhile companion for musicians, fans, and anyone curious about the human side of sound. It may not replace a rigorous musicology text, but as a lively, off‑center mixtape of musical lore, it hits the right notes more often than not.
