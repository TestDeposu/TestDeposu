# The Fortress

**Author:** Meša Selimović  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2024

Ahmed Shabo does not merely tell a story; he excavates the buried architecture of the human conscience. Set against the atmospheric, historically weighted backdrop of post-war seventeenth-century Sarajevo, Meša Selimović’s timeless masterpiece *The Fortress* has long demanded a critical framework as intricate and layered as its prose. Now, in a brilliant intersection of literary theory and performance studies, Sanja Garić-Komnenić’s scholarly offering—spotlighted within the broader landscape of *Bosnian Literature on Stage and Screen*—breathes vibrant new life into Selimović’s universe, offering a striking meditation on how great literature transcends the page to haunt the stage and screen.

At the heart of *The Fortress* lies a profound and universal theme: the search for sanctuary in a world defined by hostility, institutional indifference, and ideological rigidity. Ahmed Shabo, a returning soldier haunted by the trauma of war, finds himself alienated in a society that demands conformity while offering no genuine peace. Selimović’s primary genius has always been his ability to transform historical specificity into an intimate psychological portrait. The fortress of the title is not merely a physical structure of stone and mortar, but a complex metaphor for the defensive walls we build around our hearts, the ideological barriers erected by states, and the solitary confinement of the unyielding human soul. It is a deeply existential inquiry into isolation, love as a singular redemptive force, and the crushing weight of institutional power over the individual.

What makes Garić-Komnenić’s critical engagement with Selimović’s novel so compelling is her rigorous application of semiotics and adaptation theory. By examining the cinematic translation of *The Fortress* alongside the theatrical adaptation of Selimović’s *The Island*, Garić-Komnenić bridges the often-fractured worlds of literary theory, theatre, and film. Her comparative approach is masterfully grounded in the interplay of sign systems and unique artistic codes. She does not merely ask whether an adaptation is faithful to the source material; instead, she investigates how the cinematic lens and the theatrical stage reconstruct Selimović’s heavy, philosophical prose into entirely new sensory and semiotic vocabularies. 

Selimović’s style is famously dense, introspective, lyrical, and burdened with a heavy, beautiful melancholy. Translating such internal monologue and philosophical depth into visual and performance media is a formidable undertaking. Yet, as Garić-Komnenić brilliantly illuminates, the screen adaptation of *The Fortress* manages to capture the claustrophobia of Shabo’s existence through masterful manipulation of space, light, and pacing. The cinematic medium transforms Selimović’s psychological landscapes into tangible environments, where every shadow and stone speaks the language of alienation. 

Garić-Komnenić’s scholarly rigor shines through her deep understanding of film and theater language units. Drawing upon her extensive background in semiotics, she dissects how meaning is generated differently when a reader's solitary engagement with a page is replaced by the communal, sensory experience of a theater or a dark cinema. She successfully reconciles theoretical approaches with practical execution, demonstrating that adaptation is not a dilution of original art, but a vibrant, transformative dialogue between different artistic mediums.

For lovers of Southeastern European literature, adaptation studies, and philosophical fiction, this critical exploration is an indispensable read. It honors the monumental weight of Meša Selimović’s legacy while providing a sophisticated, contemporary lens through which to view the afterlife of a literary classic. Garić-Komnenić reminds us that true masterworks never truly end with the final chapter; they evolve, adapt, and continue to echo across stages, screens, and human hearts.
