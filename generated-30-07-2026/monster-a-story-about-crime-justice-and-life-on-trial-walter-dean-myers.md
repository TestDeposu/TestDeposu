# Monster: A Story About Crime, Justice, and Life on Trial

**Author:** Walter Dean Myers  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Is sixteen-year-old Steve Harmon an aspiring filmmaker trying to make sense of a terrifying world through the lens of a camera, or is he a remorseless monster facing life in prison for a brutal felony murder? This chilling and profound question anchors Walter Dean Myers’s landmark young adult novel, Monster, a book that has lost none of its urgent, heartbreaking relevance since its publication. As the inaugural recipient of the prestigious Michael L. Printz Award, Myers’s masterwork remains an essential cornerstone of contemporary literature, offering a searing examination of guilt, perception, and survival within the American justice system.

The brilliance of Monster lies not merely in its high-stakes courtroom drama, but in its revolutionary structure. Rather than adopting a traditional narrative format, Myers constructs the novel primarily as a screenplay written by Steve himself, interspersed with the teenager’s raw, introspective journal entries. Confined to a juvenile detention center while on trial for his alleged role in a botched Harlem drugstore robbery that left a man dead, Steve copes with his trauma and fear by filming his reality. Complete with camera angles, fade-ins, and dialogue, this cinematic framing device is a stroke of genius. It allows the reader to step directly into the fractured psyche of a frightened boy desperately trying to distance himself from the nightmare consuming his life. 

Yet, this stylistic choice does more than showcase narrative ingenuity; it serves as a brilliant thematic vehicle. By viewing the trial through a screenplay, Myers forces us to confront the slippery nature of truth and identity. A camera can pan, zoom, and cut, but it can also manipulate, framing a human being as a hero or a villain depending entirely on who is holding the equipment and who is cutting the final edit. In the courtroom, the prosecutor aggressively wields words like weapons, eager to reduce a complex, sensitive teenager into a flat, terrifying caricature—a monster. 

Myers’s prose and scriptwriting are unflinching in their social commentary. He exposes the subtle and overt prejudices woven into the fabric of the justice system, where young Black men are frequently judged before a single witness takes the stand. Through the contrasting testimonies of street-smart accomplices, weary defense attorneys, and biased prosecutors, the author paints a devastatingly realistic picture of urban youth navigating a world stacked against them. Steve is not a hardened criminal, nor is he entirely blameless; he is a confused kid standing at a moral crossroads, caught in the devastating gravity of one poor decision. 

What makes Monster truly unforgettable is its refusal to offer neat, comforting resolutions. Myers respects his readers too much to wrap the story in a bow. Instead, he leaves us grappling with ambiguity, forcing us to look in the mirror and question our own rush to judgment. Long after the final page is turned, Steve Harmon’s silent, searching gaze lingers, challenging us to see the humanity hidden beneath society's harsh labels. It is a gripping, inventive, and profoundly necessary read that continues to define excellence in young adult fiction.
