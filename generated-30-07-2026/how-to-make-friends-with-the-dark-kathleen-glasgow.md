# How to Make Friends with the Dark

**Author:** Kathleen Glasgow  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

Anatomy of the Abyss: Kathleen Glasgow’s *How to Make Friends with the Dark*

Death rarely arrives with theatrical warning; more often, it enters with the mundane cruelty of a ringing telephone. For Grace "Tiger" Tolliver, the protagonist of Kathleen Glasgow’s devastating 2019 young adult novel *How to Make Friends with the Dark*, the world shatters in a single, unceremonious second. Her mother—her anchor, her confidante, her entire universe—dies suddenly. In an instant, Tiger is cast adrift into a terrifying, unfamiliar void. What follows is not merely a story about grief, but an unflinching, visceral exploration of what it means to be swallowed whole by the dark and forced to claw your way back to the surface with bare, bleeding hands.

Glasgow, celebrated for her raw and unromanticized portrayal of mental health and trauma in *Girl in Pieces*, returns here with an even sharper scalpel. Her prose is a masterclass in contemporary YA realism—lyrical yet jagged, breathless yet deliberate. She does not write about grief from a safe, clinical distance; she drags the reader right down into the mud with Tiger. Every sentence vibrates with the heavy, suffocating weight of loss. Glasgow understands that mourning is not a linear path of stages, but a chaotic, cyclical tempest of anger, numbness, panic, and paralyzing loneliness. 

At its core, the novel’s central theme is survival in the absence of light. Tiger’s journey lays bare the agonizing vulnerability of the foster care system, stripping away any sanitized illusions. With no known father, grandparents, or extended family to catch her, Tiger’s life is unceremoniously packed into a trash bag and a suitcase. She is bounced from one temporary home to another, a transient ghost in a world that keeps spinning while hers has ground to a violent halt. Glasgow brilliantly captures the psychological disorientation of displacement—the feeling of being a permanent outsider in spaces meant to offer refuge. 

Yet, within this overwhelming darkness, Glasgow weaves a fragile, stubborn thread of hope. It arrives in the most unexpected of places: the discovery of a sister. The complex, evolving dynamic between Tiger and her newly surfaced sibling anchors the narrative, proving that family is rarely the neat, blood-bound picture we are sold. Instead, it is often forged in the fires of shared trauma and awkward, aching vulnerability. Through this relationship, Glasgow explores the messy reality of connection, teaching us that healing does not mean forgetting the dark, but learning how to navigate it without a compass.

Critics and bestselling authors alike have hailed the book as breathtaking and heartbreaking, and for good reason. Glasgow refuses to offer cheap resolutions or tied-with-a-bow endings. The emotional resonance of *How to Make Friends with the Dark* lingers long after the final page, demanding empathy from its readers. It is a rare, powerful novel that looks unflinchingly at the ugliest parts of human suffering and manages to find a pulse of resilience beneath the wreckage. 

For anyone who has ever felt the world cave in around them, Glasgow’s latest masterpiece is both a mirror and a lifeline. It is a testament to the fact that while we cannot always outrun the night, we can eventually learn to walk alongside it.
