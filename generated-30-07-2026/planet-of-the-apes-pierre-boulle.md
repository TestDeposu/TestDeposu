The novel follows three astronauts—Ulysse Mérou, Professor Antelle, and Arthur Levain—who crash‑land on a world that mirrors Earth in every superficial detail: verdant forests, breathable air, a temperate climate. Yet the paradise is a cruel illusion. Here, intelligent apes have built a rigid, technologically advanced society while humans have regressed to mute, bestial scavengers, hunted and exhibited like animals. Boulle flips the familiar hierarchy, forcing readers to confront the arrogance of assuming humanity’s inevitable supremacy.

The central theme is the relativity of civilization. Boulle uses the ape‑human inversion to ask whether culture is an intrinsic trait of a species or a contingent product of history and environment. As Ulysse struggles to communicate, to teach, and ultimately to survive, the novel probes the thin line between savagery and sophistication. The climax, a shocking revelation about the true origins of the ape civilization, delivers a twist that feels both inevitable and unsettling, echoing the satirical bite of Swift while retaining the visceral tension of a classic adventure.

Boulle’s style is economical yet evocative. He moves swiftly from lush description to terse dialogue, allowing the alien setting to emerge through sensory details rather than exhaustive exposition. His prose carries a dry, almost journalistic tone that heightens the irony; the narrator’s scientific detachment makes the horror of the ape‑run world all the more palpable. The novel’s pacing mirrors a thriller—short, punchy chapters propel the reader forward, while intermittent philosophical interludes give depth without slowing the momentum. Though the translation occasionally feels dated, the core narrative retains its urgency, proving that Boulle’s blend of adventure, satire, and speculative foresight remains remarkably potent.
