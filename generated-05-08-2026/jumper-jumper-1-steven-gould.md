# Jumper (Jumper, #1)

**Author:** Steven Gould  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

Jumper introduces Davy Rice, a teenager whose life is shattered by an abusive, alcoholic father. In a moment of desperate fear, Davy discovers he can instantly transport himself to any place he has seen or previously visited—a power that feels both miraculous and terrifyingly ordinary. Steven Gould’s debut novel turns this fantastical ability into a lens for examining the raw, often painful realities of adolescence: the urge to flee toxic environments, the search for belonging, and the struggle to define oneself when the world offers no safety net.

What sets Jumper apart from many teleportation tales is Gould’s grounding of the supernatural in meticulous, almost scientific detail. He treats Davy’s jumps not as magic but as a skill honed through practice, limitation, and consequence. Each teleport requires a clear mental image, a physical effort, and leaves Davy vulnerable to the disorientation of re‑appearing in unfamiliar terrain. This “hard” approach to a classic SF concept makes the wonder feel earned, while the emotional core remains firmly human. Davy’s internal monologue is candid and wistful, echoing the earnest voice of early Heinlein protagonists, yet Gould layers it with a contemporary psychological depth that acknowledges trauma, guilt, and the lingering impact of familial violence.

The narrative moves briskly, alternating between high‑stakes escapades—Davy’s covert jumps to libraries, foreign cities, and even the moonlit rooftops of his hometown—and quieter, introspective passages where he wrestles with the moral implications of his gift. Gould’s prose is warm and accessible, yet never simplistic; he balances action with reflection, allowing readers to feel the adrenaline of a sudden jump and the weight of Davy’s loneliness in equal measure. The novel’s pacing mirrors its titular ability: quick bursts of excitement punctuated by longer stretches of contemplative travel, much like the way Davy himself experiences the world—jumping from one moment to the next, always aware of the distance he’s covered.

Critically, Jumper succeeds as both a coming‑of‑age story and a speculative exploration of freedom. Davy’s teleportation becomes a metaphor for the adolescent desire to escape constraints—whether they are the walls of an abusive home, the expectations of a small town, or the limits of one’s own body. Yet Gould never lets the metaphor overshadow the character; Davy remains flawed, occasionally reckless, and deeply relatable. His growth is not defined by mastering his power, but by learning when to use it, when to stay, and how to forge connections despite his ability to vanish at a moment’s notice.

The book’s reception—highlighted by praise from Booklist, Publishers Weekly, Romantic Times, and Science Fiction Chronicle—underscores its blend of thrilling premise and sincere emotional resonance. It is a novel that respects the intelligence of its audience, offering enough scientific plausibility to satisfy hard‑SF fans while delivering a heartfelt, character‑driven journey that appeals to lovers of coming‑of‑age fiction.

In sum, Jumper is a compelling debut that marries the excitement of instantaneous travel with the quiet, often painful quest for identity. Steven Gould’s assured storytelling, clear‑voiced prose, and thoughtful treatment of a classic SF trope make this novel a standout entry in the young adult canon—and a worthy precursor to the sequels that followed.
