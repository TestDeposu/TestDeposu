# The Most Popular President Who Ever Lived [So Far]

**Author:** Nancy Omeara  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

**The Most Popular President Who Ever Lived [So Far] by Nancy Omeara: A Playful, Provocative Reckoning with America’s Mythic Leader**

Nancy Omeara’s *The Most Popular President Who Ever Lived [So Far]* is not a biography in the traditional sense. It is, instead, a sharp, irreverent, and deeply engaging meditation on the cult of personality that surrounds Abraham Lincoln—a man whose legacy has been stretched, shrunk, and repurposed by every generation since his assassination. Omeara, a historian and cultural critic, doesn’t just dissect Lincoln’s presidency; she dismantles the very idea of how we remember presidents, how we mythologize them, and how that mythmaking shapes our national identity.

At its core, the book is a critique of Lincoln’s enduring popularity—not as a celebration, but as an indictment of how easily history is weaponized. Omeara argues that Lincoln’s image has been so carefully curated, so often repackaged for political and ideological purposes, that he has become less a historical figure and more a Rorschach test for American values. Was he the Great Emancipator? The Preserver of the Union? The Folksy Rail-Splitter? The Stoic Martyr? Omeara suggests that the answer depends entirely on who is doing the remembering—and for what purpose.

Her prose is razor-sharp, blending historical rigor with a wit that feels both scholarly and conversational. Omeara doesn’t shy away from the absurdities of Lincoln’s legacy. She points out how his face has been plastered on everything from Lincoln Logs to Lincoln Steaks, how his image has been co-opted by both the far left and the far right, and how his words are often cherry-picked to justify any number of modern causes. There’s a delightful undercurrent of satire here, as Omeara holds up a mirror to the ways we turn history into propaganda.

What makes the book so compelling is its refusal to take Lincoln’s myth at face value. Omeara doesn’t just list his accomplishments or lament his failures; she exposes the mechanisms by which his image has been mythologized. She examines how Lincoln’s face became a symbol of national unity, how his death was turned into a collective trauma, and how his legacy has been repurposed in every major crisis since—from the Civil War to the Civil Rights Movement to the COVID-19 pandemic. The result is a book that feels both timely and timeless, a reminder that the past is never as settled as we’d like to believe.

Omeara’s style is a masterclass in accessible scholarship. She avoids jargon, but never sacrifices depth. She weaves together personal anecdotes, historical evidence, and cultural observations with a fluidity that makes the book as engaging as it is informative. Whether she’s discussing the Lincoln Memorial’s role in the 1963 March on Washington or the way his image has been used in political campaigns, she keeps the reader hooked with a mix of humor, insight, and just the right amount of skepticism.

The book’s title itself is a provocation. Who, exactly, is the "most popular president who ever lived"? Omeara suggests that popularity isn’t just about approval ratings or public affection—it’s about how deeply a president’s image is embedded in the national psyche. Lincoln fits that bill, but not because he was universally beloved in his time. His popularity, Omeara implies, is a product of later generations projecting their own desires onto him. And that’s the real subject of the book: the danger of history as wish fulfillment.

One of the most striking aspects of *The Most Popular President Who Ever Lived [So Far]* is its relevance to the present moment. In an era where political figures are constantly rebranded, where history is often rewritten to fit contemporary narratives, Omeara’s book serves as a cautionary tale. She shows how easily a president’s legacy can be twisted, how quickly a symbol can become a weapon. And yet, she doesn’t offer easy answers. Instead, she invites readers to question their own assumptions about Lincoln—and by extension, about how we remember any historical figure.

The book’s greatest strength is its refusal to let Lincoln off the hook. He wasn’t perfect, Omeara makes clear, and neither was his legacy. She doesn’t shy away from the contradictions—his ambivalence on race, his political maneuvering, his personal flaws. But she also refuses to let his flaws overshadow his significance. The result is a nuanced, balanced portrait of a man who remains one of the most fascinating figures in American history.

In the end, *The Most Popular President Who Ever Lived [So Far]* is less a biography and more a cultural critique—a book that challenges us to think more critically about how we remember our leaders, and why we do it. Omeara’s writing is intelligent, engaging, and often funny, making it a must-read for anyone interested in history, politics, or the power of myth. It’s a book that will make you see Lincoln—and America—differently.
