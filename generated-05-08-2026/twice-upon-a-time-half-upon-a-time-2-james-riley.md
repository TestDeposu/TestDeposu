# Twice Upon a Time (Half Upon a Time, #2)

**Author:** James Riley  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

Twice Upon a Time plunges readers back into the topsy‑turvy realm where beloved fairy‑tale tropes are turned inside out, and James Riley’s trademark wit keeps the pages flying. The sequel to Half Upon a Time follows Jack and May as they chase the elusive truth about May’s lineage, a quest that thrusts them into the glittering, perilous court of the Sea King. Here, mermaids clash with the infamous Pirate Bluebeard, and the stakes are as much about self‑discovery as they are about swashbuckling battles.

At its heart, the novel explores the theme of identity forged through choice rather than birth. May’s struggle to reconcile the legacy of her enigmatic grandmother with her own budding sense of self mirrors Jack’s own journey from reluctant sidekick to courageous hero. Riley deftly uses the fantastical setting—a kingdom where oceans sing and pirates plot—to examine how external labels (princess, pirate, mermaid) can both confine and illuminate who we truly are. The tension between expectation and agency fuels the narrative, giving the adventure an emotional core that resonates beyond the laughs and sword fights.

Riley’s style is a breezy blend of rapid‑fire dialogue, tongue‑in‑cheek narration, and vivid, cinematic set‑pieces. He treats classic fairy‑tale motifs like building blocks, snapping them together with unexpected twists that feel both reverent and refreshingly irreverent. The humor never undercuts the tension; instead, it heightens it, allowing readers to catch their breath between daring escapes and heartfelt revelations. Descriptions are economical yet lush—enough to paint the iridescent scales of a mermaid’s tail or the grimy decks of Bluebeard’s ship without slowing the pace. This economy makes the book ideal for middle‑grade and young‑adult readers who crave action but also appreciate moments of quiet introspection.

What sets Twice Upon a Time apart from other fractured‑fairy‑tale sequels is Riley’s commitment to character growth amid the chaos. Jack’s loyalty and May’s curiosity evolve into genuine partnership, and their banter feels authentic rather than forced. The supporting cast—ranging from sassy sea‑folk to grizzled buccaneers—adds texture, each embodying a different facet of the story’s central question: What does it mean to write your own story when the world keeps trying to hand you a script?

In sum, Twice Upon a Time delivers a rollicking, heart‑filled romp that balances humor, adventure, and a thoughtful exploration of identity. Riley’s clever reimagining of familiar tales proves that even the most well‑worn narratives can surprise us when viewed through a fresh, daring lens.
