# God-Shaped Hole

**Author:** Tiffanie DeBartolo  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

Some love stories arrive quietly, whispering sweet nothings until you close the back cover. Others, however, hit you like a freight train fueled by whiskey, vinyl records, and existential dread. Tiffanie DeBartolo’s God-Shaped Hole belongs firmly to the latter category—a raw, bruising, and deeply intoxicating modern romance that refuses to play by the rules of conventional fiction. 

At its core, the novel explores a terrifyingly simple premise: What do you do when you are handed a life sentence of inevitable heartbreak? Beatrice Jordan, a cynical LA-based artist, carries the weight of a childhood prophecy predicting her true love will die young. Because of this dark omen, she keeps her heart guarded behind iron walls. Enter Jacob Grace, an unpredictable writer who answers a personal ad with the haunting phrase, "I am seeking a friend for the end of the world." 

DeBartolo’s central theme examines the profound paradox of human attachment. We live in a world where everything we love will eventually be lost, yet we plunge headfirst into passion anyway. The title itself points to that innate, aching void inside the human spirit—the space we desperately try to fill with art, ambition, and most dangerously, other people. Beatrice and Jacob do not simply fall in love; they collide. Their connection is forged in the dimly lit corners of hole-in-the-wall record stores, fueled by the kind of kinetic, artistic energy that feels entirely electric. 

What elevates God-Shaped Hole above the crowded sea of contemporary romance is DeBartolo’s distinctive stylistic voice. Hailed by critics as "this generation’s Love Story," the narrative moves with a restless, jagged brilliance. Comparisons to Holden Caulfield are well-earned; Beatrice is sharp, fiercely independent, deeply vulnerable, and possessed of a cynical wit that cuts right through any potential saccharine sentimentality. The author writes with a visceral punch, capturing the chaotic beauty of being young, messy, and creative in a world that often demands conformity. 

This is not a clean, fairy-tale romance where everyone gets to walk off into the sunset untouched. DeBartolo embraces the ugly truths of relationships—the secrets, the mounting dread, and the sheer terror of loving someone so much that their absence would mean your own destruction. Yet, despite the looming shadow of the fortune teller’s prophecy, the book is overwhelmingly life-affirming. It dares the reader to abandon caution, to live and love as vividly as possible, and to accept that a love worth having is worth the inevitable devastation it might bring.

If you are looking for a predictable beach read that neatly ties up all its loose ends, look elsewhere. But if you want a book that will utterly wreck you, stitch you back together, and change the way you look at the written word forever, open this novel. God-Shaped Hole is a testament to the fact that it is better to have had your heart broken by greatness than to have lived an entirely protected, empty life.
