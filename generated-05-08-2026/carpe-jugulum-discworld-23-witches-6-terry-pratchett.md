# Carpe Jugulum (Discworld, #23; Witches, #6)

**Author:** Terry Pratchett  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Carpe Jugulum, the sixth entry in Terry Pratchett’s beloved Witches sub‑series, is a deft blend of gothic horror tropes and razor‑sharp satire that feels both nostalgically familiar and startlingly fresh. Set in the mountain kingdom of Lancre, the story begins with King Verence’s well‑meaning but disastrously naïve decision to invite the urbane Magpyr vampire family to celebrate the birth of his daughter. What follows is a classic “don’t let the vampire in” scenario, turned on its head by Pratchett’s trademark wit: the Magpyrs are not the brooding, coffin‑dwelling monsters of legend but wine‑sipping, garlic‑munching, sun‑loving sophisticates who have embraced modernity with the same enthusiasm they apply to their eternal thirst for blood. Their arrival forces Lancre’s inhabitants—especially the indomitable Granny Weatherwax, the earthy Nanny Ogg, the newly confident Magrat, and the eager apprentice Agnes Nitt—to confront a threat that is as much cultural as it is supernatural.

At its core, Carpe Jugulum wrestles with the tension between tradition and progress, a theme that Pratchett explores through the lens of both witchcraft and vampirism. The witches represent an older, instinctual magic rooted in the land, community, and a pragmatic understanding of human nature. The Magpyrs, by contrast, embody a veneer of Enlightenment rationality and consumerist charm that masks an ancient predatory drive. Their polite conversation, fondness for modern conveniences, and disdain for the old superstitions serve as a satirical mirror to contemporary society’s tendency to sanitize or commodify the unsettling aspects of life. The witches, as usual, are the novel’s moral compass. Granny Weatherwax’s iconic brand of hard‑won wisdom—her refusal to be swayed by charm, her insistence on facing the truth head‑on—provides the narrative’s spine. Nanny Ogg offers a warm, earthy counterpoint, reminding readers that community and kinship are as vital as any spell. Magrat’s evolution from uncertain bride to a queen who can wield both diplomacy and a broomstick underscores the series’ ongoing exploration of feminine power. Agnes, the “lost” witch with a latent talent for music and a darker inner voice, introduces an intriguing internal conflict that mirrors the external one: the struggle between the desire to belong and the need to acknowledge one’s own shadow.

Pratchett’s prose remains accessible yet layered. He peppers the narrative with footnote‑like asides that reward attentive readers without interrupting the flow, and his world‑building continues to feel both expansive and intimate. Even as he sends up vampire lore—turning the classic aversion to garlic and sunlight into a joke about dietary preferences—he simultaneously pays homage to the genre’s roots, letting readers recognize the familiar beats before he subverts them. The result is a story that feels simultaneously like a comforting return to Discworld and an inventive step forward.

Thematically, Carpe Jugulum asks what happens when old defenses—superstition, ritual, and caution—are replaced by polite conversation and modern convenience. The witches’ answer is clear: you cannot negotiate with a predator by offering it a seat at the table; sometimes you must go for the throat, or as the vampires themselves proclaim, “Carpe Jugulum!”—seize the throat. This mantra captures both the literal and figurative core of the novel: the necessity of confronting uncomfortable truths head‑on, whether they come in the form of a charming aristocrat with a fang or the subtle creep of complacency in our own lives.

In sum, Carpe Jugulum is a brilliant showcase of Terry Pratchett’s ability to meld humor, horror, and heartfelt commentary into a single, unforgettable disc. It affirms that even in a world where vampires sip chardonnay and witches argue over tea, the oldest truths—about courage, community, and the courage to stare darkness in the eye—remain as vital as ever.
