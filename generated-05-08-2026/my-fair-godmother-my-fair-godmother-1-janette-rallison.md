# My Fair Godmother (My Fair Godmother, #1)

**Author:** Janette Rallison  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

A Prom Queen’s Medieval Detour: Wit and Whimsy in Janette Rallison’s My Fair Godmother

High school is a battlefield of shifting loyalties, broken hearts, and desperate social calculations. For sophomore Savannah, the landscape is particularly brutal after her boyfriend unceremoniously dumps her—not just for anyone, but for her own older sister. In the hierarchy of teenage indignities, this sits comfortably near the top. Enter the classic trope of the fairy godmother, designed to swoop in and fix the protagonist’s romantic woes with a flick of a wand. Yet, in Janette Rallison’s 2009 YA novel My Fair Godmother, nothing goes according to plan. Rallison takes the tired conventions of magical intervention and gives them a deliciously chaotic spin, delivering a sharp, laugh-out-loud critique of teenage priorities and romantic ideals.

At its core, the novel explores the timeless theme of misplaced desires versus genuine connection. Savannah is initially consumed by superficiality: her primary objective is securing the ultimate prom date to save face and exact a petty, sweet revenge on her ex. She views life through the lens of a romantic comedy where the aesthetics of the ending matter more than the substance of the journey. However, Rallison uses the catalyst of magic to dismantle these shallow aspirations. When a literal fairy godmother—who cheerfully admits she is only "fair" at her job—botches a spell, Savannah finds herself ripped from the familiar comforts of the modern high school and dumped unceremoniously into the muddy, perilous reality of the Middle Ages. 

This temporal displacement serves as a brilliant narrative device. By removing Savannah from her smartphone, social hierarchy, and Starbucks runs, Rallison forces her protagonist to confront survival, genuine danger, and authentic human emotion. Stranded alongside her is a mysterious boy who harbors ambitions of playing the dashing prince, yet proves that chivalry is much harder when armor is heavy and dragons—or at least medieval politics—are decidedly real. The juxtaposition between Savannah’s modern teenage angst and the stark backdrop of knights and castles creates a comedic friction that drives the narrative forward with relentless energy.

Rallison’s authorial style is the true star of the show. Her prose is breezy, self-aware, and packed with rapid-fire sarcasm that captures the authentic cadence of adolescent inner monologue. She writes with a comedic timing that relies heavily on irony and hyperbole, making Savannah an endearing narrator despite her initial superficiality. Rallison doesn't lecture her readers about the importance of looking beyond the surface; instead, she lets Savannah stumble through mud, insult medieval royalty, and misinterpret romantic cues until the lesson lands naturally. The dialogue is snappy, the pacing never lags, and the romantic tension develops organically amidst the absurdity of the situation. 

What makes My Fair Godmother a standout in the YA fantasy-romance genre is its refusal to take itself too seriously while still delivering genuine emotional stakes. Rallison balances laugh-out-loud slapstick with moments of quiet realization, showing that growing up often means trading the fantasy of the "perfect prom date" for someone who will stand by you when the castle walls are literally tumbling down. It is a charming, witty escapade that proves the best kind of magic is the kind that teaches you how to laugh at yourself.
