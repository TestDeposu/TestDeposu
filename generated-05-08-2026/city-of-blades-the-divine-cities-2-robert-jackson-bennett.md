# City of Blades (The Divine Cities, #2)

**Author:** Robert Jackson Bennett  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

**City of Blades: A Masterclass in War, Faith, and the Cost of Divinity**

Robert Jackson Bennett’s *City of Blades* is more than a sequel—it’s a deepening of the philosophical and tactical brilliance that made *City of Stairs* a standout in modern fantasy. Here, Bennett doesn’t just expand his world; he dissects it, exposing the raw, brutal mechanics of power, belief, and the human (or near-human) cost of godhood. The story unfolds in the shattered remnants of Voortyashtan, a city once ruled by a god of war and death, now a battleground of factions, rumors, and a discovery that could either restore lost divinity or doom the world to chaos.

At its core, *City of Blades* is a meditation on service, sacrifice, and the illusion of heroism. General Turyin Malaghesh, the grizzled, morally ambiguous protagonist from the first book, returns as a man who has seen too much—yet still answers the call. Bennett’s strength lies in his ability to make Turyin’s cynicism and competence feel earned, not just a trope. She’s a war criminal by reputation, a survivor by necessity, and a leader who understands that orders are often just another form of violence. Her journey here is less about redemption and more about the quiet, grinding realization that even in a world where gods are dead, the struggle for meaning never ends.

The prose is as sharp as the blades that litter the streets of Voortyashtan. Bennett’s worldbuilding is immersive without being overwhelming, weaving in historical echoes, religious dogma, and the physical toll of war with a poet’s eye. The city itself is a character—crumbling, haunted, and alive with the ghosts of its past. The author’s descriptions of battle are visceral, but they’re never gratuitous; they’re studies in strategy, in the way fear and discipline shape the outcome of a fight. There’s a moment early in the book where Turyin observes a skirmish from a distance, and Bennett’s prose captures the precision of war like a painter’s brushstroke: *"The clash of steel was a symphony of steel and bone, each note a life cut short."*

What makes *City of Blades* truly compelling is its exploration of faith and its absence. The death of Voortyashtan’s god has left a void, and the city’s inhabitants fill it with cults, prophecies, and desperate rituals. Bennett doesn’t shy away from the darker side of belief—how it can be weaponized, how it can justify atrocities, and how it can also be a fragile lifeline in a godless world. The discovery that sends Turyin on her mission isn’t just an artifact; it’s a catalyst for a religious revival, and Bennett handles the theological debates with the same care he gives to his political intrigue.

The supporting cast is equally compelling. The Prime Minister, a man clinging to power in the face of collapse, is a study in desperation. The rebels, idealistic yet pragmatic, offer a counterpoint to Turyin’s hardened worldview. And then there’s the revelation of the divine artifact itself—a thing of terrible beauty, its power tied to the very fabric of belief. Bennett doesn’t rush the payoff; he lets the tension build, layering mystery with moral ambiguity until the stakes feel existential.

By the time the final act arrives, *City of Blades* has done something rare: it’s made you care about the fate of a city that was once great, now broken. It’s a story about the weight of history, the burden of leadership, and the ways in which even the most hardened souls can be undone by the weight of their own choices. Bennett’s writing is never flashy for the sake of it; every line serves a purpose, whether it’s to deepen the lore, sharpen the conflict, or force the reader to confront uncomfortable truths about power and morality.

If *City of Stairs* was a masterclass in worldbuilding and character, *City of Blades* is a masterclass in tension and consequence. It’s a book that rewards patience, that lingers in the mind long after the last page. And it’s a testament to Bennett’s ability to take a setting that could easily become a backdrop and turn it into a living, breathing entity—one that demands to be understood, feared, and, ultimately, loved.

For fans of epic fantasy that’s more than just battles and magic, *City of Blades* is a must-read. It’s a story about the cost of divinity, the nature of faith, and the quiet heroism of those who carry the weight of the world on their shoulders.
