# Joan of Arc

**Author:** Mark Twain  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

When America’s greatest humorist falls completely in love with history’s greatest saint, the result is nothing short of a literary miracle. In Joan of Arc, published in a handsome 2021 edition that revitalizes a classic, Mark Twain achieves the unexpected. Known worldwide for his sharp satire, biting wit, and iconic tales of mischief along the Mississippi, Twain approached the life of the Maid of Orléans with absolute reverence, solemn dedication, and an open heart. The book is not merely a historical account; it is a passionate, deeply moving monument to courage, faith, and the indomitable human spirit.

Twain spent a dozen years meticulously researching the historical record before putting pen to paper, followed by two intensive years of writing. The fruit of this immense labor is a novel that he himself considered to be his finest achievement. While the broad strokes of Joan’s legendary life are universally known—the teenage peasant girl hearing celestial voices, convincing a hesitant dauphin, leading desperate armies to miraculous victories, crowning a king at Reims, and ultimately facing a vicious, politically motivated trial that ended in flames—Twain breathes fresh, pulsating breath into the well-trodden history. 

The masterstroke of the novel lies in its narrative architecture. To bridge the gap between historical distance and intimate human emotion, Twain invents a fictionalized memoirist, Louis de Conte, modeled after Joan’s real-life page. Through de Conte’s eyes, we do not simply observe a distant, marble-statue saint from history books; we witness a flesh-and-blood companion. We see Joan as an ordinary child playing in the meadows of Domrémy, sharing laughter and simple sorrows, long before the weight of a nation’s destiny crushed her shoulders. This framing device allows Twain to balance the epic grandeur of the Hundred Years War with poignant, domestic tenderness, creating a brilliant contrast that heightens the tragedy of her downfall.

The central theme of the novel is the terrifying cost of absolute purity in a world governed by cynicism and corruption. Twain explores how a completely selfless, incorruptible soul moves through a brutal medieval landscape like a beacon of blinding light. Joan’s unwavering conviction and profound moral clarity terrify the establishment—both English invaders and treacherous French Burgundians alike. Twain uses her tragic trial not just as a historical retelling, but as a scathing indictment of institutional hypocrisy, religious manipulation, and mob mentality. Yet, despite the grim inevitability of the stake, the prevailing theme is one of transcendent triumph. Twain demonstrates that while tyrants and judges can destroy a fragile human body, they can never extinguish the eternal fire of a liberated spirit.

Stylistically, this book reveals a side of Mark Twain that startles and enchants readers accustomed to Tom Sawyer or Huckleberry Finn. Eschewing his trademark colloquial irony for much of the narrative, Twain adopts a lyrical, elevated tone that mimics the cadence of medieval chronicles, yet retains the crystal-clear storytelling prowess that made him a literary titan. His prose is both majestic and deeply empathetic. He paints the sweeping battlefields and the suffocating courtroom with equal artistic mastery, ensuring that every page pulses with dramatic tension.

Ultimately, Joan of Arc stands as a magnificent union of author and subject: the story of one of the greatest women in history, told by one of history’s greatest storytellers. It is a work of staggering emotional depth, historical fidelity, and literary grace that deserves a cherished place on the bookshelf of any serious reader.
