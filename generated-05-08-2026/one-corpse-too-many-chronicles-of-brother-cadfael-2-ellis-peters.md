# One Corpse Too Many (Chronicles of Brother Cadfael, #2)

**Author:** Ellis Peters  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

In One Corpse Too Many, the second installment of Ellis Peters’ acclaimed Chronicles of Brother Cadfael, the author triumphantly proves that historical mystery is not merely about solving puzzles, but about resurrecting an entire era with breath, blood, and belief. Set against the chaotic backdrop of twelfth-century England—specifically the brutal civil war between King Stephen and the Empress Maud—this novel transcends the traditional whodunit, offering a rich, atmospheric tapestry where medieval politics and quiet monastic life violently collide.

The narrative unfurls in the summer of 1138 outside the walls of the Abbey of Saint Peter and Saint Paul in Shrewsbury. When Shrewsbury Castle falls to King Stephen’s forces, ninety-four defenders loyal to the empress are mercilessly hanged as traitors. Brother Cadfael, a Welsh-born Benedictine monk whose past encompasses the Crusades and a worldly life before the cowl, is tasked with the grim duty of burying the executed men. It is here that Cadfael’s sharp eye and gentle compassion uncover a chilling anomaly: among the ninety-four dangling bodies lies a ninety-fifth corpse, one that has been deliberately strangled rather than hanged. 

This single, meticulously placed pivot point sets the novel’s central theme in motion: the sanctity of individual human life amidst mass carnage and political indifference. While the barons, kings, and military commanders view lives as expendable pawns on a bloody chessboard, Cadfael sees a soul worthy of justice. To the victors, one extra dead rebel is a statistical insignificance; to the observant herbalist and former soldier, it is an intolerable moral outrage that demands redress. Peters uses this stark contrast to explore the tension between institutional power and personal morality, showing how institutional cruelty often breeds clandestine malice.

Ellis Peters’ literary style is the true beating heart of the novel. Her prose is lush, immersive, and deceptively effortless, transporting the reader directly into the muddy, fervent, and plant-scented world of the Middle Ages. She does not merely list historical facts; she evokes the sensory details of the era—the smell of crushed herbs in the abbey garden, the damp chill of stone cells, the clanking armor of feudal lords, and the quiet rustle of a habit. Her style marries the intellectual rigor of the classic puzzle mystery with the evocative depth of historical fiction. 

Furthermore, Cadfael himself is a masterstroke of character design. He is neither a infallible mastermind nor a naive holy man. Armed with a pragmatic worldly wisdom gleaned from his adventurous youth, a profound knowledge of human nature, and a deep expertise in botany and medicine, Cadfael investigates the crime using both forensic intuition and spiritual empathy. As he pieces together disparate clues—a mysterious girl disguised in boy’s clothing, a missing royal treasure, and a single, fragile broken flower left upon the victim—Peters crafts an intricate, fair-play mystery that keeps readers guessing until the final pages.

One Corpse Too Many is a triumph of historical mystery writing. It captures the turbulence of a divided nation while remaining intensely intimate, focusing on the quiet courage of one man determined to bring hidden darkness into the light. For lovers of atmospheric storytelling, intricate plots, and unforgettable characters, Peters' medieval masterpiece remains an absolute essential read.
