# Hawkwood's Voyage (The Monarchies of God, #1)

**Author:** Paul Kearney  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

A continent consumed by holy fire, a sea shrouded in the terrifying unknown, and a desperate gamble for survival: Paul Kearney’s Hawkwood’s Voyage, the opening salvo of The Monarchies of God series, is a masterclass in relentless, grimdark fantasy. First published in the mid-1990s and enduring as a cult classic, this 2013 edition invites a new generation of readers into a world where salvation is as rare as mercy, and hope is measured in leagues sailed across a monster-haunted ocean.

At its core, the main theme of Hawkwood's Voyage is the horrific futility of ideological fracture in the face of absolute annihilation. Kearney paints a vivid, harrowing portrait of a Western World teetering on the brink. While the unstoppable, zealot-driven Merduk armies sweep across cities and cathedrals, leaving cruciform horrors in their wake, the supposedly "Faithful" are too busy tearing themselves apart. Religious fanaticism and institutional paranoia have birthed the Inceptines, a merciless inquisitorial force purging heretics and magical folk with cleansing pyres. Kearney uses this backdrop not merely for dark window dressing, but as a scathing critique of human factionalism. Even as the walls crumble, the rulers of the Monarchies would rather burn their own kingdoms than share power or tolerate difference. It is a suffocating atmosphere of impending doom, brilliantly rendered through prose that never wastes a word.

Against this backdrop of systemic collapse emerges Richard Hawkwood, the quintessential world-weary captain, and his desperate crew. Alongside Lord Murad—a king’s cousin clutching a faded, ancient logbook—Hawkwood’s voyage across the Great Western Ocean to an uncharted, unspoiled continent becomes the narrative's beating heart. This sea-crossing motif shifts the novel from a standard medieval-style turf war into a grand, perilous Age of Discovery fantasy. The ocean is not just a body of water; it is a leviathan of uncertainty, offering a fragile thread of escape from the fanaticism choking the mainland.

Kearney’s style is the true engine driving this grim voyage. Eschewing the sprawling, encyclopedia-length world-building of many modern epic fantasies, Kearney practices a sleek, ruthless economy of style. He drops the reader directly into the deep end of his world, trusting us to piece together the geopolitics, military tactics, and theological tensions through rapid-fire dialogue and visceral action. His prose is lean, muscular, and unromanticized. Battles are short, brutal, and chaotic; magic is rare, dangerous, and treated with superstitious terror; and characters are forged in the crucible of impossible choices rather than destiny-driven prophecies. 

There is an undeniable maritime grit to the chapters aboard Hawkwood’s ship, where the salty spray mixes with the quiet terror of the unknown. Kearney captures the claustrophobia of the sea and the agonizing weight of leadership. Hawkwood is no shining knight; he is a professional survivor trying to keep his ship afloat in a world intent on going under. 

Hawkwood’s Voyage is a gripping, fast-paced overture to a much larger symphony of war and exploration. It does not coddle the reader, nor does it offer easy comforts. Instead, it delivers a gritty, atmospheric punch that lingers long after the final page is turned. For fans of intelligent, high-stakes fantasy that combines historical grit with the vast terror of uncharted horizons, Kearney’s epic remains an essential, unmissable journey.
