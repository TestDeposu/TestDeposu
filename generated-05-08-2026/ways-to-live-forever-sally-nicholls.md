# Ways to Live Forever

**Author:** Sally Nicholls  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

**Ways to Live Forever by Sally Nicholls: A Luminous, Heartbreaking Chronicle of a Boy’s Last Stories**

Sally Nicholls’ *Ways to Live Forever* is not just a novel—it is a quiet revolution in children’s literature, a book that dares to confront mortality with the same curiosity and wonder it finds in the world. Through the voice of eleven-year-old Sam, a boy with leukemia who knows his time is running out, Nicholls crafts a work that is as tender as it is devastating, as playful as it is profound. This debut novel, published in 2013, is a masterclass in balancing raw emotion with childlike ingenuity, proving that even in the face of death, life’s beauty and absurdity remain irrepressible.

Sam’s narrative is structured like a collection of fragments—lists, questions, stories, and observations—each one a snapshot of his world as he navigates chemotherapy, hospital stays, and the slow unraveling of his body. The book opens with five stark, declarative sentences, the last of which delivers the inescapable truth: *"By the time you read this, I will probably be dead."* This bluntness is not cruel; it is honest, and it sets the tone for a story that refuses to sugarcoat but never loses its humanity. Sam’s voice is unfiltered, sometimes whimsical, sometimes heartbreaking, but always authentic. He is a boy who, despite knowing he is dying, still finds joy in the mundane—a favorite snack, a silly joke, the way his dog’s tail wags—and in the extraordinary, like the stories he reads or the "fantastic facts" he collects. His perspective is uniquely his own: a child’s, but one sharpened by the weight of his impending loss.

Nicholls’ genius lies in her ability to make the unthinkable feel intimate and immediate. Sam’s illness is never romanticized; the nausea, the fatigue, the fear are all present, but they are framed within the context of his life, not just his death. He writes about the "boring" parts—waiting for the bus, watching TV, arguing with his sister—as if they are just as important as the profound moments. This refusal to glorify suffering or dwell only on the tragic makes *Ways to Live Forever* deeply moving. It is a book that respects the reader’s intelligence, acknowledging that grief is not a single emotion but a tapestry of confusion, anger, love, and even laughter.

The style is deceptively simple. Nicholls’ prose is spare, often conversational, as if Sam is dictating his thoughts to a friend. There are no grand metaphors or melodramatic flourishes; instead, the power comes from the raw, unadorned truth of his experiences. For example, when Sam describes the "silly" way his parents react to his illness—his dad’s attempts at humor, his mom’s quiet strength—it is these small, human details that make the story resonate. The book also incorporates visual elements, like sketches of Sam’s "story monsters" or lists of "Things I Like," which add texture and a sense of immediacy. These touches ground the narrative in the tactile, reminding us that even in the abstract struggle against death, life is lived through touch, taste, and sight.

Yet for all its tenderness, *Ways to Live Forever* is not a book about despair. It is, at its core, a celebration of life—of stories, of connection, of the way we make meaning in the face of the unknown. Sam’s final wish is to "live forever" through the stories he leaves behind, and Nicholls honors that desire by crafting a narrative that feels eternal in its own right. The book’s structure, with its fragmented, almost collage-like approach, mirrors Sam’s own fragmented existence, but it also suggests that life, like art, can be pieced together from the smallest, most disparate parts.

There is a quiet defiance in *Ways to Live Forever*. Sam does not accept his fate passively; he fights it with the same vigor he brings to his favorite books or his obsession with dinosaurs. His resilience is not in denial but in his refusal to let his illness define him entirely. He is still a boy who loves pizza, who gets annoyed by his sister, who wonders about the universe. This balance—between the fragility of his body and the indomitable spirit of his mind—is what makes the book so compelling.

Nicholls also excels in her portrayal of Sam’s relationships. His family is drawn with warmth and complexity; his parents’ love is palpable, even in their moments of frustration or grief. His sister, Emily, is a foil to his introspection, her youthful exasperation contrasting with his quiet depth. And then there is his friend, the nurse who becomes a confidante, and the doctors who, despite their clinical detachment, are forced to see him as more than a patient. These relationships are not just backdrops but integral to the story, showing how love and connection can make even the most unbearable circumstances bearable.

The book’s themes are universal, yet it never feels preachy. Death is not the only focus; life, in all its messy, beautiful complexity, is just as central. Sam’s lists—"Things That Make Me Happy," "Things I’m Afraid Of"—serve as a reminder that even in the face of mortality, we are still trying to make sense of the world. Nicholls’ handling of these themes is sensitive but never sentimental. She does not shy away from the pain, but she also refuses to let it consume the story. Instead, she shows how pain and joy can coexist, how a child can look at the end of life and still find wonder in the smallest things.

*Ways to Live Forever* is a book that lingers long after the last page. It is the kind of story that stays with you because it is honest, because it is brave, and because it reminds you that even in the darkest moments, there is still room for light. Nicholls’ debut is a testament to the power of children’s literature to explore the deepest, most difficult truths with grace and courage. It is a book that should be read not just by children, but by anyone who has ever wondered how to live when you know your time is limited.

In the end, Sam’s story is a reminder that we are all, in some way, collecting stories—of ourselves, of those we love, of the world around us. And perhaps, in doing so, we can find a way to live forever, not in immortality, but in the memories we leave behind.
