# The Anvil of the World (Lord Ermenwyr, #1)

**Author:** Kage Baker  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2026

**The Anvil of the World: A Masterclass in Dark Whimsy and Moral Ambiguity**

Kage Baker’s *The Anvil of the World*—the first novel in the *Lord Ermenwyr* series—is a genre-defying marvel, a story that wears its influences lightly while carving out its own distinct territory in fantasy. At its heart, this is a tale of redemption, violence, and the fragile illusion of peace, wrapped in the kind of lush, subversive storytelling that feels both timeless and utterly original. Baker, a master of the Company universe’s time-travel intrigue, brings her signature wit and social commentary to this fantasy outing, but here, the stakes are personal, the world is vividly alive, and the moral gray areas are as deep as the blood feuds that haunt its characters.

The protagonist, Smith, is a man who has spent his life as a professional killer, a member of the infamous Children of the Sun—a clan where blood vengeance is both tradition and survival. Now, weary of the trade, he seeks nothing more than a quiet life in obscurity, far from the men who would slay him for past sins. But fate, as it so often does in Baker’s work, has other plans. When he agrees to oversee a caravan from the inland city of Troon to the coastal haven of Salesh, he unwittingly steps into a world of shifting loyalties, supernatural threats, and the ever-present shadow of the Master of the Mountain—a demonic figure whose gaze broods over the land like a curse. The journey becomes a crucible, testing Smith’s resolve to leave his violent past behind while forcing him to confront the consequences of his choices.

What makes *The Anvil of the World* so compelling is Baker’s ability to blend the grand and the mundane with effortless grace. The novel is steeped in the kind of rich, atmospheric detail that makes the world feel tangible—from the gourmet cuisine that hints at a society where even assassins dine on fine food, to the surreal image of 144 glass butterflies fluttering through a steamboat, a touch of magic that feels both whimsical and unsettling. There’s a playful nod to classic fantasy here, a wink to the traditions of Lord Dunsany and Fritz Leiber, but Baker never lets the story become a pastiche. Instead, she weaves in her own brand of dark humor and moral complexity, ensuring that no character is purely good or evil. Even the demons, like the decadent Lord Ermenwyr, are layered with pathos and charm, their presence adding a layer of existential dread to the adventure.

The novel’s strength lies in its exploration of themes like guilt, redemption, and the illusion of peace. Smith’s journey is less about escaping his past and more about understanding it—a process that’s as brutal as it is necessary. The caravan’s trials, from supernatural ambushes to the slow unraveling of alliances, serve as a metaphor for the violence that lingers in the soul long after the body has moved on. And yet, for all its darkness, the book never loses sight of its humanity. There’s warmth in the relationships Smith forms, even if they’re fleeting, and a quiet hope that perhaps, just perhaps, a man can outrun his demons—literally and figuratively.

Baker’s prose is deceptively simple, yet every word carries weight. Her dialogue crackles with wit and menace, her descriptions are vivid without being overwrought, and her pacing is a masterclass in tension. The novel moves at a brisk clip, but it never feels rushed—each encounter, each revelation, is earned. And when the story reaches its climax, it doesn’t shy away from the brutal consequences of Smith’s choices, delivering a payoff that’s as satisfying as it is inevitable.

What truly sets *The Anvil of the World* apart, however, is its refusal to conform to genre expectations. This isn’t a traditional fantasy quest with a chosen one and a prophecy. It’s a story about a man trying to live, about the cost of peace in a world where violence is both currency and legacy. It’s a tale that balances the absurd and the profound, the magical and the mundane, in a way that feels both fresh and deeply rooted in the traditions of the genre. Baker’s ability to make the extraordinary feel grounded—and the grounded feel extraordinary—is what makes this book a standout.

For readers who crave fantasy that’s as intellectually engaging as it is emotionally resonant, *The Anvil of the World* is a must-read. It’s a story that lingers long after the last page, not just because of its twists or its world-building, but because of the way it forces you to question what it means to be free, to be redeemed, and to leave the past behind. In an era where fantasy often prioritizes spectacle over substance, Baker’s work is a refreshing reminder that the best stories are the ones that challenge us, haunt us, and leave us changed.
