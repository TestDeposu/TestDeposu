# Brothers of the Wind (The Last King of Osten Ard, #0.75)

**Author:** Tad Williams  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

**Brothers of the Wind: A Glimpse into the Mythic Shadows of Osten Ard**

Tad Williams, the mastermind behind *The Memory of Light* and the sprawling saga of Osten Ard, has once again woven a tale that lingers like the whisper of an ancient wind. *Brothers of the Wind*, a prequel to *The Dragonbone Chair*, is not merely a story—it is a fragment of legend, a cautionary fable that explores the cost of pride, the fragility of loyalty, and the weight of a single reckless oath. Set a thousand years before the events that would shape the fate of the Sithi, this short novel is a masterclass in atmospheric storytelling, blending mythic grandeur with the intimate ache of human (or, in this case, *Sithi*) folly.

At its heart, *Brothers of the Wind* is a tragedy of brothers—Hakatri, the steadfast and Hidohebhi-slaying hero, and Ineluki, the fiery younger sibling whose pride will echo through eternity as the Storm King. Their story is told through the eyes of Pamon Kes, a Changeling servant bound to Hakatri’s service, whose voice is both a lifeline and a burden. Kes is not a Sithi; he is an outsider, a witness to the grandeur and brutality of immortal beings, and his narrative perspective grounds the myth in something achingly human. Williams deftly contrasts the eternal, godlike nature of the Sithi with the fleeting, mortal concerns of Kes, creating a tension that underscores the novel’s central theme: *how quickly hubris can unravel destinies.*

The prose is rich, immersive, and laced with the same lyrical precision that defines Williams’ work. His descriptions of Osten Ard’s landscapes—vast, mist-shrouded forests, towering cliffs, and the ever-present threat of the black dragon—are vivid enough to make the reader feel the chill of the wind or the weight of a promise made in haste. Yet, beneath the grandeur lies a quiet, devastating humanity. Ineluki’s oath to slay Hidohebhi is not just an act of defiance; it is a moment of reckless ambition, a single choice that will haunt him—and the world—for eternity. Williams doesn’t shy away from the consequences; he lets them unfold with the inevitability of fate itself.

What makes *Brothers of the Wind* so compelling is its role as a *prequel*—not just in terms of chronology, but in terms of mythmaking. It hints at the origins of the Storm King, a figure whose legend looms over the later books of Osten Ard. By exploring Ineluki’s fall, Williams foreshadows the cyclical nature of power and ruin, a theme that will resonate deeply with fans of the series. Yet, the novel stands on its own as a standalone tragedy, a meditation on loyalty, sacrifice, and the cost of pride.

The Changeling perspective is particularly striking. Kes is not a passive observer; he is an active participant in the drama, his loyalty tested in ways that force him to confront the limits of his devotion. His voice is warm, wry, and deeply human, providing a counterpoint to the Sithi’s godlike detachment. Williams uses Kes to explore themes of servitude and freedom, showing how even the most loyal servants are shaped by the hands they serve—and the choices they make when those hands falter.

The pacing is deliberate, almost ceremonial, as if the story itself is unfolding in slow motion, each word carrying the weight of inevitability. There are no grand battles here—just a single, fateful encounter with a black dragon, a broken oath, and the slow unraveling of a kingdom’s fate. It is a story of quiet devastation, where the real horror lies not in the monster, but in the choices that lead to it.

For fans of Osten Ard, *Brothers of the Wind* is a treasure—a glimpse into the mythic past that will deepen their appreciation of the world Williams has built. For newcomers, it is a perfect introduction to his signature style: rich, atmospheric, and steeped in the kind of grand, tragic storytelling that lingers long after the last page.

In the end, *Brothers of the Wind* is a reminder that even in a world of immortals, pride is a mortal sin. And sometimes, the fall is so catastrophic that it changes everything.
