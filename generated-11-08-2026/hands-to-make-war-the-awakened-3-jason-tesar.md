# Hands To Make War (The Awakened, #3)

**Author:** Jason Tesar  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Hands To Make War thrusts the reader into the climactic third act of Jason Tesar’s Awakened trilogy, where the fractured lives of Kael, Maeryn, Saba, and Adair converge against an ancient evil that threatens to unravel the Orudan Empire. The novel’s central theme—destiny forged through struggle and sacrifice—plays out on multiple fronts: a young warrior’s quest to master his own power, a mother’s fight to protect her unborn child while leading a slave uprising, a captive scholar’s search for a forgotten past, and a displaced colonel’s desperate attempt to bridge worlds. Tesar weaves these threads into a tapestry that questions whether individuals can truly shape their fate when larger forces—mythic, political, and technological—pull the strings.

What distinguishes Tesar’s storytelling is his ability to blend genre conventions without letting any one overwhelm the others. The fantasy element appears in the mystic order of clerics, the whispered prophecies, and the lingering echo of forgotten gods. Simultaneously, the sci‑fi pulse runs through the Orudan Empire’s militaristic hierarchy, the secretive technological organization that shields Adair, and the stark, almost clinical depictions of warfare that feel lifted from a near‑future conflict. Military suspense grounds the narrative in tangible stakes: siege tactics, covert exoduses, prison breaks, and council intrigues are rendered with a crisp, almost reportorial precision that makes every battle feel urgent and every political maneuver feel consequential.

Tesar’s prose is lean yet evocative, favoring short, punchy sentences during action scenes that accelerate the reader’s heartbeat, then loosening into longer, lyrical passages when characters confront internal dilemmas or uncover fragments of lore. This rhythmic shift mirrors the novel’s oscillation between external conflict and inner turmoil. The multiple points of view are handled deftly; each chapter feels like a focused vignette that nevertheless contributes to the larger mosaic. Kael’s chapters crackle with the kinetic energy of a swordsman discovering his true strength; Maeryn’s sections resonate with a quieter, steely determination as she navigates leadership and maternal love; Saba’s narrative carries the weight of a scholar piecing together a shattered identity; Adair’s interludes provide a sci‑fi anchor, his scientific curiosity contrasting with the more mystical strands of the plot.

The novel’s greatest strength lies in its world‑building, which never feels like an exposition dump. Instead, details about the Orudan Empire’s caste system, the Resistance’s covert networks, and the enigmatic “organization protecting him” emerge organically through dialogue, recovered artifacts, and the characters’ own observations. Tesar trusts the reader to infer the larger picture from these scattered clues, rewarding attentive reading with a sense of discovery that fuels the story’s momentum.

Yet the book is not without its shortcomings. The sheer breadth of plotlines occasionally strains cohesion; some secondary characters appear and disappear with little resolution, leaving threads that feel more tantalizing than tied. The pacing, while generally brisk, can falter during prolonged council debates where the political machinations become dense enough to slow the narrative’s forward drive. Additionally, the climax leans heavily on the trope of an ancient evil awakening—a motif that, while executed with sufficient vigor, may feel familiar to seasoned fantasy readers.

Overall, Hands To Make War delivers a satisfying, high‑octane conclusion to the Awakened saga. Tesar’s skillful melding of fantasy, sci‑fi, and military suspense creates a reading experience that is both intellectually engaging and viscerally thrilling. Fans of layered world‑building, morally gray protagonists, and epic confrontations will find much to admire, even as they hope for a tighter resolution of the series’ looser ends.
