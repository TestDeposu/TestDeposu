# The King's Own (Borderlands, #2)

**Author:** Lorna Freeman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

Title: The Weight of the Crown and the Cost of Magic: A Review of Lorna Freeman’s The King's Own

In the second installment of her acclaimed Borderlands saga, Lorna Freeman returns to a realm where political maneuvering is just as perilous as raw, untamed magic. Published in 2010, The King's Own takes the rich world-building of its predecessor and narrows the lens onto the heavy, often agonizing burden of transition. Freeman’s novel is not merely a tale of high fantasy; it is an intimate exploration of identity, destiny, and the agonizing friction between personal desire and absolute duty.

At the heart of the narrative is Rabbit, a protagonist who continues to captivate readers through his profound vulnerability. Struggling to reconcile the terrifying expanse of his newly awakened powers with the suffocating prestige of his position as King Jusson's heir, Rabbit is a hero defined by his reluctance. He is no swaggering conqueror stepping easily into a birthright; instead, he is a young man constantly off-balance, trying to navigate a court that views him simultaneously as a miraculous savior and a political weapon to be wielded. 

Freeman’s central theme in The King's Own is the inescapable shadow of the past. This motif is crystallized brilliantly when a man, once scorned by Rabbit’s mother, emerges from the fringes of history demanding retribution. His weapon of choice is not steel or fire, but a calculated demand: Rabbit must marry his daughter. With this single, razor-sharp plot device, Freeman elevates the narrative from a standard magical coming-of-age story into a tense domestic and geopolitical chess match. Rabbit finds himself cornered, forced to realize that his magical abilities—no matter how formidable—cannot simply blast away a social contract or a personal vendetta. 

What truly sets Freeman apart in the crowded landscape of epic fantasy is her distinct literary style. Her prose is patient, evocative, and deeply atmospheric, favoring emotional resonance and psychological realism over breakneck, superficial pacing. She has a rare gift for making the quiet moments—a hesitant conversation in a drafty corridor, the heavy silence before a council meeting, the internal terror of losing control—just as gripping as any grand confrontation. The world of the Borderlands feels lived-in and weathered, populated by complex characters whose loyalties are rarely black-and-white. Freeman avoids easy tropes; her antagonists are motivated by grievances that feel chillingly human, making the conflicts all the more resonant.

As Rabbit stumbles forward into his responsibilities, readers are treated to a masterclass in character-driven fantasy. The forced betrothal plotline introduces a crackling dynamic of tension, suspicion, and reluctant growth, proving that Freeman is just as adept at writing intricate interpersonal drama as she is at managing sweeping magical stakes. 

The King's Own is a compelling, striking continuation of a beloved saga. It challenges its protagonist—and the reader—to consider what it truly means to belong to a kingdom, and what sacrifices must be made when the past finally comes knocking at the palace gates. For fans of intelligent, character-focused fantasy that balances high-stakes politics with deep emotional truth, Freeman’s latest chapter is an absolute triumph.
