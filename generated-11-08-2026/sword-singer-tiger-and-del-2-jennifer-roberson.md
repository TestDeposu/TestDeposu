# Sword-Singer (Tiger and Del, #2)

**Author:** Jennifer Roberson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1988

In Sword-Singer, the second installment of her acclaimed Tiger and Del series, Jennifer Roberson cements her status as a master of character-driven heroic fantasy. Published in 1988, this compelling novel dives deeper into the complex, volatile dynamic between its two iconic protagonists: the fierce, emotionally guarded Northern sword master Del, and Tiger, the charming yet deadly Southron warrior of mysterious origins. Roberson takes the classic wandering-duo trope and infuses it with psychological depth, cultural friction, and visceral action, delivering a sequel that surpasses the strong foundation laid by its predecessor.

The central theme of Sword-Singer is redemption through confrontation, intertwined with the heavy burdens of tradition and personal identity. The narrative hinges on a high-stakes premise: Del must return to the frozen North, specifically to the sacred Place of Swords, to stand trial-by-combat for the slaying of her own sword-master. This journey is not merely physical, though the harsh Northern wilderness and hostile pursuers provide relentless peril; it is a profound psychological reckoning. Del is forced to confront her past, the rigid and often unforgiving codes of her people, and the trauma that shaped her stoic exterior. Roberson brilliantly explores how culture dictates honor and how the individual must carve out a sense of self despite the crushing weight of societal expectations. 

Tiger, serving as both Del’s foil and her steadfast anchor, brings a vital thematic contrast to the narrative. Representing the warmth and openness of the South, he challenges Del's rigid isolationism. Their evolving relationship forms the emotional beating heart of the book. Roberson uses their banter, growing trust, and simmering romantic tension to explore themes of partnership, vulnerability, and mutual respect. Tiger is no mere damsel in distress or standard sidekick; he is an equal force, both in wit and martial prowess, whose loyalty is tested to its absolute limits in the frozen wastes.

Roberson’s authorial style is defined by its kinetic energy, sharp dialogue, and immersive world-building. Her prose is punchy and direct, perfectly mirroring the lethal precision of the sword-singing art itself. Unlike many fantasy novels of the late 1980s that bogged themselves down in exhaustive lore dumps, Roberson reveals her world organically through the cultural clashes between Tiger and Del. The North is not just a backdrop; it is a living, breathing entity characterized by bitter cold, ancient laws, and unyielding pride, contrasting sharply with Tiger’s memories of sun-drenched climes.

Furthermore, Roberson’s depiction of swordplay remains one of the high-water marks of the subgenre. She writes combat not as mere choreography, but as a fatal dialogue between opponents—a lethal dance requiring total mental and physical synchronization. When Del enters the Place of Swords, the tension is palpable, rendered with a breathless urgency that keeps the reader turning pages long into the night.

Sword-Singer is a triumph of 1980s fantasy, offering a masterclass in how to write a sequel that deepens characters rather than simply repeating a winning formula. It is a gritty, emotional, and thrilling ride that celebrates resilience, the cost of freedom, and the rare, hard-won bond between two legendary warriors. Roberson delivers a striking adventure that leaves an indelible mark on the landscape of sword and sorcery.
