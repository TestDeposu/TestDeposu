# Spell Games (Marla Mason, #4)

**Author:** T.A. Pratt  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Spell Games (Marla Mason, #4) by T.A. Pratt plunges readers back into the neon‑slick streets of Felport, where magic is as much a street‑level hustle as it is a cosmic force. The novel opens with Marla Mason, the city’s chief sorcerer, trying to keep her apprentice oracle from blowing a fuse while simultaneously dodging the spectral specter of her own past: her con‑artist brother, Jason. What begins as a personal tug‑of‑war quickly escalates into a high‑stakes con that threatens to unleash a fungal apocalypse upon the town. Pratt’s premise is deliciously lurid—brain‑eating Borrichius spores, a wannabe mage with deep pockets, and a reclusive sorcerer who worships a mushroom god—yet it serves a deeper purpose: exploring how the ties that bind us can also be the cords that choke us.

The central theme of Spell Games is the fraught dance between duty and kinship. Marla’s official role demands she protect Felport from any threat, magical or mundane. Her personal loyalty, however, pulls her toward Jason, whose latest scheme is less a harmless grift and more a gamble with forces that could unravel reality itself. Pratt uses this tension to interrogate what it means to be a guardian when the people you love are also the ones most likely to endanger the very thing you swore to protect. The novel never lets the reader settle into a comfortable moral binary; instead, it shows Marla constantly recalibrating her sense of responsibility, weighing the cost of familial forgiveness against the safety of an entire community. In doing so, Pratt elevates the urban‑fantasy romp into a meditation on legacy—how the sins and strengths of our bloodlines shape the choices we make when the stakes are otherworldly.

Stylistically, Pratt’s prose is razor‑sharp and impatient, mirroring Marla’s own no‑nonsense attitude. Sentences snap forward with a kinetic energy that keeps the plot hurtling from one supernatural set‑piece to the next. Dialogue crackles with wit and a distinctively world‑weary sarcasm; Marla’s voice is both hardened and oddly vulnerable, allowing readers to feel her exhaustion and flickers of hope in equal measure. Pratt excels at sensory detail: the dank, earthy smell of the Borrichius spores, the slick, almost sentient feel of vegetal magic creeping through Felport’s alleys, and the neon‑glow of the city’s night market are rendered with a tactile immediacy that makes the fantastical feel palpably real. Humor is woven throughout, often arriving as a darkly comic beat that undercuts the horror just enough to keep the tone from becoming oppressive. This balance of grit and levity is a hallmark of the series and is on full display here.

What sets Spell Games apart from typical urban‑fantasy fare is how Pratt uses the bizarre premise of a “mushroom god” to explore genuine ecological anxiety without turning the narrative into a polemic. The vegetal magic isn’t just a flashy power‑up; it’s a metaphor for unchecked growth, the way invasive species can overwhelm an ecosystem if left unchecked. By framing the threat as a spore‑based apocalypse, Pratt invites readers to consider the consequences of exploiting nature for personal gain—mirroring Jason’s exploitative con‑artist mindset. Yet the novel never feels didactic; the philosophical undercurrents remain submerged beneath the action, surfacing only in moments of quiet reflection when Marla confronts the fallout of her brother’s deceit.

The pacing is relentless, yet Pratt finds space for character development. Marla’s apprentice oracle, whose magical wires are hilariously and tragically crossed, provides both comic relief and a mirror to Marla’s own struggles with control and trust. Rondeau, Marla’s partner, is drawn deeper into Jason’s sting, showcasing how easily loyalty can be twisted when ambition and desperation collide. These subplots reinforce the main theme: every character is negotiating their own version of the family‑versus‑duty conflict, making the novel’s emotional core resonate beyond the central sibling rivalry.

In terms of criticism, the novel’s breakneck speed occasionally leaves some world‑building details under‑explored. Readers new to the Marla Mason series might find themselves scrambling to catch up on the intricate magic system or the political landscape of Felport’s occult underworld. However, longtime fans will appreciate the way Pratt rewards continuity, weaving callbacks and evolving relationships into the narrative without slowing the momentum. The climax, while satisfyingly explosive, leans heavily on the deus ex machina trope of a last‑minute magical switcheroo; yet, given the series’ tradition of inventive spellcraft, it feels earned rather than cheap.

Overall, Spell Games is a thrilling, tightly plotted entry that combines the series’ signature blend of horror, humor, and hard‑boiled sorcery with a poignant examination of familial obligation. Pratt’s voice remains as distinctive as ever—acerbic, vivid, and unafraid to let her heroine stumble, bleed, and ultimately stand taller in the face of both eldritch terrors and the ghosts of her own bloodline. For readers who crave urban fantasy that bites as hard as it laughs, this installment is a compelling reason to keep Marla Mason’s name on their nightstand.
