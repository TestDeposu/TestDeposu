# Blood of Amber (The Chronicles of Amber, #7)

**Author:** Roger Zelazny  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

Roger Zelazny’s *Blood of Amber*, the seventh installment in the legendary Chronicles of Amber and the second volume of the Merlin cycle, is a dazzling testament to the author’s singular ability to blend hard-boiled cynicism with high-concept metaphysical fantasy. Picking up immediately from the breathless cliffhanger of its predecessor, this novel finds our reluctant protagonist, Merlin—half-Amberite royalty, half-Chaos sorcerer, and full-time techno-wizard—breaking free from a month-long imprisonment inside a hypnotic blue crystal cave. What follows is a dizzying, fast-paced descent into a multidimensional chess match where reality itself is the board, and the pieces possess lethal, unpredictable minds of their own.

At the heart of *Blood of Amber* lies the compelling, resonant theme of identity inherited and weaponized. Merlin is a man caught between the polarizing gravitational pulls of his lineage. As the son of Corwin of Amber and Dara of the Courts of Chaos, he embodies the perpetual friction between order and entropy. Throughout the narrative, Zelazny explores how much of our destiny is written by our parents and how fiercely we must fight to carve out a self that exists independently of that ancestry. Merlin’s internal monologue—brimming with self-deprecating wit, pop-culture-adjacent charm, and academic rigor—serves as his armor against a universe that constantly tries to define him by his bloodline. He wants to be Merle Corey, the grounded computer scientist and witty pragmatist, yet every step he takes pulls him deeper into the violent, operatic politics of his cosmic family.

Zelazny’s authorial style remains the true star of the show. Long before urban fantasy became a crowded marketplace of snarky protagonists, Zelazny was perfecting the voice of the world-weary mage navigating impossible environments. His prose is intensely economical, never wasting a sentence on superfluous world-building, instead dropping the reader directly into the sensory overload of Shadow-shifting and arcane combat. When Merlin journeys to the eerie Keep of Four Worlds to lock horns with the elusive wizard Mask, or when he makes the morally ambiguous choice to kidnap the paralyzed Jasra to gain leverage over his frenemy Luke, the narrative crackles with kinetic energy. Zelazny treats magic not as a set of rigid, Tolkien-esque rules, but as a fluid, dangerous science—part programming language, part sheer willpower. 

What makes *Blood of Amber* particularly striking is its rejection of binary morality. There are no pure heroes here, only survivors operating on shifting alliances and hidden agendas. Luke, once a trusted college friend, is now a vengeful captor. Jasra, a formidable queen, is reduced to a pawn on a terrifying chessboard. Even Merlin’s trusted sentient computer construct, Frakir the garrote, feels more alive and sympathetic than some of the flesh-and-blood entities he encounters. Zelazny masterfully orchestrates a mood of mounting paranoia; in a universe where anyone can be an illusion, a demon, or a disguised relative, the only thing Merlin can truly trust is his own quick wit.

Clocking in as a vital bridge in the larger epic, *Blood of Amber* never suffers from middle-book syndrome. It is compact, punchy, and relentless, pushing Merlin through bizarre dimensions and psychic minefields without ever losing its narrative focus. Zelazny proves once again that he is a master of scale, effortlessly shifting from intimate, sharply-realized character moments to reality-bending spectacles. For fans of inventive fantasy that refuses to take itself too seriously while still exploring profound questions of selfhood and power, this novel is an absolute triumph. It reminds us why Zelazny remains a towering figure in speculative fiction: he could make the impossible feel not only possible, but profoundly human.
