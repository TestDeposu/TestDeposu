# Night's Master (Tales from the Flat Earth #1)

**Author:** Tanith Lee  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

Night’s Master opens the Tales from the Flat Earth series with a lush, almost tactile tapestry of mythic ambition and sensual darkness. Tanith Lee, a World Fantasy Award winner, invites readers into a cosmology divided into four realms—the lofty Upperearth of gods, the shadow‑streaked Underearth of demons, the silent Innerearth of the dead, and the mortal Flat Earth that hangs between them. At the centre of this intricate lattice stands Azhrarn, Prince of Demons, whose capricious whims drive the eighteen interlocking stories that make up the novel. Rather than presenting a linear plot, Lee offers a mosaic of vignettes, each a self‑contained fable that nevertheless echoes the same underlying concerns: the tension between desire and destiny, the mutable nature of power, and the way stories themselves shape reality.

Lee’s prose is unmistakably her own—rich, lyrical, and dripping with an almost decadent sensuality. She favours long, flowing sentences that coil like incense smoke, drawing the reader deeper into the velvet‑soft dread that permeates each tale. Her diction is simultaneously archaic and fresh; she resurrects the cadence of Arabian Nights while infusing it with a modern, psychological edge. This stylistic choice serves a dual purpose: it grounds the narrative in a timeless, oral‑tradition feel, while also allowing her to explore the inner lives of characters who are as much embodiments of archetypes as they are flesh‑and‑blood beings. The result is a reading experience that feels both mythic and intimately personal.

The central theme that threads through Night’s Master is the mutable power of narrative itself. Azhrarn, as Night’s Master, is not merely a demon lord but a keeper of stories; his whims alter the fates of mortals because he can rewrite the tales that define them. Lee suggests that stories are not passive entertainments but active forces capable of constructing and deconstructing worlds. This metafictional awareness aligns the novel with works like Neil Gaiman’s Sandman—indeed, the blurb notes Lee’s influence on Gaiman—but Lee’s approach is darker, more erotic, and less concerned with heroic triumph than with the seductive danger of surrendering to one’s own desires. Her characters often find themselves at the mercy of their longings, and the outcomes are rarely redemptive; instead, they reveal the hollowness that can lie beneath fulfilled wishes.

Lee’s world‑building is exemplary in its economy. She does not bog the reader down with exhaustive geography or genealogies; instead, she lets hints and impressions accumulate. The Upper, Under, Inner, and Flat Earths are felt more than explained, inviting the reader to inhabit the spaces between the lines. This technique mirrors the fragmented, dream‑like quality of the stories themselves, reinforcing the idea that reality in Lee’s universe is as fluid as the narratives that shape it.

What makes Night’s Master particularly striking is how Lee balances the decadent with the disquieting. The sensuality is never gratuitous; it serves to highlight the vulnerability of her protagonists when confronted with forces beyond their comprehension. Demons and gods are not distant, aloof entities; they are intimate, almost familial figures whose affections can turn lethal in an instant. This blurring of the sacred and the profane creates a constant undercurrent of tension that keeps the reader turning pages, eager to see which desire will be fulfilled—and at what cost.

In sum, Night’s Master is a masterclass in dark fantasy that merges lyrical prose with a profound meditation on storytelling’s power. Tanith Lee’s style—lavish, hypnotic, and psychologically incisive—elevates a collection of fairy‑tale‑inspired vignettes into a cohesive, haunting exploration of desire, destiny, and the stories that bind us all. Readers who appreciate mythic richness tinged with erotic peril will find this novel a bewitching gateway into the Flat Earth saga.
