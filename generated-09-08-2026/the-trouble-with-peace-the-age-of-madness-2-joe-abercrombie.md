# The Trouble with Peace (The Age of Madness, #2)

**Author:** Joe Abercrombie  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

Peace is rarely the dawn of a golden age in the grim, unrelenting world of Joe Abercrombie; more often, it is simply the sound of knives being sharpened in the dark. In The Trouble with Peace, the second installment of The Age of Madness trilogy and the direct sequel to A Little Hatred, Abercrombie returns to the blood-soaked, soot-stained canvas of the Great North and the industrializing heart of the Union with a surgeon's cold precision and a cynic's dark wit. If the first book was about the friction of an old world grinding against the machinery of a new one, this middle chapter explores the rot that settles in when the initial shock of revolution gives way to the agonizing, petty realities of governance.

The central theme of the novel is brilliantly encapsulated in its title: peace is just another kind of battlefield. Abercrombie strips away the romanticism often associated with fantasy resolutions. There are no triumphant heroes riding off into the sunset; instead, there are exhausted politicians drowning in debt, glory-hounds desperate to reignite slaughter for the sake of reputation, and revolutionaries lurking in the slums, nursing grievances that industrial progress can never quite heal. The transition from war to peace does not heal the realm; it merely changes the currency of violence from blood to backstabbing, taxation, and ideological sabotage.

Abercrombie’s signature style remains the driving engine of the narrative. He writes with a cinematic, visceral intensity that few contemporary epic fantasy authors can match. His prose is punchy, remarkably rhythmic, and laced with a biting, gallows humor that offsets the relentless bleakness of the plot. But his greatest strength has always been character work, and here he operates at the height of his powers. Figures like the ambitious investor Savine dan Glokta navigate a harrowing psychological and financial ruin, refusing to let moral scruples impede their climb back to power. Meanwhile, doltish heroes like Leo dan Brock and the savage Stour Nightfall treat peace as a personal insult, scheming to return to the battlefield where they feel most alive. King Orso, perhaps one of Abercrombie's most tragically relatable creations, stumbles through a literal maze of political knives, realizing too late that his debts and enemies outnumber his friends. Elsewhere, the young Rikke struggles against the terrifying burden of the Long Eye, a prophetic affliction that threatens to tear her mind apart.

What makes The Trouble with Peace a masterclass in modern dark fantasy is its refusal to offer easy answers. The Breakers linger in the shadows, representing a very real, very chaotic desire to break the shackles of the common man, while the nobility bickers over scraps, entirely oblivious to the rising tide beneath them. Abercrombie demonstrates that systems of power are inherently self-consuming, and that change—whether forced by industry, revolution, or the whims of monarchs—leaves countless broken lives in its wake.

This is a novel about momentum and inevitability. Every character is trapped in the gears of a much larger, grinding machine, and every choice feels heavy with consequence. Abercrombie proves once again why he is a master of his craft, delivering a middle book that suffers from no sophomore slump, but rather accelerates the tragedy toward a breathtakingly catastrophic horizon. For anyone who likes their fantasy gritty, psychologically complex, and utterly devoid of cheap sentimentality, The Trouble with Peace is an essential, gripping read.
