# Lady Carliss and the Waters of Moorue (The Knights of Arrethtrae, #4)

**Author:** Chuck Black  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

**A Knight’s Dilemma: The Weight of Sacrifice in *Lady Carliss and the Waters of Moorue***

Chuck Black’s *Lady Carliss and the Waters of Moorue*—the fourth installment in *The Knights of Arrethtrae*—is a gripping tale where honor, love, and survival collide in a crucible of moral ambiguity. At its heart, this book is a meditation on sacrifice, a theme Black weaves with the precision of a master swordsman. Carliss, the series’ formidable protagonist, is no longer just a warrior; she is a woman torn between duty and devotion, forced to confront the most brutal question of all: *How far will you go to save the ones you love?* The answer lies not in glory, but in the quiet, agonizing moments of choice—where the blade must be drawn, not for victory, but for survival.

Black’s strength lies in his ability to ground high-stakes fantasy in visceral, human stakes. The plot unfurls like a storm: Carliss returns from a mission only to find her world upended by kidnapping, betrayal, and the looming threat of a Shadow Warrior’s machinations. The tension is relentless, but it’s not just the monsters or the political intrigue that keeps readers breathless—it’s the emotional undercurrent. Dalton’s near-death scene is a masterclass in tension, his life hanging by a thread as Carliss races against time. The author doesn’t shy away from the horror of the moment; instead, he amplifies it, making the reader feel the weight of every second lost. When Dalton falls, it’s not just a physical blow—it’s a psychological one, a reminder that even the strongest warriors are vulnerable.

Yet, the true brilliance of *Moorue* is how Black forces Carliss to evolve beyond her own strength. She is a woman who has spent her life proving herself in battle, but now she must face an enemy that cannot be slain with steel: the Shadow Warrior’s plan. Her journey through Moorue’s labyrinthine streets and treacherous swamps is less about combat and more about revelation. The city itself feels alive, a character in its own right, its dangers as much a part of the story as the villains. Black’s world-building here is immersive, painting a vivid picture of a realm where magic and brutality coexist. The swamp, in particular, is a masterstroke—a place of decay and hidden horrors, where survival is as much about instinct as it is about skill.

But it is the moral dilemma at the book’s core that elevates *Moorue* above mere adventure. Carliss is given an impossible choice: save Dalton, or let him die so that others may live. There is no easy answer. Black refuses to offer one. Instead, he lets the reader grapple with the weight of that decision, mirroring Carliss’s own internal struggle. This is not a story about a knight who always wins; it’s about a knight who must *choose* what to lose. The emotional toll of this choice resonates long after the final page, a testament to Black’s ability to craft narratives that linger in the mind.

Style-wise, Black’s prose is lean, efficient, and unapologetically action-driven. He doesn’t waste words on unnecessary flourishes; instead, he trusts the reader to fill in the gaps with their imagination. The dialogue crackles with authenticity, and the pacing is relentless, ensuring that the story never drags. Yet, beneath the surface, there’s a depth of character and theme that gives the book its staying power. Carliss is more than a warrior; she’s a woman navigating love, loss, and the cost of leadership. Dalton, too, is more than a love interest—he’s a reminder of the fragility of life, a catalyst for Carliss’s growth.

*Lady Carliss and the Waters of Moorue* is a book for readers who crave more than just escapism. It’s a story about the price of heroism, the burden of leadership, and the moments when even the strongest among us must decide what they are willing to sacrifice. Black doesn’t offer easy answers, but he does offer something far more valuable: a story that challenges its audience to think, to feel, and to question what it truly means to be a knight—not just in battle, but in life.

For fans of morally complex fantasy, where the real battle is as much internal as it is external, this book is a must-read. It’s a testament to Chuck Black’s skill in blending high-stakes adventure with profound emotional resonance, proving that the greatest battles are often the ones fought within.
