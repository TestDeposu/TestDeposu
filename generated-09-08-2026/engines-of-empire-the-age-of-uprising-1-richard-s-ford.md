# Engines of Empire (The Age of Uprising, #1)

**Author:** Richard S. Ford  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2023

Engines of Empire launches Richard S. Ford’s Age of Uprising trilogy with the kind of kinetic energy that makes you feel the heat of forges and the chill of political intrigue in the same breath. Set in the besieged city‑state of Torwyn, the novel opens with Sanctan Egelrath’s Draconate Ministry tightening its iron fist around the independent guilds that have long powered the region’s ingenuity. From the first page, Ford thrusts us into a world where steam‑driven contraptions hum alongside ancient sorcery, where the clatter of gears is as much a soundtrack to battle as the clash of steel. The premise is instantly recognisable to fans of Brent Weeks’ morally ambiguous power struggles, George R. R. Martin’s sprawling machinations, and David Gemmell’s relentless heroism—yet Ford manages to distil those influences into a voice that feels distinctly his own.

At its core, Engines of Empire is a meditation on the cost of liberation. The guilds, each a micro‑society of craftsmen, engineers, and arcanists, represent more than economic interests; they embody a collective ethos of self‑determination and innovation. When the Draconate Ministry seeks to erase them, the conflict becomes a clash between centralized authoritarianism and a decentralized, merit‑based order. Ford does not shy away from showing the ugliness on both sides: the Ministry’s brutal efficiency is mirrored by the guilds’ own internal feuds, secret pacts, and the temptation to wield forbidden magics for short‑term gain. This moral grayness fuels the novel’s main theme—whether true freedom can ever be seized without becoming the very tyranny one fights against.

The narrative structure amplifies this theme through a rotating cast of viewpoint characters, each offering a distinct lens on the uprising. Rosomon Hawkspur, the beleaguered matriarch of a fallen guild house, embodies stubborn resolve; her sections read like a tragic epic, steeped in loss and the fierce desire to rebuild what was shattered. Lancelin Jagdor, the famed warrior dispatched to forge alliances, provides the classic hero’s journey—yet his chapters are laced with doubt, revealing that even the greatest sword arm can falter when faced with political machinations and personal inadequacy. Tyreta’s return home introduces a fascinating twist: she arrives with newfound sorcerous abilities that are both a blessing and a curse, forcing her to reconcile her identity as a guild heir with the unpredictable power coursing through her veins. Conall’s storyline, set in a hostile foreign land, adds a layer of existential struggle; his battle against internal demons mirrors the external war, suggesting that the revolution’s success hinges not only on overthrowing tyrants but also on healing the fractures within each rebel.

Ford’s prose is lean and propulsive, favoring short, punchy sentences during action scenes and expanding into lyrical, almost cinematic descriptions when he lingers on the steampunk‑infused landscapes of Torwyn. He has a knack for making machinery feel alive—gears whine like wounded beasts, steam hisses like a dragon’s breath, and arcane sigils flicker with an unsettling intelligence. This tactile attention to detail grounds the fantastical elements in a palpable reality, making the reader feel the heat of a furnace and the sting of a blade alike. Dialogue is crisp, often laced with wit and a dry humor that prevents the narrative from becoming overly grim, even as the stakes mount.

What truly distinguishes Ford’s style is his ability to balance epic scope with intimate character moments. While the novel sweeps across battlefields, council chambers, and hidden workshops, it never loses sight of the personal costs: a mother’s fear for her child, a warrior’s guilt over a fallen comrade, a scholar’s awe—and terror—at wielding power they barely understand. These emotional beats are woven seamlessly into the larger political tapestry, ensuring that the reader’s investment remains rooted in human experience rather than abstract world‑building alone.

The pacing is relentless. Chapters end on hooks that compel you to turn the page, yet Ford never sacrifices depth for speed. Even in the midst of a frantic chase or a siege, he finds space for reflection, allowing the themes of rebellion, identity, and the ethics of power to resonate. The climax, while explosive, feels earned—a convergence of each character’s arc that leaves the guilds battered but unbroken, and the Draconate Ministry rattled but not defeated. It sets the stage for the sequel, Engines of Chaos, with tantalizing questions about how far the revolution will go and what new horrors—or miracles—await when magic and machinery intertwine further.

In sum, Engines of Empire is a strong, striking debut for the Age of Uprising series. Richard S. Ford delivers an epic fantasy that feels both familiar and fresh, marrying the grandeur of classic high fantasy with the gritty ingenuity of steampunk. His exploration of revolution—not just as a series of battles, but as a profound transformation of societies and souls—gives the novel a lasting punch that will linger long after the final page is turned. For anyone who craves fast‑paced, morally complex storytelling steeped in richly imagined worlds, this book is an essential read.
