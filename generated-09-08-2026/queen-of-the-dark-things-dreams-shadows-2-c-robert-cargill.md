# Queen of the Dark Things (Dreams & Shadows, #2)

**Author:** C. Robert Cargill  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

**Queen of the Dark Things: Where Shadows Whisper and Deals Are Made in Blood**

C. Robert Cargill’s *Queen of the Dark Things*—the sequel to *Dreams and Shadows*—is a masterclass in urban fantasy’s darker, more philosophical corners. Here, magic isn’t just a tool; it’s a curse, a bargain, and a mirror held up to the human soul. Six months after Colby, a reluctant wizard, banished an army of fairies from Austin, Texas, the city’s peace is a fragile illusion. His victory came at a price: anonymity is gone, enemies resurface, and the line between hero and monster blurs with every shadow that deepens. Now, Colby must confront not just the demons of the Limestone Kingdom, but the demons within himself—because in this world, power is never free, and redemption is a lie told by those who’ve never known true darkness.

At its core, *Queen of the Dark Things* is a story about the cost of defiance. Colby isn’t a chosen one; he’s a man broken by loss, haunted by guilt, and desperate enough to make deals with forces that should terrify him. Cargill, a screenwriter with a keen eye for myth and a knack for psychological depth, crafts a narrative where every choice feels weighty, every revelation cuts like a blade. The novel follows in the footsteps of Lev Grossman’s *The Magicians* and Kim Harrison’s *The Dead Witch Series*, but it carves its own path—a blend of Southern Gothic dread and urban fantasy, where the supernatural isn’t just lurking in the alleys but seeping into the bones of the city itself.

The prose is lush, but never indulgent. Cargill’s writing is precise, each word chosen to heighten tension or unravel a character’s psyche. The world-building is immersive, dripping with lore about the Limestone Kingdom and its inhabitants, but it never feels like an info-dump. Instead, history and myth are woven into the fabric of the story, revealing themselves in fragments—like a fairy’s whisper in the wind or a demon’s bargain carved into a mirror. The result is a world that feels both ancient and freshly breathed into existence, where magic is as much a part of the landscape as the rain-soaked streets of Austin.

Colby is a compelling protagonist, flawed and flawed in the best way. He’s not a hero by choice; he’s a man who stumbled into power and now must live with the consequences. His journey isn’t just about saving the city—it’s about saving himself from the monsters he’s become. The supporting cast is equally compelling, with enemies and allies alike serving as mirrors to Colby’s own contradictions. There’s no easy redemption here, no neat resolution. Cargill understands that the darkest stories are the ones that refuse to sugarcoat the cost of power, and *Queen of the Dark Things* delivers on that promise with brutal honesty.

Themes of sacrifice, betrayal, and the illusory nature of morality run deep. Colby’s deal with the demons isn’t just a plot device; it’s a metaphor for the choices we all make when cornered. Do we cling to morality, even when it’s killing us? Or do we embrace the darkness to survive? Cargill doesn’t offer easy answers. Instead, he invites readers to sit in the discomfort, to question what it means to be human in a world where the line between good and evil is as thin as a blade’s edge.

For fans of urban fantasy that’s more than just action and adventure, *Queen of the Dark Things* is a must-read. It’s a story about the shadows we carry, the deals we make, and the price of defying the inevitable. Cargill’s writing is sharp, his world-building rich, and his characters hauntingly real. This isn’t just a sequel—it’s a descent into the heart of darkness, where the only light comes from the choices we make in the dark.

If you loved *Dreams and Shadows* for its depth and its willingness to explore the darker corners of fantasy, you’ll find even more to love here. *Queen of the Dark Things* is a sequel that stands on its own, a tale of power, guilt, and the monsters we become when we’re forced to choose between hell and damnation.
