# The Stepsister Scheme (Princess, #1)

**Author:** Jim C. Hines  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

The Stepsister Scheme launches Jim C. Hines’s Princess series with a gleeful twist on the classic Cinderella story, turning the oft‑maligned stepsisters into the unlikely heroes of their own destiny. Rather than simply rehashing the familiar tale of a downtrodden maiden rescued by a prince, Hines asks what happens when the women who were cast as villains decide to rewrite the script themselves. The novel’s central theme is agency—how women can seize control of their narratives when the world hands them a role they never asked for. Through a blend of sharp satire and genuine affection for fairy‑tale tropes, Hines explores sisterhood, resentment, and the quiet power that comes from choosing loyalty over envy. The stepsisters, far from being one‑dimensional caricatures, are given distinct voices, motivations, and flaws that make their alliance both believable and strangely inspiring.

Hines’s style is unmistakably his own: brisk, witty, and laced with pop‑culture nods that never feel forced. He balances humor with heart, allowing the comedy to arise from situational absurdity and character quirks rather than cheap jokes. The prose moves at a brisk pace, yet he pauses just long enough to let emotional beats land—whether it’s a moment of vulnerable confession between the stepsisters or a sly jab at the Prince Charming archetype. Readers familiar with Hines’s other works (such as the Goblin Trilogy or the Magic ex Libris series) will recognize his talent for turning genre conventions on their head while still delivering a satisfying, adventure‑filled plot. Here, he uses the familiar scaffolding of a fairy‑tale kingdom to explore modern questions about identity, rivalry, and the ways women can support each other when the odds are stacked against them.

The novel’s world‑building is economical but vivid; Hines sketches a kingdom where magic is real but bureaucracy is maddening, where talking animals have agendas, and where the “happily ever after” is a contract rather than a guarantee. This setting lets the stepsisters’ scheme feel both plausible and delightfully subversive. Their plan—equal parts con, camaraderie, and catharsis—unfolds with a series of clever set‑pieces that keep the reader guessing how far they’ll go to claim their own happy endings. The result is a story that feels simultaneously nostalgic and fresh, a tribute to the fairy‑tale tradition that also dares to ask what happens when the side characters step into the spotlight.

Overall, The Stepsister Scheme is a strong opening to Hines’s Princess line: it’s funny, fast‑moving, and surprisingly thoughtful about the bonds that can form when women refuse to be defined by the stories others write for them. Fans of humorous fantasy, fairy‑tale retellings, and character‑driven plots will find plenty to love—and perhaps a new favorite sister duo to root for.
