# Days of Blood and Fire (Deverry, #7; The Westlands, #3)

**Author:** Katharine Kerr  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1993

**Days of Blood and Fire: A Spark of Adventure in Katharine Kerr’s Enchanted Westlands**

Katharine Kerr’s *Days of Blood and Fire* is a masterclass in weaving mythic grandeur with the raw, unfiltered energy of youth—where a boy’s curiosity becomes the spark that ignites a world on the brink of ruin. This seventh installment in the *Deverry* series (and third in the *Westlands* saga) doesn’t just expand the lore; it breathes new life into it, proving Kerr’s knack for balancing political intrigue, dark fantasy, and the kind of magic that lingers like a whispered secret in the wind.

At its heart, *Days of Blood and Fire* is a story about the cost of innocence. Jahdo, the ratcatcher’s son, is no hero by design—just a boy with sharp eyes and a stubborn streak, thrust into a conspiracy that dwarfs his understanding of the world. His journey from the quiet streets of Rhiddaer to the storm-tossed halls of Deverry mirrors the reader’s own disorientation as the stakes escalate from personal to cosmic. Kerr excels at making the mundane feel charged with possibility: a ratcatcher’s alley becomes a crucible of fate, and a blind bard’s songs carry the weight of prophecies yet unspoken. This is fantasy that doesn’t shy away from the grit of ordinary life—where magic is as much a part of the cobblestones as the shadows.

The real magic of the book, however, lies in its duality. Kerr pits two sorcerers—one human, one elven—against a goddess who has lost her mind, but the true battle is fought in the gray spaces between them. Rhodry Maelwaedd, the berserker bound by fate and blood to both women, is a force of nature: a man who has seen too much, who carries the weight of his past like a second skin. His presence is a reminder that in Kerr’s world, even the most noble causes are tangled in threads of violence and sacrifice. The dragon looming over the story isn’t just a symbol of doom—it’s a living metaphor for the inescapable consequences of power, a creature whose existence hinges on the fragile balance of magic and madness.

Kerr’s prose is deceptively simple, yet it crackles with the rhythm of a well-told tale. She doesn’t indulge in purple descriptions; instead, she trusts the reader to fill in the gaps with their imagination. A line like *“the air smelled of iron and old blood”* doesn’t just set a scene—it immerses you in it, making the world feel tangible. Her dialogue is sharp, her characters flawed in ways that feel earned, and her worldbuilding is so rich that even side characters—like the blind bard, whose songs carry the weight of centuries—leave a lasting impression.

What makes *Days of Blood and Fire* particularly compelling is its accessibility. Kerr doesn’t assume the reader knows the backstory; she weaves it in seamlessly, making it perfect for newcomers while rewarding longtime fans with deeper layers. The book is a bridge between the lighter, more whimsical tones of earlier *Deverry* novels and the darker, more mature themes of the *Westlands* saga. It’s a story about growing up in a world that refuses to stay still, where every choice has repercussions, and where even the smallest act of defiance can ripple into something far greater.

Ultimately, *Days of Blood and Fire* is a testament to Kerr’s ability to craft fantasy that feels both timeless and urgent. It’s a book that doesn’t just tell a story—it makes you *feel* the weight of the world on Jahdo’s shoulders, the tension in the air, the inevitability of the coming storm. For fans of intricate plots, morally complex characters, and a world where magic and politics collide, this is a must-read. And for those who’ve never dipped into the *Westlands*, it’s the perfect entry point—a story where a boy’s curiosity becomes the key to saving a kingdom, and where every page turns with the promise of something darker, something more beautiful, waiting just beyond.
