# The Knight and Knave of Swords (Fafhrd and the Gray Mouser, #7)

**Author:** Fritz Leiber  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

The final installment of Fritz Leiber’s Fafhrd and the Gray Mouser saga, *The Knight and Knave of Swords*, feels less like a tidy conclusion and more like a lingering echo—an invitation to sit by the hearth of Nehwon and listen to the wind carry the last whispers of two legendary antiheroes. After the icy perils of *Swords and Ice Magic*, the barbarian Fafhrd and the wily thief Gray Mouser have retreated to Rime Isle, each trying to stitch a semblance of ordinary life from the frayed threads of their tumultuous pasts. Fafhrd, missing a hand sacrificed to Odin, labors to reclaim his prowess with the bow; Mouser, ever the merchant‑adventurer, sets sail aboard the *Seahawk* in search of profit and perhaps a quiet harbor for his restless soul. Their attempt at domesticity, however, is swiftly undercut by the machinations of a vengeful brother‑sister duo whose stolen treasures lie buried beneath the pirates’ hoard, setting in motion a chain of dreams, curses, and sea‑born terrors that drag the pair back into the very chaos they hoped to leave behind.

At its heart, the novel grapples with the tension between the lure of peace and the inexorable pull of destiny—a theme that Leiber has woven through the series since its inception. Fafhrd and Mouser are not heroes in the classic mold; they are flawed, pragmatic, and often selfish, yet their very imperfections make them profoundly human. Their quest for respectability is constantly undermined by internal doubts and external forces that remind them—and the reader—that in Nehwon, tranquility is a fragile veneer over a world thrumming with magic, greed, and ancient grudges. Leiber uses this backdrop to explore how the past never truly releases its grip; even when the swords are sheathed, the memories of battles, bargains, and betrayals continue to shape choices, driving the duo toward confrontations that feel both inevitable and tragically self‑inflicted.

Stylistically, Leiber remains a master of lyrical, almost baroque prose. His sentences unfurl like the winding alleys of Lankhmar, rich with allusion—Shakespearean cadences, Poe‑like melancholy, Lovecraftian dread—yet never losing the propulsive energy that drives a sword‑and‑sorcery tale forward. He balances high‑flown description with gritty, earthy dialogue, allowing the reader to smell the salt of the Outer Sea, feel the sting of a cursed wind, and hear the clink of coin in Mouser’s purse. The narrative shifts fluidly between the sweeping, mythic scope of Nehwon’s cosmology and the intimate, almost claustrophobic interior lives of its protagonists, a duality that mirrors the book’s central conflict: the struggle to reconcile lofty aspirations with base instincts.

What distinguishes *The Knight and Knave of Swords* from its predecessors is the heightened sense of finality that Leiber imbues in every scene. Dreams become omens; curses take on tangible form; the sea demons that rise from the depths are as much manifestations of the characters’ inner turmoil as they are external threats. The author’s skill lies in making these supernatural elements feel inevitable outcomes of the heroes’ own choices, reinforcing the idea that in Nehwon, sorcery is less a separate force than an extension of human desire and fear.

The novel also serves as a quiet meditation on aging and legacy. Fafhrd’s struggle to regain his archery skill after losing a hand speaks to a broader anxiety about relevance in a world that venerates youth and strength. Mouser’s trading ventures, meanwhile, hint at a yearning for stability that contrasts sharply with his lifelong habit of living by his wits. Their loves—present but never dominant—offer glimpses of tenderness that soften their hardened exteriors, reminding us that even the most cynical wanderers crave connection.

In closing, Leiber’s farewell to Fafhrd and the Gray Mouser is both a celebration of the sword‑and‑sorcery tradition he helped define and a poignant reminder that the greatest adventures are often those we undertake within ourselves. The book’s blend of mythic grandeur, psychological depth, and razor‑sharp wit ensures that it will linger in the reader’s mind long after the final page is turned—a fitting capstone to a series that has, for decades, redefined what it means to wander the lands of Nehwon.
