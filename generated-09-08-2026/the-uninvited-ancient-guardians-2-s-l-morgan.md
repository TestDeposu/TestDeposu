# The Uninvited (Ancient Guardians, #2)

**Author:** S.L. Morgan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

The Uninvited (Ancient Guardians, #2) drops the reader straight into a sun‑splashed, shabby parlour where Miss Zaida Finch darns a pink silk stocking while the household’s restless energy buzzes around her. S.L. Morgan opens with a vivid, almost tactile tableau—faded wallpaper, a shrinking carpet, a crocheted shawl draped over an armchair—that does more than set a scene; it whispers the novel’s central tension between appearances and reality. The room’s “plucky defiance” mirrors its inhabitants: sharp‑tongued Agatha Kent, her devoted brother Howard, and the long‑suffering Finch, whose quiet endurance becomes the moral fulcrum of the story.

Morgan’s prose is deftly economical yet richly period‑appropriate. She balances lyrical description with snappy dialogue, allowing each character’s voice to emerge through idiosyncratic speech patterns—Agatha’s flamboyant, self‑assured repartee contrasted with Finch’s hesitant, earnest murmurs. The excerpt’s exchange over a misleading boarding‑house advertisement showcases Morgan’s talent for turning a mundane inconvenience into a revealing character study. Agatha’s willingness to stretch the truth (“It is not the business of the seller… to call attention to any little defects”) reveals a pragmatic, almost modern entrepreneurial spirit, while Finch’s horrified reaction underscores a more traditional belief that honesty is non‑negotiable, even when it threatens survival.

The main theme that unfolds from this opening is the moral elasticity of deception when motivated by love and duty. Agatha’s startling revelation—that she believes herself the reincarnation of her great‑aunt, thus justifying the ruse she plans to play on a blind boarder—propels the novel into a fascinating exploration of identity, legacy, and the lengths to which we will go to protect those we cherish. Morgan does not glorify deceit; instead, she presents it as a tangled web of good intentions, illustrated through Agatha’s ecstatic yet conflicted vision of providing for Howard’s future while simultaneously exploiting a stranger’s vulnerability. Finch’s anguished protests (“He’s bound to find out sooner or later… no good comes of cheating anybody”) serve as the novel’s conscience, reminding readers that the cost of such schemes often falls on the very people they aim to help.

Stylistically, Morgan’s narrative feels both nostalgic and immediate. Her attention to period detail—horse‑drawn carriages, the social expectations of unmarried women, the genteel desperation of taking in boarders—grounds the story in a specific time, yet the emotional undercurrents resonate universally. The pacing is brisk; the opening scene moves from domestic routine to a burst of scheming in just a few pages, promising a plot that will oscillate between tender familial moments and escalating moral quandaries. The author’s voice is warm but never saccharine, witty without undercutting the gravity of the ethical dilemmas at play.

Overall, The Uninvited invites readers to linger in a world where the line between benevolent illusion and harmful deceit is as thin as the tissue paper covering a mended stocking. Morgan’s clever blend of historical texture, sharp dialogue, and a premise that questions the very nature of self‑sacrifice makes this second installment of the Ancient Guardians series a compelling, thought‑provoking read.
