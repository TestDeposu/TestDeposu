# Once Upon a Time in the North (His Dark Materials, #0.5)

**Author:** Philip Pullman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2023

Once Upon a Time in the North returns readers to the frost‑bitten edges of Philip Pullman’s His Dark Materials universe, offering a compact yet richly textured prelude that explores how two of the series’ most iconic figures—Lee Scoresby, the laconic Texan aeronaut, and Iorek Byrnison, the battle‑scarred armored bear—first forged their unlikely alliance. Though marketed as a gift‑worthy edition graced with Chris Wormell’s full‑color illustrations, the book’s true value lies in the way Pullman distills his signature concerns—freedom, loyalty, and the moral ambiguity of survival—into a tight, adventure‑driven narrative that feels both nostalgic and freshly urgent.

The story opens with Lee winning a hot‑air balloon in a poker game, a whimsical detail that instantly situates him in the archetype of the rogue‑hero: charming, self‑reliant, and perpetually one step ahead of trouble. His dæmon, Hester, a sharp‑tongued rabbit, mirrors his restless spirit, providing both comic relief and a subtle reminder of Pullman’s insistence that inner lives are externalized in his world. When Lee’s balloon drifts northward into a frontier town teetering on the brink of capitalist exploitation, the plot thickens with the introduction of an oil‑magnate schemer, a corrupt mayoral hopeful, and Lee’s longstanding nemesis—a hired killer whose presence raises the stakes from mere mischief to lethal intrigue.

At its core, the novella examines the theme of unexpected solidarity in hostile environments. Lee, accustomed to relying on his wit and marksmanship, finds himself outmatched not only by the firepower of his adversaries but also by the moral quagmire of a town where law is for sale. His salvation arrives in the form of Iorek, a bear whose armor is as much a symbol of his displaced royalty as it is a physical barrier against the world’s cruelties. Their meeting is less a grand, destiny‑filled proclamation than a grudging, pragmatic negotiation: Lee offers Iorek a chance at vengeance and a purpose beyond the solitary exile he endures; Iorek offers Lee brute strength and a ferocity that complements the aeronaut’s precision. Through their uneasy partnership, Pullman underscores a recurring motif in his work—that true freedom is rarely achieved alone, but rather through alliances that honor each participant’s autonomy while weaving a shared purpose.

Pullman’s style here remains recognizably lyrical yet economical. He paints the icy landscape with crisp, sensory detail— the crackle of frost under balloon ropes, the metallic scent of oil, the low growl of a bear’s breath—while allowing dialogue to carry much of the character development. Lee’s speech retains its laconic, frontier wit (“I’m not a hero; I’m just a man who prefers his skies clear and his enemies farther away”), whereas Iorek’s utterances, though few, resonate with a solemn gravity that hints at the bear’s storied past. The narrative pacing mirrors a balloon’s ascent: a gentle lift, a sudden gust of conflict, and a steady, controlled glide toward resolution. Wormell’s illustrations amplify this rhythm, their full‑color plates capturing the stark contrast between the warm, sepia‑toned hues of Lee’s world and the cold, blue‑white bleakness of the Arctic north—a visual metaphor for the internal warmth of companionship against external desolation.

What makes this preview particularly striking is how Pullman uses a seemingly simple adventure to interrogate larger questions that run through His Dark Materials: the cost of progress, the ethics of exploiting natural resources, and the ways in which individuals carve out moral agency within systems designed to corrupt them. Lee’s entanglement with the oil magnate is not merely a plot device; it reflects a timeless tension between entrepreneurial ambition and communal stewardship, a theme that feels especially resonant in today’s climate‑conscious discourse. Simultaneously, Iorek’s plight—stripped of his crown, forced into exile, yet retaining a fierce sense of honor—echoes the series’ broader exploration of lost legitimacy and the struggle to reclaim identity.

In sum, Once Upon a Time in the North succeeds as both an enticing stand‑alone tale and a meaningful addition to Pullman’s canon. It delivers the thrill of a high‑stakes chase, the charm of witty banter, and the depth of a philosophical meditation on companionship and resistance—all wrapped in a beautifully illustrated package that invites readers to linger over each page. For longtime fans, it offers a nostalgic glimpse into the origins of beloved camaraderie; for newcomers, it serves as an accessible gateway into the richly layered world that has captivated readers for decades.
