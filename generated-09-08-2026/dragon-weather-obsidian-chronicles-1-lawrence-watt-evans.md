# Dragon Weather (Obsidian Chronicles, #1)

**Author:** Lawrence Watt-Evans  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

In Dragon Weather, the opening gambit of the Obsidian Chronicles, Lawrence Watt-Evans delivers a masterclass in atmospheric world-building and the timeless architecture of the revenge tragedy. While the premise—a survivor seeking vengeance against the monsters that razed his home—might seem familiar to the seasoned fantasy reader, Watt-Evans elevates the narrative through a meticulous approach to ecology and a lean, purposeful prose style.

The brilliance of the novel lies in its central conceit: the titular "Dragon Weather." By tying the emergence of these apex predators to specific meteorological conditions—sweltering heat and oppressive cloud cover—the author transforms the environment from a mere backdrop into an active antagonist. The weather becomes a ticking clock, a source of psychological dread that permeates every chapter. When the sky turns a certain shade of grey and the air grows heavy, the reader feels the same visceral claustrophobia and panic as the characters.

At its heart, Dragon Weather is a study of the intersection between powerlessness and resolve. We follow Arlian, a protagonist stripped of everything—his family, his home, and eventually his very freedom. By casting Arlian as a slave, Watt-Evans strips away the traditional tropes of the "chosen one" or the privileged warrior. Arlian’s journey is not one of destiny, but of grit. The main theme is the grueling process of self-actualization under pressure; it is about the transformation of grief into a weapon. The question posed by the synopsis—how a mere slave could hope to slay a dragon—is answered not through a magical fluke, but through a slow, methodical accumulation of knowledge and skill.

Watt-Evans’ style is characterized by a refreshing lack of pretension. He avoids the overly floral descriptions that often bog down modern epic fantasy, opting instead for a clear, logical progression of events. His writing is surgical; he provides exactly the amount of detail necessary to make the world feel lived-in without sacrificing the story's momentum. This clarity allows the horror of the dragon attacks to hit harder, as the violence is described with a stark, unsentimental realism.

If there is a critique to be made, it is that the pacing in the middle act occasionally mirrors the slow burn of the plot, but this serves the overarching theme of Arlian’s gradual ascent. The satisfaction of the climax is earned through the patience of the setup.

Dragon Weather is a gripping introduction to a new series that manages to feel both classic and fresh. It is a story of survival against impossible odds, wrapped in a world where the very air you breathe can signal your doom. For those who prefer their fantasy grounded in internal logic and driven by raw human emotion, this is a mandatory read.
