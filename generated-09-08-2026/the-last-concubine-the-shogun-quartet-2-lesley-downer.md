# The Last Concubine (The Shogun Quartet #2)

**Author:** Lesley Downer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2008

The Last Concubine, the second installment in Lesley Downer's The Shogun Quartet, is a mesmerizing tale of love, secrets, and survival set against the backdrop of 19th-century Japan. The story takes place in the women's palace in Edo, where three thousand women reside, and only one man, the young shogun, is permitted to enter. It is here that we meet Sachi, a stunning fifteen-year-old girl chosen to be the shogun's concubine.

Downer's writing is akin to a masterfully woven tapestry, with each thread meticulously intertwined to create a rich and vibrant narrative. Her prose is lyrical, transporting the reader to a bygone era, where the rigid rules of the palace and the strictures of Japanese society are palpable. The author's meticulous research and attention to detail are evident, as she skillfully recreates the opulent world of the palace, with its intricate politics and erotic rivalries.

At the heart of the novel is Sachi, a complex and multifaceted protagonist, whose journey from innocence to self-discovery is both captivating and heartbreaking. As she navigates the treacherous world of the palace, Sachi must confront the harsh realities of her existence and the secrets that surround her past. Her story is one of resilience and determination, as she struggles to assert her own identity in a society that seeks to suppress her.

One of the most striking aspects of Downer's writing is her ability to craft characters that are at once flawed and relatable. Sachi's relationships with the people around her, particularly the rebel warrior who rescues her, are nuanced and multifaceted, adding depth and complexity to the narrative. The author's portrayal of the shogun, too, is noteworthy, as she skillfully captures the intricacies of his character, revealing a man both powerful and vulnerable.

The theme of love is a dominant thread throughout the novel, and Downer explores it with sensitivity and nuance. In a society where private passions are not only discouraged but also unknown, Sachi's feelings for the rebel warrior are a source of both joy and danger. The author's handling of this theme is masterful, as she reveals the ways in which love can both liberate and destroy.

The Last Concubine is also a novel about identity, culture, and the search for truth. Sachi's journey is, in many ways, a metaphor for the search for self, as she seeks to uncover the secrets of her past and understand her place in the world. Downer's exploration of Japanese culture is fascinating, and her portrayal of the country's tumultuous history is both informative and engaging.

In conclusion, The Last Concubine is a captivating and deeply moving novel that will appeal to fans of historical fiction, romance, and adventure. Lesley Downer's writing is exquisite, and her ability to craft a compelling narrative is undeniable. This novel is a must-read for anyone interested in Japanese culture, history, and the human experience.
