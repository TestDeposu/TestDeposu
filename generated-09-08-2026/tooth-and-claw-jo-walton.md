# Tooth and Claw

**Author:** Jo Walton  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2003

Jane Walton’s World Fantasy Award-winning novel Tooth and Claw is a masterclass in subversion, taking the familiar architecture of the Victorian domestic novel and populating it with dragons. At its core, the book is a sharp, unsparing satire of Victorian class rigidity, inheritance laws, and societal hypocrisy. Yet, by stripping away the human facade and rendering her characters as literal predators—creatures defined by the literal and figurative mandate to consume the weak—Walton exposes the brutal Darwinian mechanics underpinning polite society. 

The narrative engine is set in motion by the death of the patriarch, Daigon, which triggers a scramble among his offspring. Herein lies Walton’s most brilliant stylistic stroke: she juxtaposes mundane, highly civilized Victorian preoccupations with the horrific realities of dragon biology. Family members gather not merely to mourn, but to ceremonially consume the deceased in an act of filial piety that grants them physical strength. Society’s elite openly exercise the privilege of killing and eating their weaker kin. Courting couples exchange pleasantries while evaluating each other's tonnage and vigor. Through this grotesque yet utterly logical framing, Walton explores the central theme of systemic consumption. The rich feed on the poor, the powerful crush the marginalized, and tradition justifies it all.

Walton’s style is sly, witty, and deceptively fast-paced. She adopts the tone of Anthony Trollope or Jane Austen, complete with drawing-room politics, churchmen, country houses, and rail travel, only to pivot abruptly to matters of scales, wings, and raw meat. This stylistic dissonance creates a darkly comedic tension that never feels gimmicky. Instead, it functions as a critical lens. When a daughter agonizes over the abolition movement or another sacrifices herself for her husband, the stakes are heightened by the looming threat of physical devastation. The domestic cage is real, and the bars are lined with teeth.

The character arcs—ranging from a son seeking his rightful inheritance through the courts to a daughter experiencing the perilous vulnerability of falling in love—play out with the satisfying precision of classic nineteenth-century drama. Yet, they are constantly underscored by the visceral reality of being red in tooth and claw. Plot strands weave together with delightful triumphs and necessary comeuppances, proving that structural elegance can coexist with savage subject matter.

Tooth and Claw is a rare prize: a fantasy that is simultaneously a biting social critique and an utterly charming period piece. Walton has constructed a fully realized, breathing world where the porcelain teacups rattle against the backdrop of apex predators navigating the constraints of their own polite civilization. It is sharp, brilliant, and entirely unforgettable.
