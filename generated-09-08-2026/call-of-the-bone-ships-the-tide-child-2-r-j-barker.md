# Call of the Bone Ships (The Tide Child #2)

**Author:** R.J. Barker  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

**Call of the Bone Ships: Where Honor Sails on Blood and Betrayal**

R.J. Barker’s *Call of the Bone Ships* isn’t just a sequel—it’s a storm brewing on the horizon of *The Bone Ships*, a trilogy already hailed as one of the most visceral and original works of modern fantasy. If the first book was a blade unsheathed, this is the moment it finds its mark. Barker, a master of gritty, character-driven storytelling, weaves a tale where the sea itself is a character, where loyalty is a currency as fragile as the ships that carry it, and where the return of dragons is less a myth than an inevitability.

At its core, *Call of the Bone Ships* is a story about the cost of survival. The Hundred Isles, a fractured archipelago of warring nations, are on the brink of collapse as the dead rise from the waves and the living are dragged into a war they never asked for. The crew of the *Tide Child*—led by the sharp-tongued, unyielding Shipwife Meas—finds themselves entangled in a conspiracy that threatens to unravel everything they’ve fought for. The discovery of dying slaves aboard a cursed vessel isn’t just a plot device; it’s a turning point, a moment where the line between ally and enemy blurs, and where the crew must confront the brutal truth: in this world, mercy is a luxury, and trust is a liability.

Barker’s strength lies in his ability to make history feel alive. The Hundred Isles are a world where the echoes of the past are never silent—where the bones of ancient wars still whisper from the earth, where the old gods are as much a part of the landscape as the tides. The dragons, when they return, aren’t just monsters; they’re a symptom of a world that has forgotten how to heal. The conflict isn’t just between men and beasts, but between those who cling to the old ways of honor and those who see survival as the only path forward. Meas, in particular, is a protagonist who refuses to be romanticized. She’s a woman who has lost everything—her husband, her ship, her sense of safety—and yet she fights on, not out of idealism, but because she has no choice. Her journey is one of hardening resolve, of learning that in a world where betrayal is the only constant, the only way to endure is to become as ruthless as the sea itself.

Barker’s prose is lean, precise, and dripping with atmosphere. There’s a poetry to his descriptions that doesn’t rely on grand metaphors but on the raw, unfiltered reality of his world. The sea isn’t just a setting; it’s a force of nature, indifferent and merciless. The battles aren’t just fights; they’re survival struggles, where every cut, every misstep, could mean the difference between life and death. The dialogue crackles with the tension of a world on the brink, where every word could be a lie, every promise a trap. And yet, for all its brutality, there’s a deep humanity in Barker’s work. His characters are flawed, desperate, and often broken, but they’re never less than compelling. You root for them not because they’re perfect, but because you understand them.

What makes *Call of the Bone Ships* so gripping is how it balances the epic scale of its world with the intimate, personal stakes of its characters. The war against the dragons is looming, but the real conflict is the one playing out in the hearts and minds of the crew. Meas must decide how far she’s willing to go to protect those she loves. The crew must confront their own demons, their own failures, and their own capacity for cruelty. And the reader is left with the unsettling realization that in this world, there are no easy answers, no clean victories—only the relentless march of history, and the choice of whether to be a part of it or to be swallowed by it.

Barker’s world-building is one of the trilogy’s greatest strengths. The Hundred Isles feel lived-in, their cultures and conflicts as rich and complex as any in modern fantasy. The return of the dragons isn’t just a plot twist; it’s a reflection of the world’s decay, a sign that the old order is collapsing and something new—and far more dangerous—is rising from the ashes. The book doesn’t shy away from the horrors of war, the brutality of slavery, or the moral ambiguities of its characters. It’s a story that demands to be taken seriously, not as escapist fantasy, but as a mirror held up to the darker corners of human nature.

By the time the final pages turn, *Call of the Bone Ships* leaves you breathless. It’s a book that doesn’t just tell a story; it immerses you in it, drags you into the chaos, and refuses to let go until you’re left questioning everything you thought you knew about honor, survival, and the cost of war. If *The Bone Ships* was a ship setting sail into uncharted waters, this is the moment it hits the storm—and there’s no turning back.

For fans of brutal, character-driven fantasy, this is a must-read. For those who love stories where the sea is as much a character as the men who sail it, this is a triumph. And for anyone who believes that fantasy can be both epic and deeply human, *Call of the Bone Ships* is a masterclass in world-building and storytelling.
