# Ex Machina, Vol. 1: The First Hundred Days (Ex Machina, #1)

**Author:** Brian K. Vaughan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

In the landscape of modern graphic novels, the superhero genre often falls into a predictable cycle of grand-scale destruction and moral absolutism. Brian K. Vaughan’s Ex Machina: The First Hundred Days shatters this mold, opting instead for the claustrophobic, high-stakes corridors of City Hall. By pulling his protagonist, Mitchell Hundred, out of the cowl and into the mayor’s office, Vaughan asks a provocative question: What happens to a superhero when the problems of the world can no longer be solved by punching them in the face?

The brilliance of this series lies in its grounded, almost mundane approach to the extraordinary. Hundred, the world’s first and only costumed crime fighter—a man with the uncanny ability to communicate with and command mechanical devices—retires following the tragedy of 9/11 to seek the mayoralty of New York City. The transition is jarringly realistic. We are not watching a god walk among men; we are watching a politician navigate labor strikes, budget cuts, and the polarizing cultural minefield of the Brooklyn Museum of Art. 

Vaughan’s narrative style is remarkably sharp, eschewing the flashy tropes of traditional cape-and-tights fiction in favor of a dense, dialogue-heavy political drama. He masterfully balances two timelines: the present-day headaches of municipal bureaucracy and the fragmented, mystery-shrouded history of how Hundred actually gained his powers. This dual-narrative structure creates a constant sense of unease. As a reader, you are never quite sure if the Great Machine’s past is a source of inspiration or an impending liability for his political career.

The central theme of the work is the inherent friction between power and policy. In the superhero tradition, power is typically exercised through unilateral action—the hero acts, and the problem is resolved. In the world of Ex Machina, power is compromise. Hundred finds that his unique technological abilities are nearly useless when faced with the complexities of public opinion and partisan politics. Vaughan forces the reader to confront the reality that being "super" does not make one inherently "right." Hundred is a deeply human, often flawed protagonist whose desire to fix the city is constantly hampered by the reality that his constituents have their own ideas about how New York should be run.

Visually and narratively, the book feels like a marriage of The West Wing and a speculative noir thriller. It is a slow burn that rewards patience, focusing on the character development of a man who is terrified that the most important day of his life is already behind him. Vaughan’s writing is cynical, witty, and deeply rooted in the gritty, tangible reality of early 2000s New York. 

For those who are tired of formulaic spandex adventures and crave a story that treats the superhero archetype as a springboard for political and social commentary, The First Hundred Days is essential reading. It is a work that acknowledges the danger of heroes—the idea that even the most benevolent figure is, at the end of the day, an unelected authority acting on his own whim. It is a sophisticated, sobering, and ultimately gripping examination of power in the modern age, proving that sometimes the most difficult battles aren’t fought against supervillains, but against the red tape of democracy.
