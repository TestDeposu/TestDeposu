# Burn of the Everflame (Kindred's Curse, #4)

**Author:** Penn Cole  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2024

Burn of the Everflame, the fourth installment of Penn Cole’s Kindred’s Curse series, thrusts readers once more into a world where the divine and the damned are inseparably entwined. The novel opens with Diem Bellator—a sharp‑tongued survivor eking out a grim existence on the fringes of a god‑colonized empire—receiving the cruel news that her mother has vanished without a trace. That loss is not merely a personal wound; it is the key that unlocks the labyrinthine politics of the Descendants, the bitter progeny of the deities who now rule with an iron fist. As Diem is pulled into their shadow‑laden courts, she discovers that the mystery surrounding Auralie’s disappearance is interwoven with a brewing civil war that threatens to shatter the fragile balance between mortal ambition and celestial whim.

Cole’s greatest strength lies in her ability to make the fantastical feel palpably urgent. She renders the empire’s opulent citadels and its fetid underbelly with equal vividness, allowing the reader to smell the incense‑laden halls of power and taste the grit of rebel camps in the same breath. The prose is lean yet lyrical, striking a balance between swift, action‑driven sentences and moments of introspective pause that let Diem’s inner turmoil reverberate. When she wrestles with the seductive pull of forbidden alliances, the narrative slows just enough to let the reader feel the weight of each choice—a technique that amplifies the stakes without sacrificing momentum.

At its heart, Burn of the Everflame explores the theme of inherited legacy versus self‑forged destiny. Diem’s journey is less about defeating an external tyrant than about reconciling the blood‑stained inheritance of her mother’s secrets with her own yearning for autonomy. The civil war that erupts serves as a mirror: both sides claim divine right, yet both are plagued by the same human frailties—ambition, jealousy, and the desperate need for love. Cole deftly uses this conflict to interrogate how power corrupts not only those who wield it but also those who aspire to rise above it. The romance threads, though present, never distract from the central inquiry; instead, they highlight how intimacy can become both a weapon and a sanctuary in a world where loyalty is constantly bartered for survival.

The world‑building feels both expansive and intimate. Cole drops hints of ancient god‑myths through fragmented prayers and ruined relics, letting the reader piece together a cosmology that feels lived‑in rather than lectured. This approach rewards attentive readers while keeping the pacing brisk enough for those who chase the plot’s twists. The Descendants’ court intrigue recalls the machinations of classic political fantasy, yet Cole injects a fresh, almost visceral edge—her deities are not distant, aloof figures but palpable forces whose whims can shift the tide of battle in an instant.

If there is a minor critique, it is that the novel’s rapid shifts between battlefield grit and courtly intrigue occasionally leave secondary characters under‑explored. Allies and antagonists appear with tantalizing promise, yet some fade into the background as Diem’s personal quest accelerates. Nevertheless, this does not diminish the overall impact; the focus on Diem’s perspective intensifies the emotional resonance, making each revelation feel personal and hard‑won.

Burn of the Everflame is a potent blend of savage warfare, delicate intrigue, and a heroine’s relentless pursuit of truth. Penn Cole’s style—sharp, evocative, and unafraid to linger in the moral gray—propels the story forward while inviting readers to ponder the cost of legacy and the possibility of rewriting one’s fate. Fans of the series will find the fourth book a satisfying deepening of the saga, and newcomers will be drawn in by its immersive world and compelling, flawed heroine.
