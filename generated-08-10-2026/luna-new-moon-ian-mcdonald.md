# Luna: New Moon

**Author:** Ian McDonald  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

The Moon wants to kill you. It is a harsh, unforgiving mistress of a world where every breath, drop of water, and calorie of food has a literal price tag. In Ian McDonald’s breathtaking 2015 space opera, *Luna: New Moon*, the lunar surface is not a romantic frontier of intrepid explorers, but a brutal, high-stakes corporate dystopia. McDonald takes the classic tropes of science fiction and infuses them with the gritty, unforgiving sensibilities of modern geopolitics, resulting in a novel that feels less like a distant futuristic fantasy and more like an inevitable tomorrow. 

At the pulsating heart of the narrative is the Corta family, the ambitious upstarts who shattered the established cosmic order. Led by the indomitable matriarch Adriana Corta—a woman who clawed her way to the top by wrestling the vital Helium-3 industry away from her corporate rivals—the family now sits precariously among the reigning Five Dragons. But as Adriana approaches the twilight of her formidable life, the wolves are circling. *Luna: New Moon* is primarily a tale of fierce corporate warfare, toxic familial loyalty, and the terrifying cost of survival in a society stripped of traditional laws, where everything, down to bodily fluids, is commodified. 

McDonald’s masterclass in world-building is the true engine of the novel. The Moon he constructs is richly detailed, culturally vibrant, and utterly believable. Lunar society is governed by a fascinating brand of hyper-capitalist neo-feudalism. Contracts replace constitutions, debt is the ultimate shackle, and the concept of human rights is entirely subordinate to the bottom line. Yet, McDonald avoids dry exposition, instead plunging the reader headfirst into the sensory overload of this low-gravity civilization. The lunar culture is sexually liberated, ethnically diverse, and profoundly cutthroat, presenting a society that has discarded terrestrial morality in favor of pure, unadulterated pragmatism. 

The author’s literary style is kinetic, sensory, and unapologetically complex. McDonald weaves a dense narrative tapestry that shifts perspectives among Adriana’s disparate, fiercely competitive children. Each sibling—tasked with defending their mother’s crumbling empire from external enemies and internal treachery—embodies a different facet of lunar society. The prose is sharp, rhythmic, and dripping with atmosphere, effortlessly blending hard science fiction elements with the intimate, messy emotional violence of a family tearing itself apart from the inside. 

Often dubbed *Game of Thrones* in space, *Luna: New Moon* easily transcends that reductive comparison through its sheer originality and profound engagement with how environments shape human behavior. McDonald does not just write about politics; he writes about survival, legacy, and the heavy burdens of ambition. It is a thrilling, intellectually stimulating, and visually stunning achievement that deserves a permanent place on the shelf of any serious science fiction aficionado.
