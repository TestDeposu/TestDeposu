# Ja, Inkwizytor. Młot na Czarownice (Mordimer Madderdin, #7)

**Author:** Jacek Piekara  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

In the grim, alternate reality conjured by Jacek Piekara, the crucifixion was not the end of suffering, but the beginning of a much harsher reality. In Młot na Czarownice (Hammer of Witches), the seventh installment in the long-running Mordimer Madderdin saga, Piekara continues to explore a world where Christ did not die for humanity’s sins—instead, He descended from the cross in a blood-drenched fury to reclaim the world through fire and steel.

At the center of this theological nightmare stands Mordimer Madderdin, a man who is as much a paradox as the world he inhabits. He is a servant of God, a dedicated inquisitor, and an agent of brutal justice whose methods would make the most seasoned executioners blanch. Piekara’s brilliance lies not in making Mordimer a hero, but in making him a compelling, necessary evil. The protagonist is cynical, observant, and undeniably effective, navigating a society where morality is measured in the heat of a pyre and the sharpness of a torturer’s chisel.

The prose in Młot na Czarownice is lean, muscular, and unapologetically dark. Piekara writes with a certain grit that mirrors the soot-stained streets of his version of the Holy Roman Empire. He eschews the flowery, romanticized notions of medieval fantasy, opting instead for a visceral, grounded atmosphere that feels closer to a noir thriller transposed into a world of religious fervor. The book functions like an episode in a sprawling, prestige-television drama; each chapter is a self-contained investigation of heresy that builds toward a larger, more sinister revelation.

What makes this particular volume stand out is its commitment to the central theme of systemic corruption disguised as divine mandate. Piekara does not shy away from the horrific implications of an Inquisitor’s life. He forces the reader to confront the question: What happens to a person’s soul when they are tasked with "saving" the souls of others through the most depraved acts of physical violence? The title, a nod to the infamous Malleus Maleficarum, sets the stage for a narrative deeply obsessed with the hunt for the occult and the thin line between holy zeal and absolute madness.

The author’s style is sharp, punctuated by Mordimer’s internal monologues, which are often sardonic and deeply cynical. This provides a necessary layer of human detachment, allowing the reader to observe the carnage without necessarily being consumed by it. Piekara’s world-building remains one of the strongest pillars of the series; he manages to make a world defined by its rigid, oppressive dogma feel fluid, unpredictable, and terrifyingly vast.

If you are a reader who prefers your fantasy sanitized and your protagonists morally upright, look elsewhere. Młot na Czarownice is a book for those who appreciate the shadows in the corner of the room. It is a grueling, fast-paced examination of faith, power, and the darkness that exists in the name of the light. Piekara has once again proven that in the service of an angry God, there is no room for mercy, only the cold, sharp truth of the inquisitor’s blade.
