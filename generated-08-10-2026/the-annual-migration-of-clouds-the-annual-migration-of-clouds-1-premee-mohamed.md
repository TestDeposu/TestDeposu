# The Annual Migration of Clouds (The Annual Migration of Clouds, #1)

**Author:** Premee Mohamed  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

The Annual Migration of Clouds opens with a landscape that feels both familiar and alien—a scarred Alberta where the sky is perpetually streaked with the ash of burnt forests and the silence of abandoned towns hangs heavy over cracked earth. Premee Mohamed does not waste a single sentence in establishing this world; instead, she lets the environment breathe through the sensations of her protagonist, Reid. The biting cold of a wind that carries spores, the metallic taste of fear on the tongue, the way light fractures through a haze of fungal haze—all are rendered with a precision that makes the setting feel less like backdrop and more like a character in its own right. Mohamed’s prose is simultaneously lyrical and terse, a style that matches the hopepunk ethos she embraces: even as she stares unflinchingly at the ruin wrought by climate catastrophe, she insists on the possibility of tenderness, resistance, and quiet rebellion.

At the heart of the novella is Reid, a young woman harboring Cad—a mysterious, mind‑altering fungus that seeps into thought and memory, blurring the line between self‑preservation and self‑destruction. The parasite is more than a plot device; it functions as a metaphor for the ways trauma and external pressures can infiltrate our identities, whispering doubts that make us question our own motivations. Reid’s internal struggle is palpable: she has been offered a coveted spot on a dangerous, lucrative mission that could secure her family’s future, yet the very idea of leaving feels like a betrayal of the mother and community that have shaped her. Mohamed deftly captures the tension between personal ambition and collective obligation, framing Reid’s decision not as a simple choice between self‑interest and altruism but as a negotiation of trust—trust in her own compromised mind, trust in the people who rely on her, and trust in a future that may still be worth fighting for.

The theme of community permeates every page. Mohamed shows how, in the wake of industrial collapse, the remnants of society have reorganized around small, interdependent networks where knowledge, labor, and emotional support are shared currencies. Reid’s mother embodies this ethos; her quiet resilience and willingness to shoulder the burdens of others serve as a moral compass for Reid. Yet the novella does not romanticize communal life; it acknowledges the friction, the resentment, and the fear that can arise when resources are scarce and survival hinges on collective compliance. Reid’s hesitation to leave is thus tinged with guilt—not only for abandoning her kin but for potentially exposing them to the risks her mission entails. Mohamed’s insight lies in showing that hope is not a naive optimism but an active, often painful, commitment to stay present and accountable, even when the self is fractured by external forces.

Stylistically, Mohamed’s voice is both intimate and expansive. She moves seamlessly from Reid’s visceral, first‑person sensations to broader reflections on ecological grief and the lingering echoes of a pre‑disaster world. Her sentences often carry a rhythmic cadence that mirrors the ebb and flow of the clouds Reid watches migrate—a metaphor for the transient nature of both opportunity and obligation. The language is spare yet richly textured; adjectives are chosen like brushstrokes, highlighting the contrast between the stark, desolate landscape and the fragile warmth of human connection. Dialogue is sparse but potent, each exchange revealing layers of mistrust, yearning, and the unspoken contracts that bind people together in crisis.

What sets The Annual Migration of Clouds apart in the crowded field of climate‑focused fiction is its refusal to fall into dystopian nihilism or naïve utopianism. Instead, Mohamed crafts a hopepunk narrative that acknowledges the enormity of loss while insisting that agency, compassion, and small acts of defiance can seed renewal. The novella asks readers to consider what we owe those who have lifted us up—whether that debt is repaid by staying, by leaving with the promise of return, or by finding a third way that honors both self and community. Reid’s journey becomes a mirror for anyone navigating the push‑pull between personal dreams and collective responsibility in an age of ecological uncertainty.

In just under two hundred pages, Mohamed delivers a punch‑packed, emotionally resonant story that lingers like the after‑taste of Cad itself—sharp, unsettling, yet oddly nourishing. The Annual Migration of Clouds is a testament to the power of speculative fiction to interrogate the present through the lens of a possible future, reminding us that even when the world is altered beyond recognition, the human capacity to choose, to care, and to hope remains our most enduring adaptation.
