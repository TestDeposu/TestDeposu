# Phylogenesis (Founding of the Commonwealth, #1)

**Author:** Alan Dean Foster  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1999

Long before the vast, bureaucratic, and deeply integrated Humanx Commonwealth became an established galactic superpower, its foundations were not forged in the sterile halls of diplomacy or the fiery crucibles of interstellar war. Instead, as veteran science fiction master Alan Dean Foster demonstrates in his 1999 novel Phylogenesis, the monumental merging of two vastly different species began in the mud, grime, and accidental collisions of ordinary lives. 

Phylogenesis serves as the opening salvo in the Founding of the Commonwealth series, stepping backward in time to explore the tentative, often nauseatingly difficult first contact era between humanity and the Thranx—an intelligent, insectoid race whose outward appearance triggers a visceral, instinctual revulsion in humans, a psychological barrier the two cultures are desperately trying to overcome. Yet, true to Foster’s signature style, the grand tapestry of interstellar sociology is never explored through dry historical exposition. Instead, the author grounds his universe in the chaotic, relatable struggles of low-level outcasts.

At the thematic heart of the novel is the idea that profound historical shifts are rarely orchestrated by the mighty; rather, they are stumbled into by the marginalized. Foster weaves a narrative that examines xenophobia not merely as a political stance, but as a biological hurdle. The initial disgust humans and Thranx feel toward one another is palpable, described with sensory details that emphasize the alienness of chitin versus skin, and compound eyes versus binocular vision. Yet, the central theme of Phylogenesis posits that art, curiosity, and sheer accidental proximity can bridge even the widest evolutionary chasms. 

This thematic weight is carried by an unlikely duo: Desvendapur, a frustrated Thranx poet suffering from a severe creative block, and Cheelo Montoya, a human petty criminal whose ambitions far outstrip his common sense or luck. Desvendapur is seeking inspiration beyond the sterile safety of his familiar urban environment, while Cheelo is simply looking for his next big, easy score. When their paths serendipitously cross, their immediate cultural aversions give way to a reluctant partnership that sets off a chain reaction. Through their eyes, Foster explores how culture is not transmitted downward from leaders, but synthesized sideways through shared experiences, humor, and survival.

Foster’s writing style in Phylogenesis is light, engaging, and deeply character-driven, carrying the whimsical yet thoughtful tone that made his Pip and Flinx series enduring classics. He has a unique knack for planetary world-building, populating his settings with eccentric flora, fauna, and societal quirks that make alien worlds feel lived-in rather than merely invented. While the stakes of the novel are intensely personal rather than galaxy-shattering, the reader is constantly aware of the macro-level implications of the characters' actions. Every misstep Cheelo and Desvendapur make ripples outward, unknowingly laying the groundwork for a utopian alliance that will eventually span the stars.

The pacing is brisk, blending gentle humor with moments of genuine tension as the mismatched pair navigates dangers that neither fully understands. While hard science fiction enthusiasts might wish for deeper dives into technological mechanics, Foster’s focus remains firmly on anthropology and behavioral science wrapped in a cozy, adventurous narrative shell. It is a book that rewards patience with charm, proving that the universe is often shaped not by grand designs, but by the chaotic beauty of coincidence.

Phylogenesis is a delightful, accessible entry point for both longtime fans of the Humanx Commonwealth and newcomers looking for a smart, character-focused first-contact story. Alan Dean Foster reminds us that before empires can rise, individuals must first learn to look past the terrifying, beautiful strangeness of the Other and discover a shared humanity—or thranxity—beneath the skin.
