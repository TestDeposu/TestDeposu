# Chasing the Moon

**Author:** A. Lee Martinez  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

**Chasing the Moon: A Deliciously Dark and Witty Comic Fantasy**

A. Lee Martinez’s *Chasing the Moon* is a comic fantasy that doesn’t just subvert expectations—it devours them. In a world where the apocalypse is as likely to be delivered by a sentient, ravenous monster as it is by cosmic horror, Martinez crafts a story that’s equal parts absurd, heartfelt, and deliciously grotesque. At its core, *Chasing the Moon* is a tale about found family, the absurdity of survival, and the idea that even the most monstrous among us can find redemption—or at least, a shared meal.

The premise is as audacious as it is charming: Diana, a woman drowning in the mundanity of her life, stumbles into an apartment that’s already inhabited by Vom the Hungering, a literal monster whose sole purpose is to consume everything in his path. The twist? Diana is now trapped in the apartment, forbidden from leaving, and must navigate a world where the real threat isn’t just Vom but ancient, moon-devouring entities that could unravel reality itself. The setup is so perfectly ridiculous that it’s hard not to laugh—until the stakes become undeniably grim.

Martinez’s writing style is a masterclass in balancing tone, swinging between dark humor and genuine pathos with effortless precision. The dialogue crackles with wit, whether it’s the banter between Diana and Vom or the eerie, almost poetic musings of the older, more sinister horrors. The humor isn’t just a crutch; it’s a lifeline, keeping the story from descending into pure bleakness. Yet, when the narrative turns serious, it does so with a weight that lingers. The themes of isolation, sacrifice, and the blurred line between predator and prey are explored with a depth that makes the absurdity feel earned.

What sets *Chasing the Moon* apart is its willingness to embrace the grotesque without apology. Vom isn’t just a monster—he’s a tragic figure, a being who has been shaped by his hunger, his loneliness, and his own misunderstood nature. Martinez doesn’t shy away from the horror of his existence, but she also doesn’t let him remain a one-dimensional villain. Instead, he becomes a catalyst for Diana’s growth, forcing her to confront her own flaws and the ways she’s been complicit in her own misery. The dynamic between them is one of the book’s greatest strengths, a push-and-pull of mutual dependence that feels both fresh and deeply human.

The world-building, while not as expansive as some epic fantasies, is rich in atmosphere. The apartment itself becomes a character, a claustrophobic yet strangely intimate space where the boundaries between human and monster blur. The cosmic threats looming in the background add a layer of existential dread, but Martinez grounds them in the mundane struggles of her characters. It’s a reminder that even in the face of annihilation, the smallest acts of defiance—like sharing a meal, making a promise, or simply choosing to stay—can mean everything.

One of the most compelling aspects of *Chasing the Moon* is its subversion of traditional fantasy tropes. Instead of a chosen one or a noble hero, Diana is an everyman (or everywoman) thrust into extraordinary circumstances. Her journey isn’t about becoming a warrior or a savior; it’s about learning to coexist with the monsters she’s been taught to fear. The story’s message—that redemption and survival often require embracing the darkness within us—is both radical and profoundly moving.

A. Lee Martinez’s follow-up to *Divine Misfortune* proves she’s a writer who thrives in the liminal spaces between comedy and tragedy, between the mundane and the mythic. *Chasing the Moon* is a book that refuses to be pigeonholed, a story that will leave you laughing one moment and breathless the next. It’s a testament to the power of dark humor, the beauty of flawed characters, and the idea that even in the face of apocalypse, there’s room for hope—if you’re willing to share a table with the monsters.

In the end, *Chasing the Moon* is more than just a comic fantasy; it’s a celebration of the absurd, a meditation on survival, and a reminder that sometimes, the things that try to devour us are the very things that save us.
