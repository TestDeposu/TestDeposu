# Dead Soul (In Her Name: The Last War, #3)

**Author:** Michael R. Hicks  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

When Michael R. Hicks launched his sweeping *In Her Name* saga, he established a universe where humanity’s survival hangs by a thread against the relentless, religiously fervent might of the Kreelan Empire. With *Dead Soul*, the third installment in the *The Last War* sub-series, Hicks zooms in from the macro-level geopolitics of interstellar conflict to focus on a searing, intimate tragedy of survival, resilience, and unexpected connection. 

At its core, *Dead Soul* explores the profound theme of the dehumanizing nature of total war contrasted against the stubborn flicker of the human spirit. The narrative kicks off with brutal efficiency: a young girl named Allison Murtaugh is celebrating what should have been the best birthday of her life, only to watch her world burn as Kreelan warships descend. In a flash, her family, her friends, and even her beloved horse are stolen from her by an invading force that views humanity as little more than vermin or sport. 

Yet, it is what happens after the smoke clears that defines the novel's thematic depth. Allison’s journey crosses paths with Ku'ar-Marekh—a high priestess feared among her own merciless kind as "Dead Soul." Where one might expect a straightforward tale of victim and monster, Hicks crafts a chillingly nuanced dynamic. To the battle-hardened, cold-hearted priestess, Allison is merely a fascinating curiosity, a fragile spark in a galaxy of ash. Yet, this very curiosity poses a psychological threat to a warrior caste built entirely on subjugation. While Ku'ar-Marekh orchestrates gladiatorial spectacles condemning tens of thousands of human survivors to fight to the death in brutal arenas, her strange fascination with Allison hints at a fracture in the Kreelan dogma. 

Hicks’s authorial style shines in his ability to balance sweeping, cinematic military science fiction with agonizingly personal stakes. He does not shy away from the grim realities of planetary invasion; the devastation of Allison’s town is rendered with visceral clarity. At the same time, the narrative maintains the breathless pacing characteristic of elite space opera. As Allison navigates her nightmare, the broader canvas of the universe continues to churn. Readers of the earlier volumes will relish the looming presence of familiar heroes—Ichiro Sato, Valentina, Roland Mills, and James Sparks—charging toward the theater of war. The tension builds relentlessly: will the cavalry arrive in time, or will the arena claim yet another generation of human souls?

What makes *Dead Soul* a compelling read is how it weaves elements of traditional space opera with a dash of dark fantasy and psychological grit. Hicks examines how empathy can bloom in the unlikeliest of soil, even within the heart of a merciless inquisitor. It is a story about the cost of defiance and the unbearable weight of survival when everything you love has been reduced to rubble. 

For aficionados of military sci-fi, first-contact epics, and high-stakes alien invasions, *Dead Soul* delivers a masterclass in tension and world-building. Hicks proves that the most devastating battles are not just fought with starships and plasma rifles, but within the hearts of those who survive the slaughter.
