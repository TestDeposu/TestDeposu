# The Graveyard Game (The Company, #4)

**Author:** Kage Baker  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2026

**The Graveyard Game: A Masterclass in Time, Betrayal, and the Cost of Immortality**

Kage Baker’s *The Graveyard Game*—the fourth installment in *The Company* series—is a book that doesn’t just bend time; it shatters the illusion of control. In a world where immortality is a curse as much as a gift, Facilitator Joseph and Literature Specialist Lewis embark on a desperate hunt for the truth behind Mendoza’s disappearance, only to stumble into a conspiracy so vast it rewrites history itself. Baker, ever the architect of morally complex narratives, weaves together humor, horror, and heartbreak into a tapestry that feels both intimate and epic. This isn’t just another time-travel thriller; it’s a meditation on memory, sacrifice, and the terrifying weight of knowing too much.

At its core, *The Graveyard Game* is a story about the fractures in immortality. The Company’s agents live forever, but their humanity is a fragile thing—eroded by duty, love, and the slow realization that even gods have limits. Joseph’s grief over Mendoza isn’t just personal; it’s a microcosm of the series’ central tension: the more you cling to the past, the more it unravels. Lewis, with his sharp wit and quiet rage, serves as the perfect foil to Joseph’s brooding introspection. Their dynamic is electric, a collision of loyalty and betrayal that keeps the pages turning. Baker excels at making even the most cynical characters feel deeply human, their flaws not just relatable but *necessary* to the story’s dark revelations.

The real genius of *The Graveyard Game* lies in its structure. Baker doesn’t just send her characters through time—she forces them to confront the *cost* of it. Each leap isn’t just a journey; it’s a psychological unraveling. The deeper Joseph and Lewis dig, the more they realize the Company’s history is a lie, a carefully constructed myth to keep its agents obedient. The "Silence," that ominous event after which no agent can travel, isn’t just a plot device—it’s a metaphor for the silence we all impose on ourselves to survive. What happened to Mendoza isn’t just a mystery; it’s a symptom of a system that thrives on erasure.

Baker’s prose is as precise as it is provocative. She balances the absurdity of immortality with the grim reality of its consequences, making the series’ signature humor never feel out of place. Whether she’s crafting a darkly comic exchange between Lewis and Joseph or delivering a gut-punch revelation about the Company’s origins, her writing is razor-sharp. The world-building is immersive, with each era—from the 19th century to the distant future—feeling distinct yet interconnected. The conspiracy unfolds like a puzzle, with each clue revealing another layer of deception, and Baker’s pacing ensures that the payoff is as satisfying as it is unsettling.

But *The Graveyard Game* isn’t just about answers. It’s about the questions that haunt them. What does it mean to love someone you can never truly lose? How much of your past are you willing to betray to protect your future? And most chillingly, what happens when the truth you seek is the very thing that destroys you? Baker doesn’t offer easy resolutions. Instead, she leaves her readers with the same unease that lingers after a particularly good horror film—the kind of story that stays with you long after the last page.

For fans of *The Company*, this book is a triumph, deepening the lore while staying true to the series’ themes of loyalty, sacrifice, and the inescapable weight of immortality. For newcomers, it’s a masterclass in world-building and narrative tension, proving that time travel doesn’t have to be about flashy gadgets or clichéd romances—it can be about the quiet, devastating truth of what it means to live forever in a world that’s always changing.

If *The Company* has been a slow-burn masterpiece, *The Graveyard Game* is the explosive climax it deserves. Baker doesn’t just write about the past; she makes it feel alive, painful, and impossible to ignore. And in a genre often dominated by spectacle, that’s the most terrifying thing of all.
