# Deathwish (Cal Leandros, #4)

**Author:** Rob Thurman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Rob Thurman’s Deathwish, the explosive fourth installment in the acclaimed Cal Leandros series, plunges readers back into the neon-slicked, monster-ridden underbelly of a nightmarish New York City. True to form, Thurman delivers a breathless, adrenaline-fueled masterclass in urban fantasy, combining visceral action, relentless pacing, and a uniquely gritty aesthetic. But beneath the slick wisecracks and the spilled arterial blood lies a deeper, darker exploration of the book's central theme: the inescapable weight of lineage and the desperate struggle to carve out a chosen humanity in a world designed to strip it away.

The narrative kicks into gear when a desperate vampire named Seamus hires the preternatural investigative agency run by half-human Cal Leandros and his fiercely protective brother, Niko. Seamus wants to know who is hunting him, but the job swiftly spirals into a lethal disaster when the client ends up permanently dead. Yet, this bloody gig is merely the opening salvo in a much larger war. The truly terrifying hook of Deathwish is the terrifying return of the Auphe—Cal’s pure-monster relatives. The last encounter nearly obliterated the brothers, and now, the Auphe are back not just for conquest, but for exquisite, personalized vengeance. For these sadistic fiends, Cal’s agony is the ultimate high, and they are planning to indulge.

Thurman’s signature style is the true engine driving this dark carnival. Written in Cal’s distinctively cynical, rapid-fire first-person perspective, the prose crackles with gallows humor, pop-culture-infused snark, and a raw vulnerability that prevents the action from feeling hollow. Thurman does not write polite fantasy. The New York City of Deathwish is a sensory assault of rotting neon, rain-slicked asphalt, and ancient, tooth-and-claw horrors hiding in plain sight. The action sequences are choreographed with kinetic fury, cinematic in their scope and brutal in their execution. Yet, what elevates Thurman above the crowded urban fantasy genre is his unflinching focus on the bond between Cal and Niko. Their fierce brotherhood serves as the emotional anchor of the novel, contrasting sharply with the monstrous cruelty of the Auphe family tree.

Deathwish operates on the terrifying premise that you can never truly outrun your bloodline. Cal is half-monster, constantly fighting the urges of the Auphe biology humming beneath his skin, desperately choosing light and family over the inherited darkness. Thurman weaves this internal psychological horror seamlessly with the external stakes. As the Auphe begin circling closer, targeting everything and everyone the brothers hold dear, the suspense ratchets up to a fever pitch. Every ally feels vulnerable, every safehouse feels like a trap, and the margin for error shrinks to zero. 

For returning fans of the series, Deathwish delivers everything that makes the Leandros brothers so endlessly compelling: high-stakes peril, razor-sharp banter, and a relentless onslaught of supernatural threats. For newcomers, it serves as a thrilling, baptism-by-fire introduction to one of the most wildly inventive and aggressively entertaining worlds in modern dark fantasy. Thurman refuses to pull his punches, crafting a relentless page-turner that leaves you bruised, breathless, and eager for the next round.
