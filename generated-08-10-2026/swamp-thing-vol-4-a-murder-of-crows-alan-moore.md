# Swamp Thing, Vol. 4: A Murder of Crows

**Author:** Alan Moore  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2025

Swamp Thing, Vol. 4: A Murder of Crows finds Alan Moore once again steering the murky waters of his most iconic creation into unsettling new terrain. Though the volume borrows its title from the classic omen of a gathering of corvids, the story is less about avian omens and more about the ways in which nature, myth, and the human psyche intertwine when the swamp itself becomes a character with its own memory and grudge.

At its heart, the book grapples with the theme of ecological vengeance—a motif that has threaded through Moore’s work since the early days of Saga of the Swamp Thing. Here, the swamp is not merely a backdrop; it is an ancient, sentient network of roots, spores, and whispered histories that reacts to the encroaching violence of corporate exploitation and the zealous fervor of a modern witch‑hunter cult. The titular “murder of crows” operates as both a literal harbinger of death and a metaphor for the collective guilt that haunts those who disturb the balance. Moore uses this symbol to ask a quiet, persistent question: what do we owe to the places that have nurtured us, and what price do we pay when we forget that debt?

Moore’s style remains unmistakably dense, lyrical, and unafraid of contradiction. He layers dialogue with archaic phrasing, then cuts it with stark, contemporary speech, creating a rhythm that feels like a chant drifting over stagnant water. His narration swings between the omniscient, almost mythic voice of the swamp itself and the intimate, fractured inner monologues of Figgy and her mother Blu—two women whose lineage is steeped in folk magic and whose intuition serves as both compass and curse. This polyphonic approach allows Moore to explore the theme from multiple angles: the visceral terror of being hunted, the quiet strength of maternal legacy, and the creeping dread of an ecosystem fighting back.

Visually, the art team matches Moore’s verbal richness with panels that drift from lush, almost painterly depictions of cypress knees draped in Spanish moss to jagged, nightmare‑splashed sequences where the hunters’ rites collide with the swamp’s retaliatory wrath. The use of color is particularly noteworthy: muted greens and browns dominate the quiet moments, while sudden bursts of sickly yellow and deep violet signal the emergence of supernatural force, making the reader feel the shift in atmosphere before a single word is spoken.

What elevates A Murder of Crows beyond a standard horror crossover is Moore’s insistence that the true monster is not the witch hunter or the corporate mogul, but the collective amnesia that allows humanity to treat the natural world as a resource to be consumed rather than a kinship to be honored. By binding Figgy’s personal journey to the swamp’s ancient memory, he creates a narrative that feels both intensely personal and mythically expansive—an allegory that resonates as strongly in today’s climate‑crisis conversations as it does in the folklore‑rich corners of the Louisiana bayou.

In short, this volume is a masterclass in weaving ecological dread with intimate, character‑driven storytelling. Moore’s prose remains incantatory, his symbolism layered, and his respect for the source material evident, yet he pushes the Swamp Thing mythos into fresh, unsettling territory that will linger long after the final page is turned.
