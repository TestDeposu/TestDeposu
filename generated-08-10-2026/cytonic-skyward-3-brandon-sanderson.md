# Cytonic (Skyward, #3)

**Author:** Brandon Sanderson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Stepping into the vast, unpredictable expanse of the cosmos, Brandon Sanderson returns with Cytonic, the thrilling third installment in his acclaimed Skyward series. Following the explosive revelations of Starsight, this novel pivots away from the claustrophobic dogfights of the planetary enclave of Detritus and launches headfirst into the surreal, mind-bending depths of the Nowhere. True to Sanderson’s signature form, the book expands the scope of its universe exponentially, trading traditional space opera mechanics for a deeply metaphysical journey of self-discovery.

At the heart of Cytonic lies a compelling and resonant theme: the exploration of identity and what it truly means to be human in a universe governed by fear and conformity. Spensa Nightshade has always defined herself through combat, through her ambition to be the greatest pilot, and through her fierce protection of her people. Yet, as she grapples with her rare cytonic abilities—powers that connect her to the terrifying, galaxy-wiping entities known as Delvers—she is forced to strip away the armor of her achievements. Entering the Nowhere, a dimension defying the laws of physics and linear time, Spensa is stripped of her ship and her familiar weapons. Sanderson brilliantly uses this otherworldly realm as a psychological mirror. To survive the Nowhere, Spensa cannot simply outfly her enemies; she must confront the fragments of her own psyche, her doubts, and the inherent dread of the unknown. 

Sanderson’s authorial style shines here through his masterclass in world-building and relentless pacing. While the first two books leaned heavily into military sci-fi tropes, Cytonic shifts gears into a bizarre, episodic Odyssey-esque adventure. The Nowhere is a conceptual playground, and Sanderson relishes the opportunity to invent landscapes that logic forgets, populated by eccentric characters and shifting realities. Yet, even as the narrative dips into surrealism, it never loses its emotional anchor. Sanderson grounds the cosmic weirdness in Spensa’s distinct, often humorous, and fiercely loyal voice. Her internal monologues, peppered with stubborn optimism and vulnerability, remind readers why she is one of the most endearing protagonists in modern speculative fiction.

Admittedly, by pulling Spensa away from the ensemble cast of Skyward Flight, the novel functions almost as a side-quest in the grander war against the Superiority. Some readers may miss the tight-knit camaraderie of the pilot squad that defined the early chapters of this journey. However, this narrative isolation is a deliberate and rewarding creative choice. By isolating Spensa, Sanderson allows her to evolve from a reactionary fighter into a mature, self-aware figure capable of understanding the delicate balance between the universe's disparate forces.

Cytonic is a bridge, a deeply philosophical and wildly imaginative pitstop on the road to the series' ultimate climax. It challenges its protagonist—and its readers—to look past the horizon of fear and redefine their own limitations. Sanderson weaves humor, high-stakes danger, and existential wonder into a seamless narrative that keeps the pages turning at warp speed. For fans of character-driven sci-fi and boundless cosmic invention, this flight into the unknown is more than worth taking.
