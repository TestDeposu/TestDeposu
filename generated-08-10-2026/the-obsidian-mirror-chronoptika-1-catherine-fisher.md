# The Obsidian Mirror (Chronoptika, #1)

**Author:** Catherine Fisher  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Time is not a straight line in Catherine Fisher’s universe; it is a fragile, jagged thing, easily fractured and dangerously easily rewritten. In *The Obsidian Mirror*, the opening installment of the *Chronoptika* series, the *New York Times* bestselling author of *Incarceron* returns with a chilling, atmospheric speculative thriller that blends quantum mechanics with Gothic dread. Fisher has always possessed a rare talent for constructing claustrophobic, richly textured worlds, and here she turns her keen eye toward the ultimate obsession: the manipulation of time itself.

At the heart of the narrative lies the obsidian mirror—an ancient, light-swallowing artifact of great and terrible power. It is not merely a window into the past or the future, but a fulcrum upon which reality can be violently pivoted. Men have been lost within its dark depths, the dead potentially resurrected, and entire futures annihilated. Such an object naturally attracts desperate players, and Fisher sets up a high-stakes convergence of motives that drives the plot forward with relentless momentum. Three distinct seekers converge on the mirror, each bound by incompatible desires. There is a mysterious traveler sent from a ruined future with the singular directive to shatter the artifact and prevent apocalyptic doom; a dangerous zealot obsessed with harnessing its godlike energy at any cost; and a grieving youth determined to use the mirror to locate his missing father and undo a murder. 

Fisher’s narrative style is one of her greatest strengths. Eschewing the breezy, simplistic prose found in much of contemporary young adult sci-fi, she adopts a moody, intellectual, and sharply atmospheric tone. The pacing is deliberate, cultivating an escalating sense of unease. Winter Wharf, the remote, decaying estate where much of the early mystery unfolds, acts almost as a character itself, dripping with Victorian gloom that seamlessly juxtaposes the sleek, terrifying mechanics of time travel. The author trusts her readers, dropping them into a complex web of temporal paradoxes, shifting loyalties, and ethical quandaries without offering easy hand-holding. 

The central theme of the novel examines the heavy burden of consequence and the human refusal to accept finality. Grief is weaponized in *The Obsidian Mirror*. The characters are not driven by abstract notions of heroism or villainy, but by raw, messy desperation—the inability to let go of the dead, the terror of a predetermined future, and the arrogant belief that humanity can tinker with the fabric of the universe without paying a devastating price. Fisher interrogates the morality of second chances, ultimately asking whether a life—or a timeline—restored through manipulation is worth the collateral damage it creates.

Fans of intelligent speculative fiction, intricate world-building, and genre-bending narratives will find much to admire here. Comparisons to *Doctor Who* and the dark urban fantasy of Cassandra Clare are well-earned, yet Fisher’s distinctively British literary sensibility and penchant for philosophical darkness give the book a flavor entirely its own. *The Obsidian Mirror* is a gripping, cerebral start to a trilogy that demands your full attention, rewarding the reader with a labyrinthine plot where every choice echoes across the centuries.
