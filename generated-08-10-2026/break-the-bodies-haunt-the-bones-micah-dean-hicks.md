# Break The Bodies, Haunt The Bones

**Author:** Micah Dean Hicks  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

"Break The Bodies, Haunt The Bones" is a fever‑dream of a debut that threads the grotesque intimacy of Southern gothic with the relentless grind of industrial decay. Micah Dean Hicks crafts Swine Hill as a character in its own right—a town where the dead outnumber the living, where each specter clings to the places that once held hope and now feeds on the living’s secrets, fears, and labor. The novel’s engine is the uneasy symbiosis between the haunted and the haunting: Jane carries a ghost girl who voraciously consumes the unspoken thoughts of those around her; her brother Henry is driven by a genius spirit that compels him to build bizarre, perilous machines; their mother burns anyone she touches, possessed by a lonely wraith. These possessions are not merely gothic flourishes; they function as visceral metaphors for the ways economic desperation and social alienation infiltrate the psyche, turning personal trauma into communal contagion.

Hicks’ prose is lush yet restrained, each sentence weighted with atmospheric detail that makes the rot of Swine Hill palpable. He leans into sensory overload—the smell of rusted machinery, the chill of a ghost’s breath, the hiss of steam escaping a malfunctioning contraption—to immerse the reader in a world where the line between body and spirit is constantly redrawn. The narrative unfolds in a tight, almost claustrophobic pace, mirroring the protagonists’ frantic attempts to outrun both the literal and metaphorical ghosts that pursue them. This urgency amplifies the novel’s central theme: the exploitation of labor and the haunting legacy of abandoned industry. When new arrivals seize the scarce jobs at the town’s last factory, the living and the dead erupt in violent protest, exposing how capitalism can resurrect old wounds and summon new specters of rage.

What distinguishes Hicks from other practitioners of horror‑infused social critique is his refusal to let the supernatural serve as mere window dressing. The ghosts are embodiments of collective memory—of lost dreams, of generational trauma, of the quiet desperation that settles in a town when its economic lifeblood is drained. By granting them agency and voice, Hicks forces the reader to confront how societal neglect manifests not just in empty storefronts but in the inner lives of those who remain. Jane’s struggle to protect her family becomes a battle against an ecosystem where the past is never truly buried; it lingers, whispering, shaping, and sometimes devouring the present.

Stylistically, Hicks balances lyrical description with stark, visceral action. His dialogue is terse, often laced with the eerie certainty of those who have learned to speak to the unseen. The novel’s structure—alternating between intimate interior moments and the broader, chaotic uprisings of Swine Hill—creates a rhythm that feels like a heartbeat slowing and then racing under pressure. This duality mirrors the book’s core tension: the personal versus the systemic, the intimate hauntings versus the town‑wide conflagration.

Ultimately, "Break The Bodies, Haunt The Bones" reads as a grim fairy tale for the post‑industrial age—a warning that when a community’s soul is siphoned away by neglect and exploitation, the ghosts that rise are not merely phantasms of the dead but the very manifestations of our unresolved collective sins. Hicks delivers a debut that is as unsettling as it is insightful, haunting the reader long after the final page is turned.
