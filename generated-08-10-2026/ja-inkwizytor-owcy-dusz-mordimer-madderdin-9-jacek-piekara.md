# Ja, Inkwizytor. Łowcy Dusz (Mordimer Madderdin, #9)

**Author:** Jacek Piekara  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

**A Dark and Theological Masterpiece: *Ja, Inkwizytor. Łowcy Dusz* by Jacek Piekara**

Jacek Piekara’s *Ja, Inkwizytor. Łowcy Dusz* (Mordimer Madderdin #9) is a work of dark theological fiction that reimagines the Christian faith through the lens of a brutal, unflinching vision of divine retribution. Set in a world where Christ’s wrath has descended upon the earth—where prayer invokes unforgiving vengeance and the Apostles have slaughtered half of Jerusalem—this novel is less a historical fantasy and more a grotesque meditation on faith, power, and the abyss that lies beneath piety.

At its core, *Łowcy Dusz* is a story about the cost of absolute devotion. Mordimer Madderdin, a licensed inquisitor and "Servant of God," is a man of unshakable faith—until he is thrust into a world where that faith is weaponized. The novel’s opening lines establish its tone: this is not a story of mercy, but of divine fury. The Church has become a machine of purification, and Madderdin, as an instrument of its will, must navigate a landscape where heresy is not just a sin but a capital offense. His journey takes him from the political intrigues of the imperial capital to the shadowy rituals of a heretical nobleman, and finally to the enigmatic monastery of Amszilas, where something far worse than heresy lurks—something that may redefine the very nature of salvation.

Piekara’s prose is sharp, visceral, and unapologetically bleak. He writes with the precision of a theologian and the brutality of a storyteller who understands that faith, when pushed to its extremes, becomes a force of destruction. The world he constructs is one where religion is not a comfort but a sword, where the line between saint and monster is paper-thin. Madderdin’s internal conflict—between his duty as an inquisitor and his growing skepticism—is rendered with psychological depth, making him a compelling protagonist in a genre often dominated by one-dimensional zealots.

What makes *Łowcy Dusz* particularly striking is its exploration of the darker implications of Christian doctrine. The novel doesn’t shy away from the violent, vengeful aspects of faith, presenting them not as aberrations but as logical consequences of a world where God’s will is absolute. The mention of vamps (vampires) in the synopsis is telling—these are not the romantic undead of Gothic fiction but creatures of divine punishment, another layer of the novel’s obsession with retribution. The monastery of Amszilas, with its hidden secrets, becomes a symbol of the Church’s own hidden horrors, a place where truth is as dangerous as falsehood.

Piekara’s style is both lyrical and brutal, blending theological weight with narrative tension. His descriptions of religious rituals and inquisitorial proceedings are chillingly detailed, immersing the reader in a world where faith is not a private matter but a public spectacle of power. The political intrigue that pulls Madderdin into the imperial court adds another layer of complexity, showing how religion and state collide in a dance of mutual reinforcement.

Ultimately, *Ja, Inkwizytor. Łowcy Dusz* is a novel that challenges the reader to confront uncomfortable questions about faith, authority, and the lengths to which belief can be taken. It is not a story for the faint of heart—it is dark, unsettling, and deeply theological—but it is also a masterful exploration of the abyss that lies beneath the surface of organized religion. Piekara’s work stands as a testament to the power of fiction to expose the darker corners of belief, making it a must-read for fans of dark fantasy, theological fiction, and stories that dare to question the very foundations of faith.
