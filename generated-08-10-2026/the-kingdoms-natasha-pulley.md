# The Kingdoms

**Author:** Natasha Pulley  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

In The Kingdoms, Natasha Pulley does something remarkably ambitious: she weaves a tapestry of alternative history so dense and dizzying that the reader feels as untethered from reality as her protagonist, Joe Tournier. If you are looking for a straightforward historical romance or a simple period drama, you will find yourself pleasantly ambushed. Pulley has crafted a genre-defying odyssey that functions less like a linear narrative and more like a knot that tightens the more you try to unravel it.

The premise alone is a masterclass in atmospheric hook-building. Joe Tournier wakes up in a nineteenth-century London that is not merely under French occupation but is fundamentally, culturally French. England is a colony, the language of the streets is French, and the very concept of an independent Britain feels like a fading ghost story. When Joe receives an anonymous postcard from a Scottish lighthouse, he is thrust into a hunt for his own forgotten past. But as Pulley expertly peels back the layers of Joe’s life, it becomes clear that his amnesia is not just a medical mystery—it is a temporal one.

Pulley’s prose is her greatest weapon. She possesses a rare ability to describe the physical sensation of time passing—not as a steady march, but as a treacherous, surging tide. Her writing is cerebral yet visceral, full of salt-sprayed decks, the claustrophobic tension of colonial surveillance, and the agonizing ache of a love that transcends the constraints of the calendar. The relationship between Joe and the enigmatic Captain Kite is the emotional anchor of the book. It is a slow-burn romance defined by secrecy, moral compromise, and the haunting question of how much one is willing to sacrifice to keep a loved one tethered to their version of reality.

The central theme of the novel—the ethical cost of altering the past—is handled with a sobering grace. Many time-travel narratives treat history as a playground, a place to be tweaked for a "better" outcome. Pulley takes a much darker, more philosophical approach. She posits that the past is not a malleable draft, but a tragedy that demands payment. Joe’s journey forces the reader to grapple with a difficult realization: when you change history to save the future, you are essentially erasing the version of the world that fostered your existence. It is a deeply melancholic meditation on identity, suggesting that we are the sum of our scars and the specific tragedies that shaped us.

The pacing is frantic and exhilarating, shifting from the shadowy, gaslit streets of occupied London to the icy, rebellious waters of the North Sea. Yet, beneath the high-stakes naval battles and the intricate plot twists, the book remains intensely intimate. Pulley is an author who trusts her reader’s intelligence; she does not pause to over-explain the mechanics of her time-looping world, preferring instead to immerse you in the disorientation of her characters. 

The Kingdoms is a brilliant, bruising achievement. It is a book for those who want their fiction to challenge their perception of cause and effect, and who appreciate a love story that feels as inevitable as it is impossible. Natasha Pulley has not just written a novel; she has constructed a labyrinth, and by the time you reach the final page, you will be more than happy to have lost your way in it.
