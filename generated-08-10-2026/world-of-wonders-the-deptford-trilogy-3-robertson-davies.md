# World of Wonders (The Deptford Trilogy, #3)

**Author:** Robertson Davies  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1976

Robertson Davies’s *World of Wonders* stands as a magnificent, shimmering arch over *The Deptford Trilogy*, bringing one of Canadian literature’s most ambitious narrative structures to a dazzling close. Published in 1976, this final volume shifts the spotlight entirely onto Magnus Eisengrim—formerly Paul Dempster, the boy whose premature birth and subsequent traumas set the entire trilogy’s dark mythology into motion. If the first two books, *Fifth Business* and *The Manticore*, were exercises in psychological excavation and guilt, *World of Wonders* is pure theatricality, a grand-scale illusion where the magician finally reveals his secrets, only to prove that the magic was real all along.

At the heart of the novel lies the compelling theme of identity as a construct—a performance curated, rehearsed, and ultimately mastered in the face of cruelty and abandonment. Davies explores how suffering does not merely break a person; rather, in the hands of an artist, it becomes raw material. Magnus Eisengrim, spirited away in his youth by a predatory carnival illusionist, does not merely survive his exploitation; he transmutes it. Through years of rigorous apprenticeship, traveling the rough circuits of European sideshows before ascending to the status of the world’s most illustrious stage magician, Magnus learns that the line between deception and transcendence is remarkably thin. Davies uses the metaphor of magic not just as a parlor trick, but as a profound philosophical lens through which to examine human psychology, art, and the human need for wonder in an increasingly cynical world. 

Davies’s stylistic brilliance is on full, uninhibited display here. He writes with the erudition of a classicist, the timing of a master raconteur, and the psychological acuity of a lifelong student of Jung. The novel is structured primarily as a long, spellbinding confession told over several days in a Swiss castle among a gathering of old acquaintances and ghosts from the characters' shared past in Deptford. This framework allows Davies to indulge his love for digression, folklore, theatrical history, and philosophical musing without ever losing narrative momentum. His prose is rich, dense, and luminous, layered with wit and dark irony. Every sentence feels meticulously crafted, designed to pull the reader into a trance just as surely as Magnus pulls his audiences into his stage illusions. 

What makes *World of Wonders* so striking is how it recontextualizes everything that came before it. Characters who appeared villainous or pitiful in the earlier volumes are granted complex, often heartbreaking dimensions. Davies refuses easy moral categorizations, preferring instead to show how art, vengeance, survival, and redemption are inextricably tangled together in the human psyche. 

For readers who appreciate literature that treats the mind as a labyrinth and storytelling as a high art form, this novel is an absolute triumph. It is a fittingly magnificent finale to a modern classic, reminding us that life itself is a grand stage production filled with shadows, smoke, and undeniable wonder.
