# Dead Things (Eric Carter, #1)

**Author:** Stephen Blackmoore  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

**Dead Things by Stephen Blackmoore: A Gritty, Ghostly Revenge Tale with Bite**

Stephen Blackmoore’s *Dead Things*—the first installment in the Eric Carter series—is a razor-sharp urban fantasy that blends the macabre with the mundane, wrapping them in a protagonist so morally gray and sarcastically sharp that you’ll find yourself rooting for him even as he chews through the dead and the living alike. This isn’t a story about ghosts whispering secrets or necromancers wielding grand magic; it’s about a man who *uses* the dead for a living, and when his sister’s murder drags him back to the city he swore he’d never see again, he becomes the very thing he’s spent years exorcising—vengeful, ruthless, and utterly alone.

At its core, *Dead Things* is a revenge thriller disguised as urban fantasy. Eric Carter, a necromancer with a knack for putting restless spirits to rest (for a price), is the kind of protagonist who makes you question whether he’s the hero or the villain. He’s a man who’s seen too much death, who’s buried too many bodies—both literal and metaphorical—and now, with his sister’s murder, he’s been forced back into the game. The city of Los Angeles, once a place of ghosts and half-truths, is now a graveyard of his own making, and Carter is determined to dig up the answers before the same fate claims him. But the deeper he goes, the more he realizes that some deaths aren’t just personal—they’re cosmic. The patron saint of violent death, Santa Muerte, seems to have taken an interest in him, and the ghosts he’s been paid to silence are starting to talk back.

Blackmoore’s writing is a masterclass in dark, cynical humor and visceral storytelling. Carter’s voice is a perfect blend of weary sarcasm and simmering rage, delivering lines with the kind of dry wit that makes him feel like a real person—flawed, exhausted, and utterly compelling. The prose is lean, efficient, and packed with a gritty realism that makes the supernatural feel grounded. When Carter describes the stench of a necromancer’s workshop or the hollow look in a ghost’s eyes, you don’t just *read* it—you *feel* it. The book doesn’t waste time on flowery descriptions or over-explained lore; instead, it throws you into the chaos of Carter’s world, where every interaction is a gamble, every ally could be a traitor, and every ghost might just be a weapon waiting to be unleashed.

Themes of guilt, redemption, and the cost of survival run deep in *Dead Things*. Carter is a man haunted by his past, both literally and figuratively. He’s the kind of protagonist who doesn’t seek forgiveness—he demands it, and if he doesn’t get it, he’ll take what he wants. His relationship with his sister, though never fully explored, looms over the story like a specter, a reminder of the life he left behind and the man he’s become. The book doesn’t shy away from the moral ambiguity of its protagonist, and that’s what makes Carter so compelling. He’s not a hero; he’s a survivor, and in a genre often dominated by self-righteous protagonists, that’s a refreshing change.

The supporting cast is equally intriguing, though their roles are secondary to Carter’s mission. From the gangsters with scores to settle to the mages who’ve been dead for centuries but still have teeth, every character feels like a piece of a larger puzzle. The ghosts Carter encounters aren’t just mindless spirits—they’re echoes of the past, some vengeful, others desperate for closure, and all of them a reminder that death doesn’t always stay buried. The tension between Carter and the supernatural elements of the story is well-balanced; the magic feels real, the stakes feel high, and the payoff is as satisfying as it is brutal.

One of the standout elements of *Dead Things* is its tone. Blackmoore walks a fine line between horror and urban fantasy, creating a world where the dead are as much a part of the living world as the air they breathe. The humor is dark and biting, the violence is unflinching, and the emotional core of the story is wrapped in a cloak of cynicism that makes it all the more powerful. It’s a book that doesn’t pull punches, and that’s exactly what makes it so engaging. You won’t find any grand speeches or moralizing here—just a man doing what he has to do, surrounded by ghosts and danger, and willing to burn it all down if it means getting answers.

If you’re looking for a urban fantasy series that’s fast-paced, morally complex, and dripping with atmosphere, *Dead Things* is a great place to start. It’s not a story about saving the world; it’s about saving yourself, and in the process, you’ll find a protagonist who’s as unforgettable as the world he inhabits. Blackmoore’s writing is sharp, his characters are flawed, and his story is a thrilling ride through the underbelly of a city where the dead never truly stay dead.

In the end, *Dead Things* is more than just a revenge tale—it’s a meditation on the cost of survival, the weight of the past, and the fine line between hero and monster. And with a protagonist as compelling as Eric Carter, you’ll be rooting for him to cross that line, no matter how dark it gets.
