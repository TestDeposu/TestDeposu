# Humans (Neanderthal Parallax, #2)

**Author:** Robert J. Sawyer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2024

**Robert J. Sawyer’s *Humans* Rewrites Humanity—And It’s Brilliant**

What if *Homo sapiens* had never risen to dominance? What if the Neanderthals—our clever, resilient cousins—had instead shaped civilization, science, and culture? In *Humans*, the second installment of Robert J. Sawyer’s *Neanderthal Parallax* trilogy, Sawyer doesn’t just ask these questions; he answers them with a story so vivid, so morally complex, that it forces readers to confront the very foundations of what it means to be human. This isn’t just another alternate-history sci-fi novel—it’s a mirror held up to our own assumptions, a thought experiment so bold it could redefine how we see ourselves.

Sawyer, a master of speculative fiction with a knack for blending hard science with deep philosophical inquiry, has crafted a world where Neanderthals—*Homo neanderthalensis*—are the dominant intelligent species. In this parallel Earth, *Homo sapiens* went extinct millennia ago, leaving behind only fragmented genetic traces. The Neanderthals, with their superior cold-adapted physiology, their intricate tool-making, and their complex social structures, evolved into a civilization that values cooperation, adaptability, and a deep connection to the natural world. Their science is grounded in observation and practicality; their art is functional yet expressive; their politics are built on consensus rather than hierarchy. And yet, for all their differences, they are still *us*—just a different kind of human.

The novel follows Ponter Boddit, a Neanderthal physicist whose brilliance and curiosity have made him a key figure in his world. When a mysterious portal opens between the two Earths, Ponter is sent to our world, where he encounters Mary Vaughan, a geneticist from our timeline who has been studying Neanderthal DNA. Their reunion is tender, charged with the weight of two civilizations that have never met—until now. Sawyer’s writing shines in these moments, weaving together romance, science, and existential dread with a deft hand. Ponter, with his sharp wit and unshakable curiosity, becomes an unforgettable guide to a world that is both alien and eerily familiar. His interactions with Mary—who is both fascinated and unsettled by his perspective—drive the novel’s emotional core, forcing readers to question: *What would we lose if we weren’t the dominant species? What would we gain?*

The real genius of *Humans* lies in Sawyer’s ability to make the unfamiliar feel tangible. He doesn’t just describe Neanderthal culture—he immerses the reader in it. The world-building is meticulous, grounded in real anthropology and paleontology, yet never pedantic. Sawyer understands that the best science fiction doesn’t just explain the science; it makes the reader *feel* the world. Whether it’s the way Neanderthals approach problem-solving (prioritizing teamwork over individual genius) or their relationship with technology (which is more about adaptation than innovation), every detail serves a purpose. The result is a civilization that feels as real as our own, yet utterly different—a civilization that challenges our prejudices about what intelligence, morality, and progress look like.

But *Humans* isn’t just about world-building. It’s a meditation on identity, survival, and the stories we tell ourselves about who we are. Sawyer, who has won nearly every major sci-fi award for a reason, knows that the best speculative fiction doesn’t just entertain—it provokes. In this novel, he asks: *If Neanderthals had taken over, would we still be the same species?* The answer, of course, is no. And that’s the point. Sawyer isn’t just writing about an alternate history; he’s writing about the fragility of human exceptionalism. Our world, with its emphasis on competition, individualism, and rapid technological advancement, is just one possible outcome. Another world—one where cooperation, resilience, and a deeper bond with nature define civilization—is just as valid, just as rich, just as *human*.

The prose is crisp, the pacing is relentless, and Sawyer’s knack for dialogue makes even the most complex ideas feel immediate. Whether Ponter is debating physics with a skeptical colleague or Mary is grappling with the ethical implications of genetic engineering, the writing never falters. Sawyer’s ability to balance hard science with emotional depth is what makes his work so compelling. *Humans* is no exception—it’s a novel that will leave you thinking long after the last page.

In many ways, *Humans* is the culmination of Sawyer’s career—a work that distills everything he’s learned about storytelling, science, and the human condition into a single, breathtaking narrative. It’s a book that will appeal to fans of *Hominids* (the first book in the trilogy) as well as newcomers to Sawyer’s work. It’s a book that will challenge your assumptions, make you laugh, make you cry, and leave you questioning everything you thought you knew about humanity.

If you’ve ever wondered what the world would look like if *Homo sapiens* had never become the dominant species, *Humans* is your answer. And it’s a world worth exploring.
