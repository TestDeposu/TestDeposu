# The Death of Superman

**Author:** Dan Jurgens  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

When Dan Jurgens and the collective powerhouse of DC’s Superman creative teams unleashed The Death of Superman in 2016, they revisited a monumental piece of comic book history that transcended the medium. This collected edition captures a unique cultural phenomenon—the exact moment pop culture stopped to watch the seemingly invincible Man of Steel meet his ultimate match. Far beyond a mere gimmick, the narrative serves as a gripping, high-stakes exploration of sacrifice, resilience, and the fragile boundary between civilization and utter annihilation.

At the heart of the book lies a singular, relentless theme: the heavy burden of ultimate power when faced with mindless, unstoppable destruction. Doomsday is not a traditional comic book villain driven by complex motivations, grand schemes, or ideological malice. Instead, he represents a force of nature—an embodiment of pure entropy. As Doomsday carves a path of devastation across America, the narrative strips away the typical superhero banter and philosophical debates, replacing them with a mounting sense of dread. The Justice League falls not out of incompetence, but as a sobering demonstration that some threats require the ultimate sacrifice. 

Jurgens and his collaborators—including Jerry Ordway and Louise Simonson—masterfully handle the pacing of the collection, which spans several interconnected titles like Action Comics, Adventures of Superman, and Superman: The Man of Steel. This collaborative writing style creates a sprawling, cinematic scope. Readers bear witness to the panic of Metropolis and the desperate valor of its protector through a kaleidoscope of perspectives. 

The stylistic peak of the volume arrives in Superman #75, an artistic and structural tour de force where every single page features a massive, single-panel splash. This daring layout slows down time, forcing the reader to absorb every brutal blow, every agonizing breath, and the grim inevitability of the final confrontation. It is a masterclass in visual storytelling, stripping the page count of clutter to amplify the emotional weight of the tragedy. 

Furthermore, the inclusion of Newstime: The Life and Death of Superman adds a fascinating journalistic texture to the collection. By framing the hero’s passing through the lens of a fictional in-universe magazine, the creators lean into the surreal national grief that defined the original 1992 publication. 

The Death of Superman remains essential reading. It is a relentless, emotionally resonant epic that proves even gods can fall, leaving those they protected to grapple with a world suddenly devoid of hope.
