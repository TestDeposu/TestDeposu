# Death Blows (The Bloodhound Files, #2)

**Author:** D.D. Barant  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

Death Blows thrusts readers back into the slick, shadow‑slicked streets of the Unnatural States of America, where FBI profiler Jace Valchek now navigates a bureaucratic nightmare as an NSA analyst answering to a vampire boss. D.D. Barant follows up the promising start of Dying Bites with a sequel that sharpens both plot and tone, turning the series’ premise—an ordinary human thrust into a parallel world of vampires, werewolves, and outlawed comic‑book magic—into a tightly wound thriller that feels simultaneously familiar and refreshingly original.

At its core, Death Blows explores the friction between order and chaos, law and myth. Jace’s investigation into a flash‑styled murder forces her to confront the literal power of stories: banned comics aren’t just relics; they are conduits for reality‑bending spells that can resurrect dead heroes or summon nightmare villains. Barant uses this metafictional hook to examine how narratives shape identity and authority, asking whether a society that outlaws imagination can ever truly control the monsters it creates. The answer unfolds in gritty alleyways, neon‑lit noir clubs, and the clandestine black‑market where rogue artists trade in forbidden panels—a setting that lets the author blend hard‑boiled detective tropes with the bright, exaggerated aesthetics of superhero comics.

Barant’s style is where the novel truly shines. She writes with a rapid, punchy rhythm that mirrors the adrenaline of a chase scene, yet she never sacrifices depth for speed. Jace’s first‑person voice is sharp, sardonic, and unexpectedly vulnerable; her internal monologue flips between hard‑edged profiler analysis and wry, pop‑culture‑laden asides that keep the tone light even as the body count rises. Dialogue crackles with authenticity—vampire bosses speak with aristocratic contempt, werewolf informants growl in street‑wise slang, and the occasional comic‑book narrator breaks in with exaggerated, caption‑box flourishes that remind us we’re never far from the source material’s playful spirit.

World‑building remains a strong suit. The Unnatural States feel lived‑in: bureaucratic memos sit alongside ancient grudges, and the legal ramifications of magical copyright are treated with a dead‑pan seriousness that heightens the absurdity. Barant drops hints of larger conspiracies—government agencies experimenting with crossover spells, a shadowy cabal seeking to weaponize mythic archetypes—giving the novel a sense of momentum that promises even higher stakes in future installments.

If there is a minor drawback, it lies in occasional reliance on exposition dumps that slow the breakneck pace, especially when explaining the intricacies of comic‑book magic. Yet these moments are brief and often redeemed by Barant’s knack for turning lore into vivid, visual set pieces—think a crime scene where a victim is literally “fried to the bone” while dressed as the Flash, a tableau that is as shocking as it is thematically resonant.

Death Blows succeeds as a follow‑up that deepens the series’ mythology while delivering a self‑contained, addictive mystery. It celebrates the power of stories—not just as escapism, but as forces that can shape reality, for better or worse. Fans of urban fantasy, paranormal procedural, and superhero satire will find plenty to savor, and newcomers will be hooked by Jace’s relentless drive and the world’s addictive blend of darkness and dazzle.
