# Being

**Author:** Kevin Brooks  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Being (2009) by Kevin Brooks thrusts readers straight into a pulse‑pounding nightmare that feels less like a story and more like a high‑stakes chase captured on grainy CCTV. Seventeen‑year‑old Robert Smith wakes from a routine gastroscopy to find his body a grotesque collage of plastic and metal, a revelation that instantly shatters the ordinary world he knows. The scene is stark, clinical, and disturbingly vivid—Brooks’ prose mirrors the sterile operating theatre, each sentence as precise as a scalpel, pulling the reader into Robert’s shock without flinching.

From that moment, the novel becomes a relentless manhunt across the bleak backroads and rain‑slicked cities of England. Brooks adopts a kinetic, almost cinematic style: short, jagged chapters propel the narrative forward, while intermittent flashbacks and internal monologues provide just enough breathing space to let the tension simmer before it erupts again. The author’s talent lies in balancing visceral action with psychological depth; Robert’s frantic flight is constantly underscored by his desperate quest to understand what he has become—a hybrid of flesh and foreign technology that hints at larger, shadowy experiments.

The central theme of Being is the struggle for identity when the self is no longer wholly human. Brooks explores how external forces—government agencies, shadowy operatives, and the relentless media gaze—attempt to label, contain, and erase the unknown. Robert’s ally, Eddi, offers a glimpse of normalcy and humanity, yet even her loyalty is tested as the conspiracy tightens its grip. The novel raises unsettling questions about autonomy, the ethics of scientific advancement, and the lengths to which power will go to maintain control.

Stylistically, Brooks avoids ornamental language in favor of a blunt, urgent voice that matches the protagonist’s adrenaline‑fueled perspective. Dialogue is terse, often laced with suspicion, while descriptive passages focus on sensory details—the chill of a deserted train station, the metallic taste of fear, the hum of distant surveillance drones—immersing the reader in a world that feels both familiar and eerily altered.

What makes Being compelling is its ability to marry a breakneck plot with a genuine exploration of what it means to be human in an age where the line between biology and technology blurs. Brooks delivers a thriller that is as intellectually provocative as it is viscerally exciting, leaving readers breathless and questioning the very nature of their own existence long after the final page.
