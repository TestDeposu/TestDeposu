# El rostro ajeno

**Author:** Kōbō Abe  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1964

To read Kōbō Abe is to willingly step into a labyrinth where the walls are made of mirrors and the exit is a trapdoor to the subconscious. Published in 1964, the same year that solidified his international masterpiece The Woman in the Dunes, El rostro ajeno—widely known in the English-speaking world as The Face of Another—stands as a chilling, masterclass interrogation of identity, alienation, and the masks we wear to survive the gaze of society. 

The premise is deceptively simple, bordering on the clinical. An unnamed research scientist suffers a catastrophic laboratory accident that leaves his face hideously scarred, a grotesque topography of burns that alienates him not only from the world at large, but intimately and devastatingly from his own wife. He becomes a phantom in his own home, a man erased by his own flesh. Driven by a volatile mix of shame, scientific detachment, and a desperate, creeping malice, he embarks on a dangerous project: to construct a perfect, lifelike artificial face. But Abe is not interested in science fiction tropes of plastic surgery; he is interested in existential horror. Once the mask is completed and worn, it begins to alter the wearer. Who is speaking behind the synthetic skin—the man who was, or the stranger he has become?

Abe’s style is uniquely his own, heavily indebted to the cold logic of existentialism yet dripping with the visceral, surreal atmosphere of a fever dream. Often heralded as Japan’s Kafka, Abe writes with a clinical detachment that paradoxically heightens the emotional terror of his narratives. He approaches psychological decay with the precision of a surgeon, dissecting human motives until they are laid bare and bleeding. In El rostro ajeno, the prose is razor-sharp, rhythmic, and claustrophobic. The protagonist’s internal monologue functions as a courtroom where he is simultaneously the accused, the prosecutor, and the doomed judge. 

The novel’s central theme—the "otherness" of the self—resonates with terrifying modern relevance. Long before the era of curated social media avatars, digital filters, and algorithmic identities, Abe was diagnosing the modern malady of performative existence. He posits that the face is not merely a biological necessity, but our primary social currency, the boundary line between the private self and the public monster. By creating a false face, the protagonist hopes to achieve absolute freedom, untethered from his past identity. Instead, he discovers that a mask demands a master; the artificial skin begins to dictate his desires, leading him toward a plot of calculated revenge against the very society that cast him out. 

Through notebooks left behind, philosophical tangents, and tense domestic encounters, Abe builds a narrative suspense that does not rely on traditional plot twists, but on the agonizing unfurling of a fractured mind. The wife becomes an enigmatic antagonist, the laboratory a modern-day panopticon, and the mask a symbol of the ultimate alienation of mid-century industrial man.

El rostro ajeno is not a comfortable read, nor is it meant to be. It is a scalpel to the throat of human vanity and a profound meditation on what remains of us when our reflection is stolen. Abe demands that we look into the abyss of our own artificiality—and warns us that if we stare too long at a stranger in the mirror, the stranger might just stare back.
