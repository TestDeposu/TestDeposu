# This is Not a Game (Dagmar #1)

**Author:** Walter Jon Williams  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

**This Is Not a Game: Where the Lines Between Fiction and Reality Collapse**

Walter Jon Williams’ *This Is Not a Game* (Dagmar #1) is a thrilling, razor-sharp exploration of how far we’ll go to escape—or dive into—the illusions we create. At its core, this near-future cyber-thriller is a chilling meditation on the blurred boundaries between online escapism and real-world consequence, wrapped in a high-stakes mystery that feels eerily prescient in an era of deepfake culture, AI personas, and the psychological hunger for digital identity.

The novel follows Dagmar, a woman whose life becomes inextricably tangled with the virtual worlds she designs. When she stumbles upon a real-life conspiracy that mirrors the puzzles she crafts for her online games, she’s drawn into a labyrinthine chase that spans continents—from the neon chaos of Los Angeles to the shadowy underbelly of international finance. But the true horror isn’t just the danger she faces; it’s the unsettling realization that the game *has* her. The fictional characters she’s created begin reaching out, the problems she’s solved in simulation now demanding real-world solutions. And the deeper she digs, the more she uncovers about her own past—including a secret gaming group from college that may hold the key to her survival.

Williams, a master of speculative fiction with a knack for dissecting human obsession, doesn’t just tell a story here; he dissects the psychology of immersion. The novel’s strength lies in its unflinching portrayal of gamers—not as mindless addicts, but as people who, in their own ways, seek control in a world that feels increasingly chaotic. The online communities Dagmar navigates are vibrant, flawed, and deeply human, their dynamics rendered with the same sharp wit and social insight that made *The Rift* a standout. Yet, the real genius of *This Is Not a Game* is how it flips the script: the "players" aren’t just passive consumers of fiction; they’re the architects of their own nightmares.

The prose is lean, precise, and effortlessly immersive, with Williams balancing cyberpunk grit with dark humor. His descriptions of the digital and physical worlds feel lived-in, whether it’s the glitchy, hyper-realistic simulations of the games or the gritty, human-scale details of Dagmar’s real-life entanglements. The pacing is relentless, with each revelation pulling the reader deeper into the mystery, much like a well-designed puzzle game that refuses to let you quit. There’s no easy resolution, no neat separation between the virtual and the real—just the creeping dread of a world where the lines have dissolved entirely.

Critics have rightly praised *This Is Not a Game* for its incisive commentary on technology’s role in shaping reality. Gary Wolfe of *Locus* notes how Williams, drawing from his own experience as a gamer, captures the subculture’s duality: the thrill of agency paired with the disorientation of living in a mediated existence. The novel doesn’t just ask whether we’re playing games—it asks what happens when the games start playing *us*. And the answer is terrifyingly plausible.

What makes this book even more compelling is its timeliness. In an age where AI-generated voices, deepfake videos, and virtual influencers blur the line between fiction and fact, Williams’ exploration of digital identity feels eerily prophetic. The novel doesn’t just entertain; it challenges readers to question how much of their own lives they’ve outsourced to the simulations they create.

Ultimately, *This Is Not a Game* is a thrilling, thought-provoking ride—a cyber-thriller that’s also a psychological study of human desire, a satire of digital culture, and a cautionary tale about the cost of escapism. Williams’ writing is sharp, his world-building immersive, and his narrative impossible to put down. If you love stories that challenge your perceptions of reality, this is a must-read.
