# Wanderlust (Dragonlance: Meetings Sextet, #2)

**Author:** Mary Kirschoff  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

To step into the pages of Mary Kirschoff’s Wanderlust is to willingly lose oneself in the rugged, windswept expanse of Ansalon before the world broke apart. As the second installment in the Dragonlance: Meetings Sextet, this modest volume punches well above its weight, carving out a vital space in a legendary franchise often dominated by grand wars and sprawling pantheons. Kirschoff achieves something quietly remarkable here: she takes the grand mythos of Dragonlance and shrinks it down to the human—and Kender—scale, offering a poignant exploration of what it truly means to wander.

At the heart of the narrative lies the defining theme of displacement and the search for belonging. While epic fantasy is frequently obsessed with destiny, crowns, and apocalyptic threats, Wanderlust thrives on the margins of the map. Kirschoff examines the psychological weight of the road. For her characters, wandering is not merely a physical journey or an impulsive whim; it is a coping mechanism, an existential state, and, occasionally, a desperate escape from grief and expectation. The author strips away the armor of legendary heroism to reveal the vulnerable, restless souls beneath. Through sparse yet evocative prose, she captures the bittersweet ache of the horizon—the eternal promise that something better lies just over the next hill, tempered by the quiet loneliness of never quite staying put.

Kirschoff’s style is characterized by a deceptive simplicity. She writes with a workmanlike clarity that serves the source material admirably, eschewing flowery indulgence in favor of emotional resonance and brisk pacing. Yet, beneath this unpretentious exterior lies a keen ear for dialogue and a sharp, observational wit. Kirschoff understands the inherent risks of writing fantasy that relies heavily on established lore: it can easily curdle into pastiche or fan service. Instead, she treats the world of Krynn with a lived-in authenticity. Landscapes feel weathered; towns smell of damp earth, ale, and woodsmoke; and the magic feels ancient and unpredictable rather than mechanical. 

What makes Wanderlust particularly striking is Kirschoff’s handling of character dynamics. She possesses a rare dexterity in balancing levity with melancholy. The narrative moves effortlessly from moments of sharp, character-driven humor to stretches of quiet introspection. The author refuses to sanitize her subjects; they are flawed, stubborn, and frequently running from their own shadows. This psychological realism elevates the book from a mere tie-in adventure to a genuine character study. Kirschoff’s pacing ensures that the pages turn themselves, yet she wisely allows her characters moments to simply breathe, reflecting on the firesides they are leaving behind and the uncertainties waiting in the dark.

For both longtime veterans of the Dragonlance saga and newcomers stumbling upon the ruins of Ansalon for the first time, Wanderlust delivers a surprisingly rich experience. It reminds us that before heroes save the world, they must first figure out who they are when no one is watching. Mary Kirschoff crafts a compelling miniature epic about the spaces between destinations, proving that the most important journeys are not measured in leagues traveled, but in the pieces of ourselves we discover—and leave behind—along the way.
