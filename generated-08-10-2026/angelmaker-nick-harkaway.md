# Angelmaker

**Author:** Nick Harkaway  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

Angelmaker bursts onto the page like a wind‑up toy set loose in a mad scientist’s laboratory—bright, noisy, and impossible to ignore. Nick Harkaway takes the seemingly modest premise of a clock‑maker trying to outrun his gangster father’s shadow and spins it into a kaleidoscopic romp that blends spy thriller, steampunk fantasy, and outright farce. At its heart the novel asks a deceptively simple question: can we ever truly escape the legacy we inherit, or must we remake it on our own terms? Joe Spork’s quiet workshop becomes the battleground where inherited violence collides with personal redemption, and the ticking of gears mirrors the relentless pressure of history pressing down on the present.

Harkaway’s prose is the book’s true engine. He writes with a lyrical exuberance that feels both earnest and mischievous, layering dense, almost Joycean wordplay onto a narrative that never loses its forward momentum. Sentences tumble over one another like clockwork parts finding their place, each phrase crafted to surprise while still serving the story’s breakneck pace. The result is a style that feels simultaneously literary and pulpy—a rare alchemy that lets Harkaway linger on a vivid image of a pug‑loving retired spy one moment and launch into a breath‑less chase through London’s fog‑shrouded alleys the next. His humor is sharp but never cynical; it springs from the absurdity of his characters’ predicaments rather than from cheap satire, giving the novel a warm, almost affectionate tone even as it threatens global annihilation.

The novel’s strengths lie in its inventive world‑building and its refusal to stay pigeonholed. Harkaway stitches together motifs from John Ruskin’s aesthetic philosophy, Cold‑War espionage tropes, and classic pulp villainy into a cohesive, if delightfully chaotic, whole. The supporting cast—mad monks, psychopathic killers, a brass‑tongued accomplice named Polly—are each given enough quirk to feel memorable without overshadowing Joe’s internal journey. Edie Banister, the retired agent whose unfinished mission drives the plot, serves as a perfect foil to Joe: her world‑weary competence highlights his nascent courage, and their partnership evolves from reluctant alliance to genuine camaraderie.

If there is a minor flaw, it is that the novel’s sheer density of ideas can occasionally overwhelm the reader’s ability to pause and reflect. The plot hurtles forward with such vigor that quieter, character‑driven moments sometimes get swallowed by the next explosion of gadgetry or monologue. Yet this very momentum is also what makes Angelmaker so addictive; you keep turning pages not just to see what happens next, but to marvel at how Harkaway can keep tossing new, brilliant inventions into the mix without losing narrative coherence.

In short, Angelmaker is a head‑spinning, heart‑warming adventure that celebrates the joy of making—whether it’s fixing a clock, defusing a doomsday device, or rewriting one’s own destiny. Harkaway’s style is as inventive as his plot, delivering a reading experience that feels both nostalgically familiar and thrillingly new.
