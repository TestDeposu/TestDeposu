# The Chessmen of Mars (Barsoom, #5)

**Author:** Edgar Rice Burroughs  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

Step into the crimson dust and swirling, dying world of Edgar Rice Burroughs’s fifth Barsoom masterpiece, The Chessmen of Mars. Originally serialized in the early 1920s and enduring as a timeless pillar of planetary romance, this installment proves that the grandmaster of pulp fiction had lost none of his inventive verve. While readers often return to Mars for the sweeping heroics of John Carter, Burroughs wisely pivots the spotlight in this outing, offering a vibrant, pulse-pounding narrative driven by a formidable new generation and elevated by one of the genre’s most chillingly brilliant thematic conceits.

At the heart of the novel is Tara, Princess of Helium, daughter of the iconic John Carter and Dejah Thoris. Rightly rejecting the damsel-in-distress trope that often bogged down early twentieth-century genre fiction, Burroughs crafts Tara as fiercely independent, headstrong, and refreshingly unimpressed by superficial charm. When a fierce Martian storm strands her in the eerie, uncharted wilds of Barsoom, she is swiftly captured by the Kaldanes—a hauntingly grotesque alien species consisting of monstrously oversized, hyper-intelligent brains resting atop pathetic, crab-like legs. This encounter immediately introduces the novel’s central theme: the complex, sometimes parasitic relationship between intellect and physicality, mind and body, soul and machine. 

Burroughs explores this philosophical dichotomy brilliantly through the Kaldane Ghek and his symbiotic counterpart, the Rykor. While the Kaldanes possess supreme mental faculties, they are physically helpless without their engineered bodies, forcing a poignant meditation on dependence, evolution, and identity. It is intellectual science fiction wrapped in the colorful, visceral trappings of sword-and-planet adventure, showcasing Burroughs’s underrated capacity for world-building beyond mere swashbuckling.

True to the author’s signature style, however, the cerebral soon yields to the relentlessly breathless. Enter Prince Gahan of Gathol, who—posing in disguise as a humble warrior—rescues Tara, only for the pair to be captured yet again by the decadent, death-obsessed inhabitants of the ancient city of Manator. Here, Burroughs shifts gears from survivalist horror to high-stakes spectacle. The novel builds toward its unforgettable centerpiece: a brutal, gladiatorial game of Martian Chess, known locally as Jetan, where the living pieces on the board are human combatants forced to fight to the absolute death. 

This lethal tournament is vintage Burroughs at his absolute peak. He orchestrates the action with cinematic pacing, turning every tactical move on the board into a desperate struggle for survival. The atmosphere is thick with ancient decay, aristocratic cruelty, and the desperate grit of freedom fighters. Through the shifting dynamics between Tara and Gahan—whose disguises and trials force them to earn one another's mutual respect—Burroughs injects a deeply satisfying romantic tension that anchors the fantastic chaos.

Stylistically, The Chessmen of Mars represents the pinnacle of the planetary romance genre. Burroughs writes with an infectious, breakneck enthusiasm that sweeps the reader past any logistical improbabilities. His Mars is not merely a setting, but a living, breathing tapestry of lost empires, terrifying monsters, strange philosophies, and undying romance. The prose is lush, theatrical, and unapologetically dramatic, perfectly suited for a world where empires rise and fall on the whims of a die or the edge of a long-sword.

For longtime fans of the Barsoom saga, this novel offers the perfect blend of nostalgic comfort and wildly original concepts. For newcomers, it serves as an ideal entry point into a universe where courage defies impossible odds, and where the human spirit—whether tested by parasitic aliens or forced into the bloody squares of a death-match—always finds a way to move forward. Edgar Rice Burroughs remains the undisputed king of Martian escapism, and The Chessmen of Mars is a gleaming jewel in his imperial crown.
