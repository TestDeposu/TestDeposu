# Howling Dark (The Sun Eater, #2)

**Author:** Christopher Ruocchio  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

In the vast, star-strewn landscape of modern space opera, few voices possess the gravitational pull of Christopher Ruocchio. With Howling Dark, the second installment of The Sun Eater, Ruocchio doesn’t just continue the odyssey of Hadrian Marlowe; he masterfully expands the scale of the tragedy, proving that this series is less a mere adventure and more a searing meditation on the burden of history and the inevitability of myth.

If the series opener, Empire of Silence, was a study in isolation and the forging of a legend, Howling Dark is an immersion into the jagged reality of a man attempting to rewrite the trajectory of a civilization. Picking up decades after the events of the first novel, the story follows Hadrian as he hunts for the elusive planet Vorgossos, driven by a quixotic, desperate hope to achieve peace with the Cielcin—the very aliens who have plagued humanity for centuries.

The central theme here is the heavy weight of choice in the face of predestination. We are constantly reminded through Hadrian’s retrospective narration that he is the man who will eventually destroy a sun and slaughter billions. By positioning his protagonist as a seeker of peace, Ruocchio creates a gut-wrenching irony: every noble gesture, every diplomatic reach, and every act of mercy feels like a step toward a catastrophic, pre-ordained destiny. It is a brilliant play on the trope of the "Chosen One," subverting it to explore the suffocating nature of fame and the impossibility of true agency when the universe already has your funeral pyre prepared.

Ruocchio’s prose remains the series’ greatest asset. His style is opulent, rhythmic, and steeped in a "future-ancient" tone that owes as much to Gene Wolfe’s labyrinthine intellectualism as it does to the sweeping, operatic sensibilities of Frank Herbert. He writes with the gravity of a historian documenting a collapse, utilizing a heightened, almost liturgical vocabulary that demands the reader’s full attention. Yet, for all its grand philosophical scaffolding, the book never loses its visceral bite. The sequences beyond the borders of the Sollan Empire—a haunting frontier populated by transhuman horrors and the wreckage of forgotten civilizations—are rendered with a chilling, atmospheric clarity that feels plucked from the darkest corners of gothic horror.

The transition from the aristocratic, claustrophobic corridors of the Empire to the brutal, nomadic existence of a mercenary captain allows Ruocchio to stretch his world-building muscles. We see the decay of the Sollan hegemony through the eyes of those who exist in its margins, reinforcing the idea that the "peace" Hadrian seeks is a direct challenge to the warmongering inertia of his own people. 

Howling Dark is not a comfortable read, nor is it intended to be. It is a dense, melancholic, and immensely ambitious middle chapter that refuses to offer the easy payoffs of traditional science fiction. It is a slow-burn epic that prioritizes the internal erosion of its protagonist over mindless explosions, though when the violence finally arrives, it is as sudden and devastating as a supernova.

For readers who seek a narrative that blends the scale of an interstellar war with the intimate, agonizing self-reflection of a man trying to outrun his own shadow, Ruocchio has delivered a masterwork. Hadrian Marlowe is one of the most compelling figures in contemporary fiction, and as the fires of his future burn ever brighter, it becomes impossible to look away.
