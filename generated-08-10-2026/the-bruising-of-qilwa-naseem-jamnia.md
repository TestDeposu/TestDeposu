# The Bruising of Qilwa

**Author:** Naseem Jamnia  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

In The Bruising of Qilwa, Naseem Jamnia constructs a stunningly intimate yet politically charged debut novella that proves magnificent worlds do not require thousands of pages to leave a lasting mark. Set within a rich, queernormative, Persian-inspired secondary world, Jamnia introduces readers to a landscape that feels simultaneously ancient and achingly contemporary. At its core, this is a story about the immigrant experience, the resilience of chosen family, and the heavy, often transactional nature of survival in a society that conditionally welcomes the displaced. 

The narrative follows Firuz-e Jafari, a nonbinary practitioner of traditional Sassanian blood magic who has fled the slaughter of their homeland for the relative safety of the Free Democratic City-State of Qilwa. Working at a free healing clinic alongside their employer Kofi and mentoring a volatile orphan named Afsoneh, Firuz attempts to build a quiet life. However, tranquility is shattered when Firuz and Kofi uncover a strange, rapidly spreading disease that manifests as mysterious, debilitating bruises on its victims. As panic mounts, the affliction becomes a political weapon, and the marginalized refugee population is swiftly blamed for ineptly performed magic. 

Jamnia’s thematic ambition is immediately apparent, anchoring high fantasy tropes to raw, real-world sociopolitical friction. The author deftly explores the immigrant condition—the constant balancing act of assimilation, the precariousness of legal status, and the exhaustion of having to prove one’s worth just to exist. Blood magic in Qilwa is not merely a flashy system of spells; it is a profound metaphor for bodily autonomy, heritage, and the literal and figurative lifeblood that immigrants expend to carve out a home. When the new disease threatens Qilwa, it triggers systemic xenophobia, mirroring how public health crises in our own world are weaponized against vulnerable populations. Jamnia refuses to offer easy answers, demonstrating instead how systemic prejudice is deeply entrenched in bureaucracy and institutional indifference.

Stylistically, Jamnia’s prose is precise, evocative, and deeply empathetic. The world-building is delivered with an impressive economy of language. Rather than overwhelming the reader with sprawling infodumps, Jamnia immerses us in the sensory details of Qilwa—the smells of the clinic, the weight of magical exertion, the interpersonal warmth of a found family sharing a meal. The queernormative setting is handled with refreshing casualness; identities are accepted without exposition, allowing the emotional weight of the narrative to focus squarely on Firuz’s internal struggles with trauma, responsibility, and familial duty. 

Firuz makes for a compelling, wonderfully flawed protagonist. They are fiercely protective, occasionally stubborn, and profoundly weary, embodying the dual burden of being both a healer and a survivor. The dynamics between Firuz, Kofi, and Afsoneh form the beating heart of the novella. Through them, Jamnia explores the complexities of generational trauma and the healing power of chosen kin. 

The Bruising of Qilwa stands shoulder-to-shoulder with modern speculative fiction classics like The Broken Earth Trilogy and The Four Profound Weaves. It is a gripping, compassionate, and intellectually stimulating read that manages to be both a thrilling mystery and a poignant political critique. Jamnia has crafted a remarkable debut that pulses with life, demanding that we look at how we treat the stranger at our gates.
