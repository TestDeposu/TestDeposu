# The Day the Dead Came to Show and Tell (Newsflesh, #3.3)

**Author:** Mira Grant  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

The Day the Dead Came to Show and Tell drops readers straight into the cracked‑linoleum hallway of Zombie Elementary, a place where the hum of fluorescent lights tries to drown out the distant moans of the Rising. Mira Grant—writing under her Seanan McGuire guise—uses the confined world of a grade‑school classroom to sharpen the larger anxieties that have powered the Newsflesh trilogy since Feed first appeared. The novella’s central theme is the illusion of safety: the belief that walls, routines, and the innocence of childhood can shield us from a world that has already fallen apart. Grant strips that illusion away layer by layer, showing how the children’s earnest show‑and‑tell projects—drawings of family pets, sketches of favorite superheroes—become grotesque counterpoints to the creeping dread that seeps in through cracked windows and whispered rumors. The horror isn’t just in the shambling zombies outside; it’s in the way the adults cling to normalcy, in the forced laughter that rings hollow when a bite is discovered, and in the stark realization that truth, once a commodity to be reported, is now a liability that could get you killed.

Grant’s style remains as crisp and relentless as ever. She blends the journalistic urgency that defined her Newsflesh reporting voice with a keen ear for the cadence of child speech, creating a tonal dissonance that feels both unsettling and oddly authentic. Sentences are short, punchy, and often end on a grim beat, mirroring the staccato rhythm of a school bell that signals both recess and impending doom. Her world‑building is economical yet vivid: a single description of a hand‑sanitizer station repurposed as a makeshift triage point tells us more about the collapse of societal infrastructure than pages of exposition could. The novella’s pacing never flags; each scene pushes the narrative forward while allowing moments of quiet, almost painful, reflection on what is lost when the adult world fails to protect its youngest members.

What makes this entry in the Newsflesh canon particularly striking is how it reframes the series’ long‑standing meditation on media manipulation. In Feed, the danger lay in how information could be weaponized; here, the danger is in what information is withheld. The teachers’ attempts to maintain a curated narrative—of safety, of normalcy—mirror the larger propaganda machines that have plagued the series, but the setting makes the manipulation feel intimate and immediate. When a child’s innocent drawing of a zombie is mistaken for a threat, the line between harmless imagination and lethal paranoia blurs, forcing the reader to question who gets to define truth in a world where the truth itself is a moving target.

The emotional core of the story beats in the relationship between the protagonist—a bright, inquisitive fifth‑grader—and her weary teacher, who oscillates between protector and pragmatist. Their exchanges are laced with a tender, almost melancholic humor that underscores the tragedy of children forced to grow up too fast in a world that offers no guarantees. Grant’s ability to evoke empathy for both the young and the jaded, without resorting to sentimentality, showcases why her work continues to resonate across horror, science fiction, and speculative fiction audiences.

In a mere handful of pages, The Day the Dead Came to Show and Tell delivers a compact yet powerful punch: a reminder that sanctuaries are only as strong as the stories we tell ourselves to keep the dark at bay, and that, when those stories falter, the dead will inevitably find a way to crash show‑and‑tell. It’s a concise, chilling addition to the Newsflesh mythos that feels both essential for longtime fans and an accessible entry point for newcomers curious about how Grant balances terror with tenderness.
