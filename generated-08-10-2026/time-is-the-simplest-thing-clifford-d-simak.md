# Time Is the Simplest Thing

**Author:** Clifford D. Simak  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

Clifford D. Simak was never a writer interested in the cold, metallic triumph of hardware. While his contemporaries in the mid-twentieth century were often preoccupied with the nuts and bolts of rocketry, Simak looked toward the quiet, unsettling frontiers of the human psyche. In Time Is the Simplest Thing—first published in 1961 and remaining a haunting pillar of speculative fiction—he delivers a narrative that feels less like a space opera and more like an existential pursuit, proving once again why he remains one of the few Grand Masters to truly understand the loneliness of the stars.

The premise is deceptively cinematic: the Fishhook Corporation has commodified the impossible. By employing "parries"—individuals with the telepathic sensitivity to traverse the cosmos with their minds—they bring back the riches of distant worlds without ever burning a drop of fuel. It is a corporate dystopia rooted in the terrifying efficiency of the twenty-second century. But the story pivots sharply when Shepherd Blaine, a loyal agent, finds himself the vessel for an alien intelligence. What follows is a high-stakes flight from a corporate monolith that views him as a malfunctioning asset, set against a backdrop of a populace whipped into a frenzy of anti-parapsychological paranoia.

The brilliance of the novel lies in Simak’s pacing. He eschews the frantic, blaring urgency of modern thrillers in favor of a melancholic, intellectual tension. Simak’s prose is earthy, contemplative, and deeply empathetic. He writes about the "other" not as a monster to be conquered, but as a bridge to a wider consciousness. When Blaine flees, the reader is not just watching a man outrun hired guns; we are watching a human being struggle with the sudden, crushing expansion of his own soul.

Simak’s style is defined by a gentle, pastoral humanism that feels distinctively, almost stubbornly, his own. Even when describing the vast, terrifying emptiness of interstellar travel, he grounds the narrative in the fragility of the human condition. He explores the theme of alienation—the psychological distance between the explorer and the society that feeds on the fruits of his dangerous work—with a deft hand. He asks the uncomfortable question: what happens to a species that has grown too fearful of its own evolution?

By turning the "alien abduction" trope on its head, Simak explores the terrifying burden of enlightenment. Blaine is not just a fugitive; he is a man who has touched the infinite and found the parochial nature of humanity unbearable. The horror of the book does not come from the alien consciousness residing in his mind, but from the realization that his fellow humans have chosen ignorance and tribalism over the cosmic truth he now carries.

Time Is the Simplest Thing remains a staggering achievement because it dares to be quiet in a genre that often screams. It is a story about the intersection of corporate greed and the limitless potential of the mind, a theme that feels more relevant in our era of pervasive data-tracking and digital isolation than it did when it was first penned. Simak reminds us that while technology can bridge the physical gap between worlds, it is empathy—the ability to connect with another consciousness, however foreign—that truly determines our place in the universe.

For those looking for a science fiction novel that prioritizes philosophical depth and atmosphere over flashy pyrotechnics, this is a masterpiece of the genre. Simak does not just build a world; he invites you to sit with its discomfort, to share in its loneliness, and ultimately, to hope for a transcendence that lies just beyond the horizon of human fear. It is a classic that continues to resonate, reminding us that the greatest distances we travel are not through space, but within ourselves.
