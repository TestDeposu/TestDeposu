# Upsetting the Balance (Worldwar, #3)

**Author:** Harry Turtledove  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1996

**Upsetting the Balance: Harry Turtledove’s Masterstroke of Alternate-History Horror**

Harry Turtledove’s *Upsetting the Balance*—the third installment in his *Worldwar* trilogy—isn’t just a sequel; it’s a brutal escalation of cosmic dread, where humanity’s desperate unity against an otherworldly menace becomes a desperate dance with annihilation. If the first two books (*The War Against the Worlds* and *The Battle for Earth*) set the stage for Earth’s desperate alliance with its former enemies, *Upsetting the Balance* plunges us into the abyss of what happens when survival itself demands the unthinkable. Turtledove, ever the master of alternate history, doesn’t just reimagine World War II—he turns it into a global apocalypse, where the stakes are no longer political or ideological, but existential.

At its core, *Upsetting the Balance* is a story about the fragility of civilization when faced with forces beyond comprehension. The "Thrans," those monstrous invaders from the *Worldwar* series, are no longer just a distant threat—they’re a relentless, adaptive horror, striking with weapons and tactics that defy human logic. Cities burn. Armies collapse. And in the face of such overwhelming odds, the nations of Earth—once locked in mortal hatred—are forced into an uneasy truce. The United States, Nazi Germany, the Soviet Union, China, and Japan, each with their own grievances and ideologies, now stand shoulder to shoulder, their old rivalries drowned out by the roar of alien war machines. Turtledove doesn’t shy away from the irony: the very alliances that once tore the world apart are now the only thing keeping it from total destruction.

But the real horror isn’t just the Thrans—it’s the cost of humanity’s desperation. Turtledove explores the darkest corners of war without sentimentality. Nuclear weapons, once the domain of superpowers, are now deployed with terrifying frequency, reducing cities to radioactive wastelands. The book doesn’t glorify these acts; it shows their futility, their madness. Meanwhile, guerrilla warfare becomes a desperate last stand, as ordinary people and resistance fighters use every trick imaginable to survive against an enemy that seems invincible. The result is a grim, relentless narrative where hope is a luxury, and every victory feels like a pyrrhic one.

Turtledove’s prose is as sharp as it is unflinching. He doesn’t indulge in melodrama; instead, he delivers a cold, hard look at the realities of war—both human and alien. His descriptions of battle are visceral, his political maneuvering is intricate, and his portrayal of the Thrans is chillingly effective. The book doesn’t just tell you that humanity is losing; it makes you *feel* the weight of that loss, the exhaustion, the terror, and the slow realization that even if they win, the world they’re fighting for may no longer exist.

What makes *Upsetting the Balance* particularly compelling is its refusal to offer easy answers. There are no heroes here, only survivors. The United States, despite its technological edge, is stretched thin. Germany, with its advanced weapons and desperate tactics, is fighting a war it can’t win. The Soviet Union endures under the weight of its own brutality. And China, occupied by the Thrans, faces a future that’s even darker than the present. Turtledove doesn’t let us rest on the idea that humanity will triumph—because in this world, triumph might mean nothing if the world itself is reduced to ashes.

The book’s strength lies in its balance—between the grand strategic battles and the intimate, personal stories of those caught in the crossfire. Whether it’s a soldier in the Pacific, a scientist racing to build a weapon that might save the world, or a civilian trying to protect their family, Turtledove gives each character a voice, each struggle a weight. It’s a testament to his skill as a storyteller that he can make the fate of nations feel as personal as the fate of an individual.

By the end of *Upsetting the Balance*, you won’t just be wondering if humanity can survive—you’ll be wondering if it’s worth surviving. Turtledove doesn’t offer a neat resolution; he leaves you with the haunting question of whether the world, after all this, will ever be the same. And in that uncertainty, he delivers one of the most gripping and terrifying alternate-history narratives ever written.
