# The Color of Law: A Forgotten History of How Our Government Segregated America

**Author:** Richard Rothstein  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

The Color of Law: A Forgotten History of How Our Government Segregated America is not merely a chronicle of past injustices; it is a meticulously documented indictment of the very institutions that were supposed to protect and advance the public good. Richard Rothstein, a senior fellow at the Economic Policy Institute and a longtime scholar of housing policy, dismantles the comforting myth that residential segregation in the United States arose spontaneously from private prejudice or market forces. Instead, he shows, with relentless clarity, that federal, state, and local governments deliberately engineered—and continue to sustain—a landscape where Black families are systematically denied access to the wealth‑building opportunities tied to homeownership.

The book’s central theme is deceptively simple yet profoundly disturbing: segregation is a product of law, not luck. Rothstein builds his argument layer by layer, beginning with the New Deal era’s public housing projects, which were explicitly designed to separate white and Black tenants. He then moves through the Federal Housing Administration’s underwriting manuals that redlined neighborhoods deemed “high risk” simply because they housed African‑American residents, the Veterans Administration’s denial of GI Bill benefits to Black veterans, and the myriad zoning ordinances, suburban development incentives, and urban renewal programs that reinforced racial boundaries. Each chapter is anchored in concrete evidence—court cases, congressional testimony, policy memos, and statistical analyses—so that the reader never wonders whether the author is extrapolating from anecdote. The result is a forensic portrait of policy as a weapon of racial hierarchy.

What makes Rothstein’s style compelling is his ability to marry rigorous scholarship with narrative accessibility. He avoids the dense jargon that often alienates general readers from academic works, opting instead for clear, direct prose that reads like a well‑paced investigative report. Yet he never sacrifices depth for simplicity; each claim is buttressed by footnotes and a bibliography that would satisfy even the most demanding historian. The tone is earnest and urgent, but never polemical. Rothstein lets the facts speak, allowing the moral weight of the evidence to emerge naturally. This restraint makes the book’s conclusions all the more powerful: when the reader finishes a chapter on, say, the 1949 Housing Act, the realization that a single piece of legislation helped lock generations of Black families into under‑resourced neighborhoods feels less like an accusation and more like an unavoidable inference.

Beyond its historical recounting, The Color of Law functions as a call to action. Rothstein does not merely diagnose; he prescribes. He outlines concrete policy remedies—reinvesting in segregated communities, enforcing anti‑discrimination laws, revising zoning codes, and expanding affordable housing initiatives—that directly address the governmental roots of segregation he has exposed. By linking past injustices to present‑day disparities in wealth, education, and health, he bridges the gap between history and contemporary activism, inviting readers to see themselves as participants in an ongoing struggle for equity.

The book’s impact has been reflected in its reception: a New York Times bestseller, a Notable Book of the Year, and a frequent reference in legislative debates and court cases concerning housing discrimination. Its influence extends beyond academia into grassroots organizing, where activists cite its chapters to argue for reparative measures and to challenge the “de facto segregation” narrative that lets officials off the hook.

In sum, Richard Rothstein’s The Color of Law is a masterful blend of scholarly rigor and moral clarity. It rewrites the conventional story of American segregation, revealing how law and policy—not just personal bias—shaped the spatial and economic divides that persist today. For anyone seeking to understand the structural foundations of racial inequality—or to help dismantle them—this work is essential reading.
