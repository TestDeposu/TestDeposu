# The Stainless Steel Rat Goes to Hell (Stainless Steel Rat, #9)

**Author:** Harry Harrison  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1998

When it comes to science fiction satire, few characters possess the enduring, slippery charm of James Bolivar diGriz, better known to the galaxy as the Stainless Steel Rat. In Harry Harrison’s 1998 installment, The Stainless Steel Rat Goes to Hell, the master con artist and intergalactic thief finds himself facing a crisis far more terrifying than any heavily guarded bank vault: domestic tranquility followed swiftly by absolute, agonizing boredom. But as series fans well know, idleness for the Rat is merely the prelude to chaos. Harrison returns to his signature franchise with a breezy, tongue-in-cheek adventure that pokes fun at organized religion, technological hubris, and the eternal human obsession with the afterlife. 

The narrative kicks off on a lavish pleasure planet where Jim is desperately trying to adapt to a life of retirement alongside his equally lethal and equally brilliant wife, Angelina. This domestic bliss is abruptly shattered when Angelina vanishes after visiting the Temple of Eternal Truth—a slick, enigmatic spiritual institution promising its gullible, wealthy patrons a guaranteed sneak peek at Heaven. Naturally, this heavenly preview comes with a heavy price tag. Refusing to let his wife be out-conned by professional spiritual grifters, Jim infiltrates the Temple. However, instead of finding earthly justice, he is unceremoniously banished to a realm that looks, acts, and smells suspiciously like literal Hell, complete with sulfur, brimstone, and enthusiastic, pointy-tailed devils. 

At its core, the main theme of the novel is the timeless battle between institutionalized hypocrisy and individual liberty. Harrison uses the faux-spiritual Temple of Eternal Truth and its surprisingly high-tech underworld as a satirical playground to skewer spiritual profiteering. The concept of Hell in the novel is not merely a theological destination, but a bureaucratic nightmare engineered by cosmic con artists. Jim diGriz, the ultimate secular humanist and professional grifter, represents the triumph of clever pragmatism over dogmatic control. He refuses to accept divine or infernal judgment lying down, proving that a sharp mind, a stolen gadget, and an unshakable ego are far more powerful than any mythological punishment. 

Harrison’s style remains as effervescent, fast-paced, and unapologetically campy as it was when the series began in the 1960s. By 1998, the author had perfected the rhythm of the space opera farce. The prose is snappy, driven by snappy dialogue and Jim’s wry, first-person narration. Harrison does not bog the reader down with heavy techno-babble or grim philosophical despair; instead, he treats the absurdities of the universe with a joyous, lighthearted cynicism. The world-building is playfully cartoonish, blending high-tech sci-fi tropes with classic mythological imagery in a way that feels like a Saturday morning cartoon script written by a misanthropic philosopher. 

While long-running book series often suffer from fatigue by their ninth installment, Harrison keeps the energy remarkably high by leaning into the sheer ridiculousness of the premise. The Stainless Steel Rat Goes to Hell may not reinvent the wheel, nor does it strive for deep emotional gravity, but it delivers precisely what its audience craves: pure, unadulterated escapism. It is a brisk, highly entertaining romp that reminds us why Jim diGriz remains one of science fiction’s most endearing scoundrels. For veterans of the series, this trip to the underworld is a welcome reunion with an old friend, and for newcomers, it is a delightfully irreverent entry point into a classic sci-fi universe.
