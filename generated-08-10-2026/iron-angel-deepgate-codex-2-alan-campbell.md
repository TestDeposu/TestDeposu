# Iron Angel (Deepgate Codex, #2)

**Author:** Alan Campbell  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2008

Uriels of the industrial age and gods of brine and fog collide in Iron Angel, Alan Campbell’s blistering, relentlessly inventive sequel to his acclaimed dark fantasy debut, Scar Night. If the first volume introduced readers to the dizzying, vertigo-inducing architecture of Deepgate—the chained city suspended precariously over an endless abyss—this second installment plunges us directly into the howling void beneath. Campbell expands his canvas with the terrifying confidence of a veteran world-builder, delivering a sophomore effort that is darker, grander, and far more emotionally resonant than its predecessor.

At the heart of Iron Angel lies a compelling, grim exploration of divine abandonment and human resilience. Campbell’s universe is one where the celestial and the clockwork bleed into one another, creating a brutal theological battleground. The death of the god Ulcis in the previous novel has ruptured the cosmic order, leaving the gates of Hell yawning wide open. The central theme of the novel examines what happens to mortals when their deities are not only fallible, but actively destructive or indifferent. As the god Cospinol slowly drags his massive skyship toward the teetering city, bound to a grotesque titan of a man, Campbell paints a vivid picture of subjugation and divine tyranny. The gods here are not distant, benevolent figures of worship; they are heavy, breathing entities of blood, brine, and terrible purpose, imposing their cosmic agendas regardless of the collateral damage inflicted upon humanity.

Against this apocalyptic backdrop, Campbell deploys his signature style: a breathless fusion of baroque dark fantasy and gritty steampunk aesthetics. His prose is muscular, visceral, and unyielding. He does not merely describe a scene; he immerses the reader in sensory overload, utilizing the choking toxic fumes of burning structures, the metallic groans of failing suspension chains, and the damp, claustrophobic chill of the abyss. This is a world built on rusted iron, rotting rope, and ancient stone, yet it pulses with a strange, tragic life. The atmosphere is perpetually heavy with impending doom, perfectly mirroring the psychological state of a city placed under ruthless martial law. 

The narrative engine is driven by its deeply flawed, fiercely compelling characters. The dynamic between the toughened young angel Dill and the assassin Rachel Hael anchors the sprawling chaos in genuine human stakes. Following a harrowing spiritual hijacking—where Dill’s soul is cast into Hell while a ghostly archon commandeers his flesh—their desperate flight from Spine assassins forms the beating heart of the book. Campbell excels at writing outsiders who refuse to stay down, and Rachel’s fierce determination to reclaim her friend transcends the typical tropes of genre fiction, elevating the stakes from a mere clash of pantheons to a profoundly personal rescue mission.

Iron Angel is a masterclass in expanding a secondary world without losing the tight, claustrophobic tension that made the original premise so gripping. Campbell refuses to offer easy resolutions or comforting moral binaries. Instead, he constructs a sprawling, terrifyingly beautiful tapestry where angels are weapons, gods are tyrants, and humanity must fight tooth and nail just to carve out a few more breaths of air above the abyss. For fans of dark, ambitious speculative fiction that dares to stare directly into the void, this novel is an absolute triumph.
