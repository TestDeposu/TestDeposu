# Exultant (Destiny's Children, #2)

**Author:** Stephen Baxter  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2004

Stephen Baxter’s 2004 novel *Exultant*, the second installment in the *Destiny’s Children* sequence, stands as a towering monument to the uncompromising nature of hard science fiction. Known for his staggering temporal scales and bleakly pragmatic view of human survival, Baxter returns to the unimaginably vast canvas of the Xeelee Sequence, delivering a narrative that is as intellectually dizzying as it is emotionally grueling. If modern science fiction often retreats into the comfortable confines of human-scaled space opera, Baxter shoves his readers headfirst into the crushing abyss of deep time and cosmic warfare.

At the heart of *Exultant* is a war that has raged for over twenty thousand years between humanity and the enigmatic, god-like alien race known as the Xeelee. It is a conflict defined by staggering asymmetry; humanity is outmatched, cornered, and facing the grim prospect of absolute extinction. Yet, out of this desperation rises Pirius, a young pilot whose act of insubordination—flying into the future and returning—shatters the linear constraints of causality. This temporal transgression results in a fascinating narrative bifurcation: Pirius is split into two distinct entities, the older, battle-hardened "Pirius Blue" and his younger, uninitiated counterpart, "Pirius Red." 

Through this clever temporal mechanics device, Baxter explores his defining theme: the sheer malleability of identity in the face of cosmic indifference. The dual narrative allows Baxter to dissect how prolonged, existential warfare alters the human psyche. Pirius Blue carries the weight of a possible victory, yet he is crushed under the bureaucratic and military machinery of Commissary Nilis, while Pirius Red is taken to Earth to confront the horrifying, foundational truths of the society he has been bred to defend. The novel asks a chilling question: when a species fights for its very survival for millennia, does it eventually destroy the very soul of what it is trying to save? 

True to his reputation, Baxter does not offer easy comfort or sentimental resolutions. His style is resolutely hard-science driven, steeped in rigorous extrapolation, astrophysics, and a cyberpunk-inflected cynicism that prevents the narrative from tipping into starry-eyed space fantasy. The technology is heavy, the physics are punishing, and the political structures governing humanity's desperate remnants are cold and utilitarian. Baxter treats humanity not as the triumphant masters of the cosmos, but as a stubborn, bruised organism clinging to survival by a microscopic thread. 

Yet, for all its chilliness and bleakness, *Exultant* achieves moments of breathtaking transcendence. Baxter possesses a rare, Asimovian vision for the macro-evolution of our species, tracing the agonizing metamorphosis of humanity as it bends, breaks, and reshapes itself to match the terrifying scale of the universe. The pacing is punchy, the world-building is overwhelmingly ambitious, and the stakes could not possibly be higher. 

Ultimately, *Exultant* is not a light weekend escape; it is a demanding, intellectually rigorous, and ultimately triumphant exploration of endurance against the dark. Baxter reminds us that even when faced with annihilation and the chilling indifference of deep space, the human drive to fight, question, and evolve remains our most formidable weapon. It is hard science fiction at its absolute finest—absurdly ambitious, technically brilliant, and utterly unforgettable.
