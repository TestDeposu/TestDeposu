# Lady Tremaine

**Author:** Rachel Hochhauser  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2026

**Lady Tremaine by Rachel Hochhauser: A Subversive, Sparkling Reimagining of the Evil Stepmother**

Rachel Hochhauser’s *Lady Tremaine* is not just a retelling of *Cinderella*—it’s a masterful dismantling and reinvention of the very idea of the wicked stepmother, wrapped in prose so lush and sharp it feels like a blade honed by a master smith. This is a book that dares to ask: What if the woman we’ve been taught to despise was the only one brave enough to fight for her daughters in a world that would rather see them broken? With the same intellectual rigor as *Circe* and the soaring romance of *Bridgerton*, Hochhauser crafts a tale that is as much about power and sacrifice as it is about love—though love, in this story, is not the gentle, passive kind we’re used to. It is fierce, complicated, and often painful.

At its core, *Lady Tremaine* is a story about the myth of the perfect mother. Lady Etheldreda Verity Isolde Tremaine Bramley—Ethel, to those who know her—is a woman who has spent her life clinging to respectability, to the crumbling remnants of her late husband’s title, to the fragile illusion that she can secure her daughters’ futures through marriage. But Ethel is no villain. She is a survivor, a woman who has lost everything twice over—her first husband, her second, and now, the fragile stability of her home. Her stepdaughter, the one we might expect to be the heroine, is instead a prickly, defiant force who refuses to be molded into the "proper" daughter Ethel believes she must be. And yet, it is Ethel who carries the weight of the world on her shoulders, who makes the impossible choices, who risks everything for a chance at a better life.

Hochhauser’s genius lies in her refusal to let Ethel be a one-dimensional figure. She is not the villain of the story, nor is she the heroine—she is something far more interesting: a woman caught between the expectations of a cruel world and the love she cannot help but feel, even for the daughter who has spurned her. The novel’s strength lies in its unflinching portrayal of maternal love, not as something soft and nurturing, but as something raw, demanding, and sometimes destructive. Ethel’s love for her daughters is not the kind that bends to their will; it is the kind that shapes them, that pushes them, that refuses to let them be anything less than they are capable of being. And when that love collides with the sordid secrets of the royal family—secrets that could destroy her daughters’ futures—Ethel must choose between the security she has spent her life chasing and the truth she knows will set them free.

The prose is a joy to read, rich with detail and a rhythm that feels like a dance. Hochhauser’s descriptions are vivid, her dialogue crackling with wit and tension. The world she builds is one of opulence and decay, where the gilded cages of aristocracy hide rot beneath the surface. The peregrine falcon, a symbol of both freedom and predatory instinct, becomes a character in its own right, a reminder of the wildness that still lingers in Ethel despite her attempts to tame it. The royal ball, a moment of potential transformation, is rendered with all the glamour and danger of a high-stakes game, where one wrong move could mean ruin.

But what makes *Lady Tremaine* truly unforgettable is its subversion of the fairy tale. The wicked stepmother is not wicked—she is a woman who has been forced into the role by circumstance, who has been taught that love is a currency to be traded, not a right to be claimed. And the stepdaughter? She is not the damsel in distress. She is the one who sees through the lies, who refuses to be the perfect daughter, who demands to be seen for who she truly is. Hochhauser’s take on the Cinderella myth is not about finding a prince or a happy ending—it is about the messy, complicated reality of motherhood, of love, and of the choices we make when the world seems stacked against us.

This is a book that will resonate with anyone who has ever felt the weight of expectation, who has ever loved someone fiercely and been met with resistance, who has ever had to choose between the life they thought they wanted and the truth they knew they had to face. It is a celebration of women who refuse to be gentle, who refuse to be broken, who refuse to let the world dictate the terms of their love. And in doing so, Hochhauser has written something truly original—a story that feels both timeless and utterly of its time, a battle cry for a new kind of heroine, one who is not afraid to be flawed, to be angry, to be human.

*Lady Tremaine* is destined to be one of the most talked-about books of the year, not just because of its gripping plot or its dazzling prose, but because it dares to ask the questions we’ve been too afraid to ask about the women we’ve been taught to fear. It is a book that will leave you breathless, that will make you laugh, that will make you cry, and that will stay with you long after the last page. In a world that still needs to hear it, this is a story about the strength of mothers—and the daughters who refuse to be tamed.
