# Dragons of Summer Flame (Dragonlance: The New Generation, #2)

**Author:** Margaret Weis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

A Summer of Reckoning: Margaret Weis and the Burden of Legacy in Dragons of Summer Flame

In the grand tapestry of epic fantasy, few worlds evoke the nostalgic warmth of hearth-fire and steel quite like Krynn. With Dragons of Summer Flame, veteran co-creator Margaret Weis steps back into this beloved universe to shepherd the Dragonlance saga into its next evolutionary phase. Originally published as the grand bridge between the legendary Heroes of the Lance and their offspring, this novel serves as both a passing of the torch and a scorched-earth deconstruction of the mythology fans thought they knew. 

At its core, the novel pulses with a singular, driving theme: the inescapable weight of generational inheritance. In the shadow of the War of the Lance, the world of Krynn is locked in a deceptive lull, baking under a parched and blistering summer sun. Yet, this climate is merely a mirror for the simmering tensions of the soul. Weis explores how children must perpetually walk, stumble, or actively rebel against the towering monuments of their parents. 

Nowhere is this thematic resonance more striking than in the character of Steel Brightblade. As the son of the noble, martyred Sturm Brightblade and the fearsome dark warrior Kitiara Uth Matar, Steel is the ultimate battleground of bloodlines. Watching him ride to attack the High Clerist’s Tower—the very fortress where his father breathed his last—is a masterclass in tragic irony. Weis deftly maneuvers Steel through an identity crisis that forms the emotional beating heart of the book. He is not merely a soldier; he is a walking question mark, forced to decide whether he is bound to repeat the sins of his mother or reclaim the honor of his father.

Parallel to this internal war, the narrative expands to encompass a sweeping, apocalyptic external threat. Palin Majere, reeling from profound grief, seeks to breach the terrifying depths of the Abyss in pursuit of his enigmatic uncle, Raistlin. Simultaneously, a mysterious human girl named Usha emerges in Palanthas, claiming the lineage of the archmage while carrying a secret of catastrophic proportions. The ancient ogre-like Irda, desperate to stave off the relentless advance of the Knights of Takhisis, have unwittingly shattered the cosmic status quo by unleashing Chaos—the primordial god of destruction—upon Krynn. 

Weis’s authorial style in this installment is defined by a desperate, breathless urgency. While early Dragonlance adventures often favored a measured, quest-like progression, Dragons of Summer Flame moves with the frantic energy of a world unraveling at the seams. The prose is unpretentious and cinematic, prioritizing emotional momentum and high-stakes consequence over slow-burn world-building. Weis understands her audience; she knows they are returning to Krynn out of a deep-seated affection for its history. Consequently, she weaponizes that affection, putting beloved legacies in genuine, unshielded peril. 

The introduction of Chaos elevates the narrative from a standard political-military fantasy into a sprawling metaphysical cataclysm. It shifts the paradigm: this is no longer just a territorial war between Knights and mortals, but a struggle for the very existence of the universe. Weis handles this escalation by contrasting macro-level destruction with intimate, character-driven agony. Every spell cast, every sword drawn, and every family secret unveiled carries a devastating cost.

Dragons of Summer Flame is a bold, uncompromising turning point for the Dragonlance canon. Margaret Weis does not coddle her readers or her characters. She delivers a blistering, high-stakes narrative that proves the sins of the past cannot simply be buried—they must be confronted, fought, and ultimately survived. For veterans of the Chronicles and newcomers alike, this summer is destined to be unforgettable, scorching every expectation in its path.
