# Finder (Finder Chronicles #1)

**Author:** Suzanne Palmer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

**Finder: A Space Opera Heist with Heart and Edge**

Suzanne Palmer’s *Finder* isn’t just another sci-fi heist story—it’s a razor-sharp, fast-paced romp through the underbelly of interstellar politics, where the real treasure isn’t the stolen ship but the messy, unpredictable lives that get tangled in its wake. At its core, *Finder* is a tale of found family, moral ambiguity, and the cost of survival in a universe that rewards ruthlessness but punishes isolation. Palmer, a Hugo Award-winning author, crafts a narrative that’s equal parts swashbuckling adventure and introspective character study, blending the grit of a noir detective with the cosmic scale of classic space opera.

The protagonist, Fergus Ferguson, is a repo man with a conscience—or at least, a conscience that’s been honed by necessity. He’s the kind of antihero who’d rather be called a *finder* because it sounds less like a thief and more like a problem-solver. His latest job—a straightforward heist to reclaim a stolen starship—quickly spirals into chaos when he stumbles into a colonial war, alien threats, and a web of alliances he never asked for. What begins as a transactional mission becomes a moral quagmire, forcing Fergus to confront the fact that his skills might mean more to the people of Cernee than his own survival.

Palmer’s strength lies in her ability to balance high-stakes action with sharp, witty dialogue and deeply flawed but compelling characters. Fergus is a man who’s seen too much, trusted too little, and yet still finds himself drawn into the lives of others. His journey isn’t just about reclaiming the *Venetia’s Sword*—it’s about learning that some debts can’t be paid in credits alone. The supporting cast, from the cunning ex-nobleman Arum Gilger to the scrappy locals of Cernee, are all vividly drawn, each with their own agendas and vulnerabilities. Even the alien threat, initially dismissed as myth, looms like an ominous shadow, adding an element of existential dread to the mix.

The prose is lean, efficient, and packed with Palmer’s signature wit. Her descriptions are vivid without being overwrought, and her world-building is subtle but immersive. The far-flung colony of Cernee feels lived-in, its politics as tangled as its space mines, and the tension between human ambition and the unknown forces of the cosmos is palpable. There’s a sense of urgency in every scene, whether Fergus is dodging mercenaries or outmaneuvering a corporate warlord, but there’s also room for quiet moments that reveal the emotional weight beneath the action.

What makes *Finder* stand out is its refusal to simplify its characters or its conflicts. Fergus isn’t a hero; he’s a survivor, and his choices are rarely clean. The same goes for the factions he navigates—none are purely good or evil, and the moral gray areas force readers to question who the real villains are. This nuance elevates the story beyond a typical heist narrative, making it a thought-provoking exploration of loyalty, redemption, and the cost of belonging.

For fans of space opera, *Finder* delivers the thrills they crave—high-speed chases, clever schemes, and a touch of the supernatural—but it also offers something rarer: emotional depth. Palmer doesn’t shy away from the messiness of human relationships, and the result is a story that lingers long after the last page. It’s a book that rewards both the adrenaline junkie and the reader who enjoys a good, morally complex character.

In the end, *Finder* is a testament to Palmer’s skill as a storyteller—she knows how to make a heist feel personal, how to turn a war into a character study, and how to make the universe feel vast yet intimate. It’s a book that doesn’t just entertain; it challenges, and that’s what makes it unforgettable.
