"The Rhesus Chart, the fifth entry in Charles Stross’s Laundry Files series, thrusts Bob Howard—once a low‑level IT wizard now promoted to junior manager—into a murky blend of corporate intrigue, bureaucratic satire, and Lovecraftian horror that feels both familiar and freshly unsettling. Stross continues his trademark recipe of mixing hard‑science minutiae with eldritch mythos, turning the mundane mechanics of government paperwork into a conduit for existential dread. The novel’s central theme pivots on the tension between institutional inertia and the relentless, almost vampiric, appetite of forbidden knowledge; it asks how an organization designed to contain the uncanny can itself become a breeding ground for the very threats it swore to suppress.

At its heart, the story follows a rash of inexplicable afflictions among traders at a prestigious London merchant bank. Symptoms—superhuman strength, preternatural speed, mind‑control abilities, acute photosensitivity, and an insatiable craving for blood—mirror classic vampire lore, yet Stross grounds them in a plausible, if grotesque, biochemical mechanism tied to the titular Rhesus factor. The infection spreads like a corporate virus, exploiting the bank’s high‑frequency trading floors and the ruthless ambition of its denizens. Bob’s investigation peels back layers of obfuscation: memos buried in SharePoint, endless compliance trainings, and the ever‑present hum of Buffy reruns that serve as both comic relief and a dark mirror to the agency’s own pop‑culture desensitization.

Stross’s style shines brightest in the juxtaposition of dry, almost clinical exposition with bursts of sardonic wit. Footnotes, a hallmark of his Laundry universe, appear sparingly here, offering tantalizing glimpses into occult mathematics without derailing the narrative momentum. The prose is lean, propelled by short, punchy sentences that mimic the frantic pace of trading floors and espionage tradecraft. Yet when the narrative slips into the supernatural, Stross slows just enough to let the dread seep in: descriptions of chanting in subterranean vaults, the slick, oily sheen of blood‑soaked ledgers, and the uncanny way light seems to recoil from infected skin read like miniature horror vignettes tucked inside a techno‑thriller.

What makes The Rhesus Chart particularly compelling is its interrogation of modern capitalism as a fertile ground for eldritch exploitation. The merchant bank, a symbol of neoliberal excess, becomes a literal blood‑sucking entity; its traders, driven by quarterly bonuses and algorithmic greed, are unwitting hosts for a parasite that feeds on both vitality and ambition. Stross does not merely recycle vampire tropes; he reframes them as a metaphor for the parasitic nature of unchecked financialization, where human lives are reduced to data points and the cost of profit is measured in souls. This thematic layer elevates the novel beyond a simple monster‑hunt, inviting readers to consider how bureaucratic systems—whether governmental or corporate—can inadvertently nurture the very horrors they aim to control.

The Laundry itself, portrayed as a tangled web of obsolete protocols and inter‑departmental rivalry, functions as both antagonist and reluctant ally. Bob’s promotion forces him to navigate the agency’s Kafkaesque hierarchy, where securing a budget for field equipment requires navigating a labyrinth of forms that would make a Kafka protagonist weep. The humor arises from the absurdity of fighting eldritch horrors with staplers and memos, yet the underlying tension is genuine: every delay, every ignored warning sign, brings the infection closer to a critical mass that could spill onto London’s streets. Stross balances these tones deftly, allowing the comedy to highlight rather than undermine the stakes.

Characterization remains strong. Bob Howard retains his endearing mixture of competence and self‑deprecation, his growth evident as he learns to wield both a sigil‑etched Glock and a PowerPoint presentation with equal aplomb. Supporting figures—such as the weary occult analyst Mo, the ever‑skeptical HR liaison, and the enigmatic bank executive whose smile hides a centuries‑old pact—are rendered with enough depth to feel like genuine participants in the cosmic struggle rather than mere plot devices.

Critics might note that the novel’s reliance on financial jargon could alienate readers unfamiliar with high‑frequency trading mechanisms. However, Stross mitigates this by embedding explanations within Bob’s internal monologue, turning potentially opaque concepts into narrative tension. The pacing occasionally stalls during extended exposition of the Laundry’s internal politics, but these sections serve to reinforce the theme of bureaucratic obstruction, making the eventual payoff feel earned.

In sum, The Rhesus Chart is a deft amalgamation of satire, horror, and spy craft that showcases Charles Stross’s ability to mutate genre conventions into something freshly unsettling. Its main theme—the corrupting symbiosis between institutional lethargy and eldritch opportunism—resonates in an age where corporate scandals and governmental overreach dominate headlines. Stross’s style, marked by rigorous detail, dry humor, and a Lovecraftian undercurrent, ensures that the novel is both intellectually satisfying and viscerally thrilling. For fans of the series and newcomers alike, it offers a compelling reminder that the most dangerous monsters often wear suits, hide behind spreadsheets, and thrive in the shadows of our own making."
