# Head On (Lock In, #2)

**Author:** John Scalzi  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

Head On picks up where Lock In left off, thrusting readers back into a near‑future where Haden’s Syndrome has spawned a whole new class of “threeps”—remote‑controlled robotic bodies that let the paralyzed experience the world through steel and servo. John Scalzi wastes no time dropping us into the gritty, high‑octane arena of Hilketa, a sport that blends medieval weaponry with futuristic avatars, where the objective is as brutal as it is absurd: snatch an opponent’s head and carry it through the goalposts. The premise alone is a sparkling example of Scalzi’s gift for taking a wild speculative idea and grounding it in the visceral thrill of a procedural murder mystery. When a star athlete collapses dead on the field, FBI agents Chris Shane and Leslie Vann are called in, and the novel becomes a tightly wound investigation that peels back the glossy façade of a booming entertainment industry to reveal greed, corruption, and the unsettling question of what it means to be truly alive when your body can be swapped out at will.

The main theme that pulses through Head On is the negotiation of identity in a world where the flesh is optional. Scalzi uses Hilketa as a microcosm for larger societal debates about disability, autonomy, and the commodification of the self. The threeps are not just cool tech; they are extensions of the users’ will, yet they also become objects that can be owned, rented, sabotaged, and even destroyed. As Shane and Vann interview players, owners, sponsors, and fanatics, the narrative constantly asks: Who is responsible when a threep malfunctions? Where does personal agency end and corporate liability begin? The sport’s violent spectacle forces characters—and readers—to confront the uncomfortable thrill of watching bodies (even synthetic ones) suffer for entertainment, echoing contemporary concerns about extreme sports, esports, and the ethics of immersive VR. Scalzi never lets the philosophical weight become a lecture; instead, he weaves it into snappy interrogations, tense stakeouts, and the dry wit that has become his trademark.

Speaking of trademark, Scalzi’s style shines brightest in the dialogue. Chris Shane’s first‑person narration remains as crisp and self‑aware as ever, balancing the gravity of FBI protocol with a laid‑back, almost colloquial ease that makes the heavy world‑building feel accessible. Leslie Vann serves as the perfect foil—her Hard‑nosed pragmatism and sharp humor create a buddy‑cop dynamic that feels both familiar and fresh. Their exchanges crackle with the kind of rapid‑fire banter that would sit comfortably alongside a classic police procedural, yet each line is laced with sci‑fi nuance: a threep’s servomotors whine in the background of a tense stakeout, a sponsor’s logo flickers on a visor during a heated argument, and the omnipresent hum of the Haden network underscores every scene. Scalzi’s prose is economical; he never lingers on description for its own sake, instead using precise, evocative details to paint a world that feels lived‑in and immediate. The result is a narrative that moves at the breakneck speed of a Hilketa match while still allowing moments of quiet reflection on the human cost of technological advancement.

What truly elevates Head On beyond a simple genre mash‑up is Scalzi’s commitment to grounding the fantastical in emotional truth. The murder investigation is compelling, but the novel’s heart beats in the quiet moments—Shane’s thoughts about his own threep, Vann’s memories of life before the syndrome, the tentative relationships forming between players who share more than just a digital avatar. These human (or, perhaps, post‑human) beats remind us that no matter how sophisticated the gear, the core drives—loyalty, ambition, fear, love—remain unchanged. Scalzi’s ability to marry high concept speculation with intimate character study is what makes his work resonate across readers who crave both brain‑twisting ideas and gut‑punch storytelling.

In sum, Head On delivers a chilling, thought‑provoking ride through a future where sport, spectacle, and sovereignty collide. It showcases Scalzi’s trademark blend of razor‑sharp wit, breakneck pacing, and profound sociological inquiry, all wrapped in a mystery that keeps you guessing until the final whistle. Whether you’re a fan of Lock In, a lover of hard‑boiled procedurals, or simply intrigued by the ethical puzzles of emerging tech, this novel offers a satisfying punch of adrenaline and intellect that lingers long after the last page.
