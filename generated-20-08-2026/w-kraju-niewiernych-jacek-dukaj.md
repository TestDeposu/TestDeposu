# W kraju niewiernych

**Author:** Jacek Dukaj  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

Jacek Dukaj is a titan of Polish speculative fiction, a writer who refuses to treat world-building as mere background dressing and instead uses it as a crucible for philosophical and metaphysical inquiry. W kraju niewiernych (In the Land of the Infidels), a monumental collection of short stories, captures the author at his intellectual peak. It serves as a dazzling kaleidoscope of visionary futures where technology, theology, and human consciousness violently collide.

From the very first page, Dukaj drops the reader into labyrinthine realities without a safety net. The collection features iconic pieces such as Katedra (The Cathedral)—the literary foundation for Tomasz Bagiński’s Oscar-nominated animated masterpiece—alongside Ruch Generała, Irrehaare, and the provocative Ziemia Chrystusa. Each story functions as a self-contained universe governed by its own unforgiving laws, yet all share a common DNA: an unyielding ambition to push the boundaries of science fiction beyond traditional genre tropes.

What distinguishes Dukaj’s prose is his astonishing fecundity of imagination. As critic Wojciech Orliński aptly notes, Dukaj casually conjures up rich, complex worlds—complete with their own socio-political structures, physics, and historical weights—only to abandon them after a few dozen pages, as if casually proving that the well of speculative thought is bottomless. Readers encounter Iron Generals, Blood Eaters, virtual realities anticipating the phenomenon of The Matrix, and alternate histories where Christ did not die on the cross. Yet, underneath the glittering surface of techno-thriller elements and cosmic vistas lies a deeper, more unsettling investigation.

As Marek Oramus points out, the true core of Dukaj’s work is how radically altered physical and technological realities force a painful reformation of our relationship with the divine. In Dukaj’s universes, humanity’s latest creations inevitably deform and transcend their creators, introducing novel forms of suffering, existential dread, and religious ecstasy. Despite the title, one might argue these stories actually take place in the land of the faithful—or at least the deeply obsessed—where faith and metaphysics are stress-tested against the cold mechanics of tomorrow.

W kraju niewiernych is not comfortable reading. It demands intellectual stamina, challenging the audience to digest dense concepts, unfamiliar terminology, and staggering paradigm shifts. Yet, the reward is an intoxicating sense of literary expansion. Dukaj proves that science fiction can still be literature of the highest order: bold, provocative, and utterly unforgettable. For anyone seeking to understand the zenith of modern Polish speculative fiction, this collection is an absolute mandatory pilgrimage.
