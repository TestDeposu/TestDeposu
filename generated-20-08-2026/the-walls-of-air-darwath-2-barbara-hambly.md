# The Walls of Air (Darwath, #2)

**Author:** Barbara Hambly  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

The Walls of Air, the second installment of Barbara Hambly’s Darwath trilogy, thrusts readers back into a world where the thin veil between ordinary life and the encroaching Dark is about to tear completely. Having fled the nightmare of the Void with a fragile band of survivors, Gil—now a hardened warrior—and Rudy—reluctant wizard‑in‑training—have carved out a precarious refuge in a distant keep. Yet Hambly wastes no time in reminding us that safety is an illusion; the Dark is patient, relentless, and now it has found a way to follow them across the realms.

At its core, the novel explores the theme of **resilience in the face of inevitable decay**. Hambly refrains from offering easy triumphs; instead, she shows how hope is forged not in the absence of fear but through the stubborn willingness to act despite it. Gil’s transformation from a carefree motorcyclist to a steadfast protector mirrors Rudy’s painful apprenticeship—each step forward is bought with loss, doubt, and the quiet, grinding cost of leadership. The author’s prose mirrors this tension: crisp, almost journalistic in its clarity when describing battle or travel, then slipping into lyrical, almost poetic passages when the characters confront the intangible horrors of the Dark. This stylistic duality makes the reader feel the physical exhaustion of a forced march and the metaphysical dread of an unseen, consuming force in the same breath.

Hambly’s strength lies in her ability to ground epic stakes in deeply human moments. A shared smile over a meager meal, a whispered confession of fear, the weight of a child’s innocent trust—these details anchor the sprawling conflict and prevent the narrative from becoming a mere tableau of swords and sorcery. The world‑building, while familiar to fans of high fantasy, feels lived‑in: the keep’s stone corridors echo with the ghosts of previous defenses, the surrounding wilderness whispers with old magic, and the Dark itself is less a monolithic villain than a creeping corruption that seeps into motives, memories, and even the characters’ sense of self.

What separates The Walls of Air from many middle‑book entries is its unwillingness to stall. Hambly uses the sequel as a crucible, testing the protagonists’ ideologies and pushing them toward choices that will reverberate into the trilogy’s finale. The pacing is relentless—scenes of frantic flight are interspersed with quiet, introspective interludes that allow the reader to catch breath before the next onslaught. This rhythm mirrors the characters’ own oscillation between action and reflection, making the narrative feel both urgent and emotionally resonant.

In sum, Barbara Hambly delivers a tense, nerve‑wracking continuation that balances visceral action with profound emotional depth. Her style—sharp when needed, lyrical when the moment calls—serves a story about how ordinary people cling to humanity when the world threatens to erase it. The Walls of Air is not merely a bridge between books; it is a stand‑alone testament to endurance, sacrifice, and the flicker of light that refuses to be snuffed out by the Dark.
