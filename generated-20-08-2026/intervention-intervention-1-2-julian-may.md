# Intervention (Intervention #1-2)

**Author:** Julian May  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1987

**A Quantum Leap in Humanity: Julian May’s *Intervention*—Where the Mind Becomes the Battleground**

Julian May’s *Intervention* is not merely a story about the emergence of superhuman minds—it is a seismic shift in the very fabric of human evolution. Set against the backdrop of Cold War paranoia, nuclear brinkmanship, and the quiet desperation of a world teetering on the edge of annihilation, this novel asks a question that lingers long after the final page: *What happens when humanity’s greatest asset becomes its most dangerous weapon?* With the precision of a scientist and the grandeur of a mythmaker, May crafts a world where the boundaries between genius and monstrosity, salvation and tyranny, blur into something both thrilling and unsettling.

At its core, *Intervention* is an origin myth—a prelude to May’s sprawling *Saga of Pliocene Exile*, but a work in its own right, a sweeping epic that spans nearly seven decades. The story begins in 1945, a time when the world is still reeling from the horrors of atomic warfare, and ends in 2013, a future where the very nature of human cognition has been irrevocably altered. The catalyst? A sudden, unexplained surge in metapsychic abilities—telepathy, precognition, mind-over-matter—emerging in individuals scattered across the globe. Some are born with the power to bend reality; others are cursed with it. The result is a society both fascinated and horrified by these "operants," who are alternately worshipped as saviors and hunted as abominations.

May’s genius lies in her ability to ground this fantastical premise in the gritty realities of human nature. The Remillard family, the novel’s anchor, serves as a microcosm of the broader struggle. Their descendants will later feature prominently in *Pliocene Exile*, but here, they embody the conflicted soul of the metapsychic operant: torn between the desire to protect humanity from its own folly and the fear of becoming the very thing they seek to prevent. The novel follows their journey—from the isolation of their early discoveries to the public revelation of their powers, and finally to the brutal confrontation with a world that sees them as either gods or demons. Along the way, May weaves in themes of exploitation, fear, and the ethical dilemmas of power, all while maintaining a relentless pace that keeps the reader hooked.

What makes *Intervention* so compelling is its refusal to romanticize the metapsychic operants. May presents them as flawed, often terrified individuals, their abilities as much a curse as a gift. The novel’s early chapters are steeped in tension as the Remillards grapple with the isolation of their condition, their relationships strained by the weight of their newfound powers. But as the story progresses, the stakes escalate dramatically. Governments, corporations, and rogue factions all vie for control of these operants, turning them into pawns in a game far larger than themselves. The result is a narrative that feels both intimate and epic, a personal tragedy played out against the backdrop of global catastrophe.

May’s writing is a masterclass in balancing scientific plausibility with speculative grandeur. She grounds her metapsychic abilities in what could be plausible evolutionary leaps—sudden mutations, genetic predispositions, or even environmental triggers—rather than relying on outright magic. This approach makes the story feel less like fantasy and more like a cautionary tale about the unintended consequences of pushing human potential to its limits. The dialogue crackles with wit and urgency, the action sequences are visceral, and the world-building is meticulous, from the political intrigues of the Cold War to the scientific debates over the nature of consciousness.

Yet, for all its intellectual rigor, *Intervention* never loses sight of its emotional core. The novel’s strength lies in its characters—ordinary people thrust into extraordinary circumstances, their humanity intact even as they wield powers that could reshape the world. The Remillards, in particular, are deeply relatable, their struggles with trust, fear, and identity resonating long after the final revelation. Their journey is one of self-discovery, but also of confrontation: with their own fears, with the world’s prejudices, and with the moral weight of their choices.

By the time the novel reaches its climax, the question of whether the metapsychic operants will save humanity or doom it hangs in the balance. May leaves the answer ambiguous, refusing to offer easy resolutions. Instead, she presents a world where the line between hero and villain is as fluid as the minds that bend reality. This ambiguity is what makes *Intervention* so enduring—a story that lingers not just for its spectacle, but for its profound questions about what it means to be human in an age of limitless potential.

In many ways, *Intervention* is a novel ahead of its time. Published in 1987, it anticipates modern anxieties about AI, genetic engineering, and the ethical implications of human enhancement. Yet, it is also timeless, a meditation on power, fear, and the cost of progress. May’s vision is both expansive and intimate, a testament to her ability to blend hard science with deep emotional resonance. For readers who crave stories that challenge the boundaries of possibility while staying rooted in the complexities of human experience, *Intervention* is a must-read—a quantum leap in speculative fiction that redefines what it means to be extraordinary.
