# The Walking Dead, Vol. 2: Miles Behind Us

**Author:** Robert Kirkman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2004

**The Walking Dead, Vol. 2: *Miles Behind Us*—A Masterclass in Survival and Humanity’s Fragility**

Robert Kirkman’s *The Walking Dead* has always been more than a zombie story—it’s a brutal, unflinching exploration of what happens when civilization collapses, and the raw, unvarnished truths of human nature emerge. *Vol. 2: Miles Behind Us*, collecting issues #7-#12, deepens this narrative with a relentless pace, sharp character development, and a haunting sense of isolation. Here, the survivors’ journey isn’t just about avoiding the dead; it’s about confronting the living—each other—and the terrifying realization that trust is the most fragile resource in a world where every stranger could be a threat.

At its core, this volume is about the slow unraveling of order. Rick Grimes, still haunted by the man he shot in the first issue, is no longer the calm, level-headed hero of the early comics. He’s exhausted, desperate, and increasingly forced to make choices that blur the line between survival and morality. His reunion with his family—Lori, Carl, and later, Maggie—is bittersweet, a fragile hope in a world that keeps pulling them apart. But Kirkman doesn’t let them rest. The farm owned by Hershel Greene, a man who’s spent years preparing for disaster, offers a temporary sanctuary, but even here, paranoia festers. Hershel’s caution isn’t just practical; it’s a mirror to the group’s growing fractures. Who can they trust? How far will they go to protect themselves?

Kirkman’s writing is unapologetically bleak, but it’s also deeply human. He doesn’t glorify violence or survival—he exposes its cost. The introduction of new characters like the volatile Shane and the enigmatic Hershel forces the group to confront their own biases. Shane, in particular, becomes a dark reflection of Rick, a man who believes the ends justify the means. Their dynamic is electric, charged with tension that simmers just below the surface, setting up one of the series’ most explosive conflicts. Meanwhile, Hershel’s farm, with its barricades and hidden supplies, becomes a symbol of the new world’s precarious balance—security vs. freedom, isolation vs. community.

The art, by Tony Moore and later Charlie Adlard, complements this tone perfectly. Moore’s detailed, almost painterly style captures the grit of the world, from the decaying streets to the desperate faces of the survivors. The action is visceral, the horror palpable, but never gratuitous. The dead aren’t just monsters; they’re a constant, inescapable presence, a reminder that the real threat isn’t just the walkers—it’s the chaos they unleash.

What makes *Miles Behind Us* so compelling is how it balances hope with despair. The survivors find moments of warmth—Carl’s innocence, Maggie’s resilience, the quiet moments between Rick and Lori—but these are always undercut by the looming threat of violence. Kirkman doesn’t offer easy answers. There’s no grand plan, no clear hero. Just people, struggling, making mistakes, and sometimes, tragically, failing each other.

By the end of this volume, the group is more fractured than ever. The farm is no longer safe. The survivors are more divided. And the question lingers: Can they ever truly trust one another again? Kirkman doesn’t rush the story. He lets the tension breathe, letting the reader feel the weight of every decision, every betrayal, every small mercy. That’s the genius of *The Walking Dead*—it’s not just about the dead; it’s about the living, and the terrible things they’re capable of when the world falls apart.

If you’ve read the first volume and found yourself hooked by the raw, unflinching storytelling, *Miles Behind Us* delivers even more. It’s a masterclass in character-driven horror, where the real monsters aren’t the walkers—it’s the darkness that emerges in the hearts of men when they’re forced to survive.
