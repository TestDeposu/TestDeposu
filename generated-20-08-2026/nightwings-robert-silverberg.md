# Nightwings

**Author:** Robert Silverberg  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Nightwings is a haunting meditation on what it means to endure when the sky itself becomes a threat. Robert Silverberg opens the novel with a simple, almost mythic proclamation—“Roum is a city built on seven hills”—and from that first line he pulls the reader into a far‑future Earth where oceans have swallowed continents, societies have fractured into guilds, and the ever‑watchful eyes of the Watchers scan the heavens for the return of alien invaders. The premise is familiar enough to feel comforting, yet Silverberg twists it into something uniquely his own: a world where survival hinges not on firepower alone, but on the fragile act of remembering.

At its core, Nightwings explores the tension between observation and memory. The protagonist, a Watcher named only by his guild, is trained to scan the stars for danger, to be the first line of defense. Yet his companion, Avluela the Flier, embodies a different kind of vigilance—one that looks inward, toward the stories and histories stored in the Hall of the Rememberers. When a moment of distraction allows the alien “star people” to breach the watch, the novel shifts from a taut, almost procedural chase to a pilgrimage of reclamation. The Watcher’s solitary journey to Roum becomes a quest not just for a physical stronghold, but for the collective memory that might give humanity a reason to fight back. Silverberg uses this structure to ask a quiet but pressing question: What good is warning the world if no one remembers why the warning matters?

The author’s style is where the novel truly shines. Silverberg’s prose is lean yet lyrical, each sentence calibrated to evoke a stark, wind‑scoured landscape while also resonating with mythic undertones. He avoids exhaustive exposition; instead, he lets the world reveal itself through the protagonist’s senses—the taste of dust on the tongue, the distant hum of alien engines, the echo of ancient bells in Roum’s hollowed hills. This technique creates an immersive immediacy that makes the far‑future feel both alien and eerily familiar. His dialogue is sparse but purposeful, often carrying the weight of cultural lore in a few well‑placed phrases, a hallmark of Silverberg’s ability to blend science fiction with the timelessness of myth.

What distinguishes Nightwings from many of its contemporaries is its refusal to glorify technology or militarism as the salvation of humanity. Instead, the novel suggests that salvation lies in the preservation of narrative— in the act of remembering who we were, what we valued, and why we chose to watch the skies in the first place. The Hall of the Rememberers, half‑ruined and half‑revered, becomes a symbol of cultural continuity, a repository that could either inspire a new beginning or become a mausoleum if ignored. Silverberg’s treatment of memory as both a weapon and a burden adds a philosophical depth that elevates the story beyond a simple survival tale.

Readers who appreciate the world‑building of Greg Bear or the anthropological nuance of Ursula K. Le Guin will find much to admire here. Silverberg’s skillful balance of action, introspection, and lyrical description makes Nightwings a compact yet powerful read—one that lingers long after the final page, prompting reflection on how societies confront existential threats and what they choose to carry forward.

In sum, Nightwings is an evocative, tightly crafted novella that uses a classic alien‑invasion premise to explore the enduring power of memory and the quiet heroism of those who remember. It is a testament to Silverberg’s enduring talent for marrying speculative imagination with profound human insight.
