# 10 Blind Dates

**Author:** Ashley Elston  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

Ashley Elston’s 10 Blind Dates reads like a holiday mixtape—sweet, a little bittersweet, and impossible to put down once the first track starts. Set against the humid, magnolia‑scented backdrop of South Louisiana during Christmas break, the novel follows Sophie, a high‑school senior whose plans for a quiet, make‑out‑filled vacation with longtime boyfriend Griffin implode when he announces he wants space. Seeking refuge at her grandparents’ house, Sophie is thrust into a whirlwind orchestrated by her boisterous extended family: ten blind dates, each arranged by a different relative, over the next ten days. What begins as a punitive scheme to distract a heartbroken teen evolves into a candid exploration of freedom, expectation, and the messy process of figuring out who you are when the people who know you best keep trying to rewrite your story.

At its core, the book tackles the tension between parental protection and personal autonomy—a theme that resonates strongly with young‑adult readers navigating the bridge between adolescence and adulthood. Sophie’s overprotective parents, embodied by her mother’s incessant checking‑in and her father’s quiet disapproval of any risk, represent the safety net that can feel suffocating when one craves independence. The blind‑date gambit, devised by her well‑meaning but meddlesome Nonna, serves as both a literal and figurative device: each date forces Sophie to step outside her comfort zone, confront unfamiliar social scripts, and, most importantly, make choices that are hers alone. Through these encounters—ranging from a sultry underground party set up by her sweet cousin Sara to a ludicrous living nativity staged by her outrageous Aunt Patrice, complete with a toddler‑sized shepherd and a wailing baby—Elston uses humor to highlight how societal and familial expectations can be both absurd and oddly enlightening.

The holiday setting is more than mere window dressing; it amplifies the stakes of Sophie’s internal conflict. Christmas, traditionally a time of family unity and nostalgic ritual, becomes a mirror reflecting Sophie’s feeling of being trapped within a script she did not write. The twinkling lights, the scent of gingerbread, and the incessant holiday music underscore the contrast between the external merriment and her internal dislocation. Yet, as the days progress, the same festive atmosphere also offers moments of genuine connection—shared laughter over burnt cookies, quiet conversations on a porch swing, and the unexpected warmth of cousins who understand her plight without needing to fix it. Elston deftly balances the saccharine with the sincere, ensuring that the holiday cheer never feels forced or superficial.

Stylistically, Elston’s prose is lively and accessible, marked by a brisk pacing that mirrors the countdown structure of the plot. Sophie’s first‑person narration is candid and self‑aware, peppered with witty asides that keep the tone light even when the emotional undercurrents run deep. Dialogue crackles with regional flavor—Louisiana idioms, playful teasing, and the occasional French‑Cajun expression—giving the story an authentic sense of place without alienating readers unfamiliar with the culture. The author’s knack for snappy, comedic set‑pieces (the underground party, the nativity fiasco, a chaotic family game night) showcases her talent for situational humor, while quieter, introspective scenes reveal a softer, more nuanced side of Sophie’s voice. This duality prevents the novel from tipping into pure farce; instead, it grounds the comedy in genuine emotional growth.

One of the book’s strengths lies in its ensemble cast. Each family member who sets up a date is distinct—not merely a plot device but a fully realized personality whose own quirks and motivations subtly influence Sophie’s perception of love and loyalty. Aunt Patrice’s flamboyance, Cousin Sara’s earnestness, and even the gruff yet caring grandfather provide a mosaic of perspectives that enrich the central romance triangle. When Griffin reappears, begging for a second chance, the narrative does not simply revert to a tidy reconciliation; instead, Sophie’s renewed self‑knowledge forces her to weigh what she truly wants against what is familiar and safe. The ensuing confusion is palpable, and Elston handles it with a deft hand, allowing the protagonist’s indecision to feel realistic rather than frustrating.

If there is a criticism to be made, it is that the ten‑date framework occasionally strains credulity—some of the setups feel contrived for the sake of variety rather than organic character development. A few of the dates blur together in their comedic excess, and the rapid succession can leave readers yearning for a bit more depth in certain interactions. However, these moments are relatively minor in the grand scheme; the novel’s brisk tempo and festive charm largely compensate for any structural hiccups.
