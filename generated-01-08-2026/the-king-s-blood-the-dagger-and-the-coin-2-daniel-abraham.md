# The King's Blood (The Dagger and the Coin, #2)

**Author:** Daniel Abraham  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

Daniel Abraham’s *The King's Blood*, the second installment in *The Dagger and the Coin* series, proves that epic fantasy does not need to rely on screaming prophecies and chosen ones to be utterly mesmerizing. Published in 2012, this middle volume deepens the geopolitical chess match and moral complexities established in *The Dragon’s Path*, shifting its gaze from the initial tremors of upheaval to the full, devastating weight of war and institutional corruption. 

The central theme of Abraham’s work is the terrifying banality of evil and how grand historical shifts are forged by personal ambition, economic leverage, and cognitive dissonance. Unlike traditional fantasy where heroes are defined by martial prowess or magical destiny, Abraham examines the mechanics of power. Geder Palliako perfectly embodies the novel’s exploration of tragic descent. Once a sympathetic, bookish misfit, Geder rises to become a national hero and protector of the crown prince in Antea. Yet, Abraham brilliantly illustrates how insecurity and a desperate need for belonging can curdle into monstrous tyranny. Geder does not see himself as a villain; he is simply doing his duty, blinding himself to the atrocities he sanctions. This chilling psychological realism is a masterclass in character development, showing how ordinary men can become the architects of ruin.

Parallel to Geder’s dark ascent is the journey of Cithrin bel Sarcour, whose narrative serves as a brilliant critique of early capitalism and institutional control. Having built her reputation on financial audacity and forged documents, Cithrin finds herself trapped in a gilded cage where every calculated move is scrutinized by powerful banking interests. Abraham uses her story to highlight a core philosophical truth of the series: blood and steel may conquer nations, but gold owns them. When war finally erupts, Cithrin must navigate the treacherous waters of corporate survival, proving that economic warfare can be just as ruthless as any battlefield.

Complementing these grounded perspectives is the broader canvas of a world sliding toward madness. Abraham weaves in elements of ancient secrets and forgotten dragon lore, but these fantastical elements never overshadow the human cost. The looming apocalypse feels heavy precisely because it is driven by human flaws—greed, fear, and pride—rather than a faceless dark lord. 

Abraham’s literary style is the true anchor of the novel. Eschewing the flowery, excessive prose often associated with epic fantasy, his writing is clean, deliberate, and deeply psychological. He prioritizes quiet, tension-filled conversations in drafty council rooms and counting houses over bombastic action sequences, making the eventual violence all the more impactful. Every character, from the main protagonists to the supporting figures, speaks with a distinct, authentic voice that breathes life into the world.

*The King’s Blood* is a sophisticated, sobering, and utterly gripping continuation of a modern fantasy masterwork. It challenges the reader to look past the romanticized myths of war and examine the gears of commerce and ambition that truly turn the world. For fans of intelligent, character-driven fantasy that respects its audience's intellect, Abraham’s latest chapter is an absolute triumph.
