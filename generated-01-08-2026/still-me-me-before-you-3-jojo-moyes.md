# Still Me (Me Before You, #3)

**Author:** Jojo Moyes  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

Stepping back into the world of Louisa Clark feels much like reuniting with an old, eccentric friend whose wardrobe is as vibrant as her resilient spirit. In Still Me, the third installment of Jojo Moyes’s immensely popular series that began with the cultural phenomenon Me Before You, our heroine trades the familiar drizzle of rural Britain for the glittering, high-stakes glamour of New York City. Yet, beneath the polished veneer of Fifth Avenue high society and the demands of her impossibly wealthy employers, Moyes anchors the narrative in a deeply human exploration of identity, reinvention, and the daunting courage it takes to truly discover who you are when everyone expects you to remain the same.

Moyes has always possessed a distinct literary style that effortlessly bridges the gap between commercial women’s fiction and profound emotional drama. Her writing is characterized by an acute observational wit, snappy dialogue, and a rare empathy for the beautifully flawed. In Still Me, this style shines through Louisa’s unmistakable narrative voice. Lou is a protagonist who narrates her own life with self-deprecating humor, boundless warmth, and a fierce, albeit frequently tested, optimism. Moyes avoids the trap of making Lou a static symbol of perseverance; instead, she portrays her as a work in progress—someone stumbling through grief, ambition, and imposter syndrome in a city that demands perfection. 

The central theme of the novel pivots on the friction between belonging and becoming. Having previously been defined by her caregiving role for the late Will Traynor, and later by her paralyzed grief in the aftermath, Lou arrives in New York determined to finally live a life of her own making. The Manhattan backdrop serves as a brilliant thematic mirror. Just as the city is a chaotic blend of historical grit and towering aspiration, Lou’s internal landscape is torn between the comforting gravity of her working-class British roots and the intoxicating, terrifying potential of a global future. Moyes uses this physical and cultural displacement to ask a poignant question: When you have survived a life-altering heartbreak, how do you rebuild a self that isn't entirely built around the people who need you?

While the plot occasionally leans into the predictable cadence of romantic comedy tropes—complete with mistaken identities, glamorous galas, and long-distance relationship strains—Moyes elevates the material through her refusal to offer easy resolutions. The romance subplots are secondary to Lou’s primary journey of self-actualization. The author understands that true growth is rarely linear; it involves messy missteps, professional failures, and the terrifying realization that outgrowing your old life often means leaving people behind, even when it hurts. 

Still Me serves as a triumphant closing chapter to a trilogy that captured millions of hearts, not because it wraps every loose end in a neat bow, but because it honors the messy, ongoing work of living. Jojo Moyes delivers a poignant, witty, and ultimately uplifting critique of modern womanhood, reminding us that no matter how far we travel or how drastically our circumstances change, the hardest and most important journey is always the one back to ourselves.
