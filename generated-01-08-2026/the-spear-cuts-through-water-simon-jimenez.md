# The Spear Cuts Through Water

**Author:** Simon Jimenez  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

Myth-making in the modern literary landscape is a delicate art, too often suffocated by the heavy armor of world-building tropes and predictable magic systems. Yet, with his 2022 sophomore novel, The Spear Cuts Through Water, Simon Jimenez does not merely construct a fantasy world; he breathes volatile, bleeding life into one. Winner of the Crawford and British Fantasy Awards, this is a ferocious epic that strips away the sterile distance of traditional high fantasy and thrusts the reader directly into the pulse, sweat, and ash of a dying land. 

The premise is deceptively classical yet instantly subversive. The Moon Throne, a dynasty of tyrannical emperors and their monstrous offspring known as the Three Terrors, maintains its iron grip on a suffering populace by draining the power of an imprisoned, ancient god. When this deity finally escapes into the world, she is aided by two unlikely mortals: Jun, a disgraced guard burdened by a haunted past, and Keema, a fierce outcast fighting for a future that seems perpetually out of reach. Together, they embark on a desperate, five-day pilgrimage across a broken landscape, pursued by the very children the god bore—gods hunting god, mortals protecting the divine, all while the shadow of the Moon Throne looms heavily behind them.

Yet, to reduce Jimenez’s novel to its plot is to miss the staggering ambition of its execution. The beating heart of The Spear Cuts Through Water lies in its profound exploration of legacy, identity, and the heavy, generational cost of belonging. The Moon Throne is not just a political regime; it is a tumor of institutionalized cruelty that infects the earth, the water, and the minds of the people. Jimenez uses this backdrop to interrogate how power is inherited and weaponized, asking what remains of a culture when its myths are forged in chains. The relationship between Jun and Keema evolves against this bleak horizon into something remarkably tender—a testament to human resilience and queer love finding root in the most inhospitable soil. 

It is, however, Jimenez’s singular narrative style that truly elevates the book into a masterclass of modern speculative fiction. The prose is nothing short of hypnotic, operating with a cadence that feels closer to oral storytelling, folklore, and theatrical tragedy than standard genre fiction. Jimenez employs a shifting, kaleidoscopic perspective that dips seamlessly between the omniscient whispers of the cosmos and the desperate, granular reality of individuals on the road. The narrative voice frequently breaks the fourth wall, inviting the reader to sit by the fire and bear witness to a tragedy unfolding in real-time. This oral-tradition framing device creates an intimacy that is both startling and immersive; you do not merely read about the Spear cutting through water—you feel the cold spray and hear the echoes of a chorus lamenting a doomed world.

The action sequences are painted in visceral, operatic strokes, blending visceral horror with breathtaking beauty. Jimenez balances the grand scale of mythic confrontation with the quiet, devastating grief of ordinary people caught in the machinery of empire. Every page vibrates with sensory detail—the smell of copper and damp earth, the blinding glare of a false moon, the agonizing weight of survival. 

The Spear Cuts Through Water is a triumphant achievement that challenges the boundaries of epic fantasy. It is brutal, poetic, fiercely political, and deeply human all at once. Simon Jimenez has crafted a modern myth that demands to be read aloud, remembered, and passed down. For those weary of the predictable and hungry for a novel that will utterly transform their understanding of what fantasy can achieve, this pilgrimage is an absolute must.
