# Watchmen #1: At Midnight, All The Agents…

**Author:** Alan Moore  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

**Alan Moore’s *Watchmen* #1: *At Midnight, All the Agents…*—A Masterpiece of Moral Chaos and Cosmic Despair**

Alan Moore’s *Watchmen* is not just a comic—it is a philosophical dissection of power, morality, and the fragility of human belief in a world where gods walk among us. The 2013 reissue of the final volume, *At Midnight, All the Agents…*, is a haunting, intellectually rigorous conclusion to one of the most influential graphic narratives ever created. Here, Moore and artist Dave Gibbons—now with the benefit of hindsight and the weight of their own legacies—deliver a story that is as unsettling as it is brilliant, a tale where the cost of justice is measured in blood, betrayal, and the slow unraveling of the human soul.

At its core, *Watchmen* is a meditation on the illusion of heroism. The original series, published in 1986, shattered the superhero genre by exposing its hypocrisies: the vigilantes who claim to fight for truth and justice are often as corrupt as the criminals they hunt. In *At Midnight, All the Agents…*, Moore deepens this critique, exploring how even the most idealistic among them—like the idealistic but flawed Adrian Veidt (Ozymandias)—are complicit in systems they never fully understood. The final arc forces its characters to confront the fact that their battles were never truly about saving the world, but about saving *themselves*—their egos, their delusions of grandeur, their need to believe in a higher purpose when none may exist.

The narrative structure is a masterclass in tension. Moore weaves together threads of time, fate, and alternate realities, revealing how the choices of the past—even the smallest, most seemingly insignificant ones—ripple into the present. The Doomsday Clock, a metaphor for humanity’s self-destructive tendencies, ticks down not just to war, but to existential collapse. The final act is a brutal reckoning: the world is saved, but at what cost? The survivors are left hollow, their victories pyrrhic. Moore refuses easy resolutions, instead offering a world where the only certainty is chaos—and even that is a construct of human fear.

Moore’s prose is dense, layered with references to philosophy, history, and mythology. His dialogue crackles with wit and cynicism, but beneath the surface lies a profound sadness. He doesn’t just write about superheroes; he writes about the human condition, about the ways we mythologize ourselves into gods and then watch as our own hands destroy the altars we built. The final issue is particularly devastating because it forces the reader to sit with the consequences of their own investment in the story. By the end, you’re not just left with a sense of closure—you’re left with the gnawing realization that the world *Watchmen* depicts is one we’ve already lived in, just with different costumes.

Dave Gibbons’ art remains a cornerstone of the series, his detailed, almost painterly panels giving the story a weight that feels both timeless and urgently modern. The final issue is a visual tour de force, with sequences that are as visually stunning as they are emotionally devastating. The contrast between the grandeur of Gibbons’ compositions and the bleakness of Moore’s themes creates a dissonance that lingers long after the last page.

For fans of *Watchmen*, this reissue is a must-read—not just for the story’s conclusion, but for the way it recontextualizes everything that came before. For newcomers, it’s a gateway into one of the most important works of 20th-century comics, a story that asks uncomfortable questions about heroism, morality, and the stories we tell ourselves to make sense of a broken world.

In the end, *Watchmen* is not just a comic; it’s a warning. And in an era where the line between myth and reality is thinner than ever, its lessons feel more urgent than ever before.
