# The Interestings

**Author:** Meg Wolitzer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

The summer of Nixon’s resignation, six teenagers converge at an arts camp called Spirit in the Woods, bonded instantly by a collective, glowing moniker: The Interestings. They are teenagers armed with raw, idiosyncratic talent, convinced that their gifts will not only set them apart from the mundane world, but safely charter their futures. But as Meg Wolitzer demonstrates in her brilliant 2013 novel, adolescence is a distorted mirror. It reflects potential, not destiny. 

Wolitzer’s central, aching theme is the brutal roulette wheel of adult life, specifically how talent, ambition, and artistic validation diverge wildly once the protective bubble of youth bursts. The novel tracks these characters across decades, observing the quiet tragedies and unexpected triumphs of middle age. Jules Jacobson arrives at camp an aspiring comic actress, only to eventually trade her stage dreams for a practical, ordinary existence. Jonah, a startlingly gifted musician, eventually abandons his guitar entirely to become an engineer. Yet, against this backdrop of deferred dreams, their peers Ethan and Ash—now married—achieve staggering, wealthy success, their early artistic promise ballooning into a life of expansive privilege and access.

What makes The Interestings a towering achievement is not merely its scope, but Wolitzer’s surgical yet deeply empathetic examination of envy. Envy is rarely a loud, cinematic villain in this book; instead, it is a persistent, low-grade fever that settles into the cracks of long-term friendships. Jules loves Ash and Ethan, yet she must constantly navigate the invisible chasm created by their wealth and continued creative fulfillment. Wolitzer captures the specific, adult humiliation of realizing that the people who knew you when you were formless have outpaced you, forcing you to recalibrate your self-worth against their gleaming realities. 

Stylistically, Wolitzer writes with a luminous precision that places her firmly in the upper echelon of contemporary literature. She possesses a rare balance: she is as intellectually ambitious as her male contemporaries often praised for sweeping cultural critiques, yet her prose is anchored by an acute emotional intelligence. She understands that the small, agonizing, and tender moments of human interaction often reveal more about our societal structures and inner lives than grand thematic gestures. Her sentences hum with observation, rendering the awkwardness of teenage yearning and the quiet resignations of middle age with equal grace. 

Ultimately, The Interestings transcends the traditional coming-of-age narrative to become a profound meditation on survival, class, and the heavy burden of expectation. It asks the uncomfortable question that haunts every creative soul: What happens when the spotlight moves on, and you are left in the dark, tasked with building a meaningful life out of the remnants of a discarded dream? Wolitzer answers not with cynicism, but with a deeply moving humanity, making this a modern classic that speaks directly to anyone who has ever looked at a friend's success and wondered, Why them and not me?
