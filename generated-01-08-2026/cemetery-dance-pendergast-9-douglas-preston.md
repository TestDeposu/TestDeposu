# Cemetery Dance (Pendergast, #9)

**Author:** Douglas Preston  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Dead men do not usually walk the streets of Manhattan, let alone leave trails of butchery in their wake. Yet, in Cemetery Dance, the ninth installment of Douglas Preston and Lincoln Child’s wildly popular Special Agent Pendergast series, the impossible becomes terrifyingly routine. Published in 2009, this breathless thriller delivers a potent mix of police procedural, Gothic horror, and occult mystery. While the synopsis provides the bare bones of the plot—the tragic assault on journalist William Smithback and archaeologist Nora Kelly by a neighbor who was supposedly dead and buried—it only scratches the surface of the psychological dread and atmospheric brilliance that define the authors' signature style.

At its core, the main theme of Cemetery Dance is the terrifying permeability of the modern world to ancient, hidden forces. Preston and Child masterfully juxtapose the gleaming, cynical machinery of New York City with the archaic, blood-drenched traditions of Obeah and Vodou. The city is portrayed not merely as a backdrop, but as a living, breathing labyrinth harboring dark secrets just beneath the asphalt. The narrative explores how easily civilized lives can be upended when they collide with primal belief systems that defy rational explanation. When the rule of law and forensic science hit a brick wall, it falls to the eccentric, aristocratic FBI Agent Aloysius X.L. Pendergast to peer into the abyss where science and superstition blur.

Preston and Child’s writing style is a masterclass in pacing and sensory immersion. They write with a cinematic flair that keeps the pages turning at a relentless velocity, yet they never sacrifice atmosphere for speed. The authors excel at creating claustrophobic tension, whether it is through the eerie security camera footage of a corpse returning home or the oppressive, sweat-soaked air of a secretive cult enclave where no outsider has ever survived. The dialogue is sharp, and the action sequences are choreographed with precision. Furthermore, the authors maintain a delicate balance between the intellectual puzzle-solving of traditional mysteries and the visceral shocks of horror fiction. Pendergast himself remains an intoxicating literary creation—part Sherlock Holmes, part gothic villain, and entirely enigmatic—whose cold intellect is fascinating to observe as it confronts forces that logic alone cannot defeat.

While the novel leans heavily into the supernatural tropes that fans of the series have come to adore, it also carries a profound emotional weight. The attack on Smithback and Kelly hits close to home for longtime readers, injecting genuine grief and high personal stakes into an investigation that might otherwise remain an abstract puzzle. This emotional grounding elevates Cemetery Dance above standard genre fare, ensuring that the terror feels earned and the heroes' quest feels urgent. 

Ultimately, Cemetery Dance is a thrilling, spine-chilling roller coaster that reminds us why the Pendergast series remains a titan of modern suspense. Preston and Child prove once again that they are unrivaled architects of the macabre, capable of dragging readers deep into the shadows and leaving them begging for more. It is a dark, twisting dance with death that will linger in your mind long after you turn the final page.
