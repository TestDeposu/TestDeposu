# Somewhere In Time

**Author:** Richard Matheson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1975

Richard Matheson is widely celebrated as a titan of horror and speculative fiction, a Grand Master whose pen often exposed the fragile borders of our reality. Yet, in his 1975 masterpiece, Somewhere In Time, Matheson pivots away from sheer terror to explore an emotion just as potent and infinitely more delicate: obsessive, transcendent love. Winner of the World Fantasy Award, this novel is not merely a romance draped in sci-fi tropes; it is a profound philosophical meditation on memory, longing, and the stubborn defiance of the human heart against the relentless flow of time.

The narrative centers on Richard Collier, a contemporary man whose life lacks anchor until he visits the historic Hotel del Coronado in San Diego. There, a photograph of Elise McKenna—a celebrated actress from the late nineteenth century—strikes him with the force of a revelation. What begins as a curious fascination quickly metastasizes into an all-consuming passion. Through sheer force of will, self-hypnosis, and an absolute rejection of chronological boundaries, Richard wills himself backward to the year 1896. In the gaslit elegance of the Victorian era, he finds his soul mate, proving that true love is a force capable of bending the universe itself.

Matheson’s stylistic brilliance lies in his grounding of the impossible. Where other writers might rely on elaborate, gadget-driven time travel, Matheson treats the journey through time as an internal, psychological, and spiritual awakening. His prose is meticulous, understated, and intensely atmospheric. By contrasting the gritty, mundane realities of modern life with the romanticized yet vividly rendered textures of 1896 San Diego, he creates a sensory tapestry that makes Richard’s temporal leap feel startlingly plausible. The author does not rush the journey; instead, he lingers in the quiet spaces of anticipation and discovery, allowing the reader to feel the intoxicating vertigo of stepping outside of history.

At its core, the central theme of Somewhere In Time is the triumph of emotional truth over physical laws. Matheson poses a haunting question: How much of our lives are defined by the moments we refuse to let go of? Richard’s quest is romantic, yes, but it is also existential. It speaks to the universal yearning to connect with someone so deeply that mortality and linear time become mere inconveniences. 

Though immortalized in popular culture by its beloved 1980 film adaptation starring Christopher Reeve and Jane Seymour, the original novel remains a vastly superior exploration of obsession and devotion. It is a haunting, bittersweet triumph that lingers in the mind long after the final page is turned, reminding us that true passion knows no boundaries—not even the barrier of years.
