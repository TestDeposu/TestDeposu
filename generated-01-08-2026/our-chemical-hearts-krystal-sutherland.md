# Our Chemical Hearts

**Author:** Krystal Sutherland  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

Krystal Sutherland’s 2016 debut, Our Chemical Hearts, boldly subverts the sugar-coated conventions of the contemporary young adult romance, carving out a messy, bruised, and profoundly authentic niche in the genre. Often compared to the literary blueprints of John Green and Rainbow Rowell, Sutherland’s novel is far less interested in the giddy perfection of falling in love and vastly more fascinated by the devastating, transformative architecture of heartbreak. Through her sharp wit and unflinching emotional honesty, Sutherland delivers a potent reminder that our first loves rarely shape us by making us whole; rather, they reshape us through our fractures.

At the thematic core of the novel is the painful dismantling of the manic pixie dream girl trope, viewed through the cynical yet hopelessly romantic lens of protagonist Henry Page. Henry has spent his adolescence waiting for the cinematic lightning strike of first love, the kind promised by pop culture and literature. Instead, he gets Grace Town. Dragging a foot, leaning heavily on a wooden cane, wearing oversized men's clothing, and smelling faintly of cigarettes and isolation, Grace is the antithesis of a glossy romantic ideal. She is haunted, elusive, and profoundly broken by a tragedy she keeps buried beneath layers of defensive armor. 

Sutherland’s greatest thematic triumph lies in her deconstruction of Henry’s savior complex. Initially, Henry fancies himself the architect of Grace’s redemption, believing that his affection can stitch together her tattered pieces. However, Sutherland resists the easy, toxic narrative that love is a cure-all. She paints a far more sophisticated portrait of trauma and grief, illustrating that some wounds cannot simply be kissed away by a devoted partner. Grace is not a puzzle for Henry to solve, nor is she a project to be completed. Through their inevitable friction and heartbreak, Henry is forced to confront the harsh reality that loving someone deeply sometimes means standing back while they rebuild themselves on their own terms. 

Stylistically, Sutherland writes with a crackling, observational cadence that elevates the high school setting. Her prose is spiked with dry humor, vivid metaphors, and a delightful obsession with science and literature—most notably the Japanese art of kintsugi, where broken pottery is mended with gold, making the scars the most beautiful part of the object. This metaphor permeates the novel's stylistic DNA. Sutherland's writing treats her characters' emotional vulnerabilities not as flaws to be hidden, but as the golden seams that ultimately define them. Her dialogue is snappy and believable, capturing the chaotic cadence of teenage desperation without sacrificing emotional weight. 

Furthermore, Sutherland refuses to offer her readers a neat, tied-up fairy-tale ending. Our Chemical Hearts respects its audience too much for easy consolations. It acknowledges that first loves are often chemical catalysts—volatile reactions that burn hot, leave scorched earth behind, and permanently alter our molecular structure. We do not walk away from them unscathed; we walk away changed. 

In a literary landscape frequently crowded with predictable tropes, Our Chemical Hearts stands out as a visceral, bittersweet triumph. Krystal Sutherland has crafted a brilliant, bruising debut that honors the agonizing beauty of youth. It is a vital read for anyone willing to brave the wreckage of first love and discover the resilient gold hidden within our deepest heartbreaks.
