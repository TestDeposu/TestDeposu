# Amadeus

**Author:** Peter Shaffer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2002

Envy is perhaps the most corrosive human emotion, capable of turning admiration into a quiet, consuming poison. Peter Shaffer’s masterpiece, Amadeus, stands as one of the most brilliant theatrical explorations of this dark psychological labyrinth. While the published text in question serves as a comprehensive study guide derived from The Gale Group’s Drama for Students series, the enduring brilliance of Shaffer’s original play commands center stage. At its core, the work is not merely a historical dramatization of the relationship between Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart and Antonio Salieri; it is a profound philosophical interrogation of mediocrity wrestling with genius, and the tragic torment of recognizing divine grace in another while finding only silence in oneself.

Shaffer constructs his narrative as a sprawling, confession-style memory play, framed by an aged Salieri looking back on his life from the precipice of obscurity. The stylistic genius of Amadeus lies in its brilliant deployment of theatricality. Salieri acts as both the protagonist and the unreliable narrator, directly addressing the audience as his "ghosts of the future." This metatheatrical device breaks the fourth wall effortlessly, pulling the reader—or viewer—into an intimate complicity with Salieri’s bitterness. Shaffer juxtaposes the rigid, pious conventions of late 18th-century Vienna with the explosive, vulgar, yet transcendent brilliance of Mozart’s music. Through this sharp contrast, the author explores the central theme: the agony of the second-rate artist who possesses just enough taste to recognize true greatness, but lacks the divine spark to create it. 

Salieri is not a traditional villain; he is a deeply sympathetic and terrifyingly recognizable figure. He is a man who made a bargain with God—virtue and diligence in exchange for fame and musical immortality. Yet, when the Almighty bestows His musical genius upon a foul-mouthed, giggling juvenile delinquent like Mozart, Salieri’s faith fractures. Shaffer brilliantly frames Salieri’s war not against Mozart the man, but against God Himself. How can a benevolent Creator mock a devout servant by channeling His voice through a buffoon? This theological crisis elevates Amadeus from a standard period piece into a universal meditation on ambition, jealousy, and the cruel distribution of talent.

Mozart, meanwhile, is rendered by Shaffer with heartbreaking vulnerability. Far from the polished icon of classical music history, Shaffer’s Mozart is a tragic figure—infantile, socially inept, yet possessing an ear tuned directly to the cosmos. When Mozart sits at the keyboard, the vulgarity melts away, replaced by a purity that leaves Salieri physically sick with envy. Shaffer’s stylistic brilliance shines in how he uses music not just as a backdrop, but as an active, breathing character that dictates the emotional rhythm of the text.

For students, researchers, and lovers of dramatic literature, engaging with Shaffer’s work through structured analysis unlocks the rich layers of historical context, thematic depth, and psychological nuance that the play offers. It challenges the reader to look inward and confront their own inadequacies. Amadeus remains a striking triumph because it reminds us that while mediocrity may rage against genius, genius speaks for eternity, leaving the envious to fade into the very silence they so desperately tried to fill.
