# Almond

**Author:** Sohn Won-Pyung  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

A striking exploration of empathy, isolation, and the fragile threads of human connection, Sohn Won-Pyung’s international sensation *Almond* is a quiet triumph of contemporary translated fiction. Originally published in South Korea in 2017 and brought to English-speaking audiences in 2020 via Sandy Joosun Lee’s seamless translation, the novel immediately sets itself apart through its unflinching, matter-of-fact narrative voice. It is a coming-of-age story unlike any other, centered not on a protagonist overflowing with youthful passion, but on one utterly devoid of it.

At the heart of the novel is Yunjae, a sixteen-year-old boy diagnosed with alexithymia—a neurological condition marked by underdeveloped amygdalae, the almond-shaped structures in the brain responsible for processing fear, anger, and emotional responses. To help him navigate a world that demands emotional reciprocity, Yunjae’s mother and grandmother construct a meticulous domestic survival guide. They fill their apartment above a secondhand bookstore with colorful Post-it notes instructing him on the social choreography of daily life: when to smile, when to offer condolences, and when to express gratitude. 

Sohn’s thematic brilliance lies in how she uses Yunjae’s neurological condition as a magnifying glass for our own society. While Yunjae is medically incapable of feeling, the world around him is often violently, apathetically cold. This central irony peaks on Christmas Eve—Yunjae’s sixteenth birthday—when a random act of horrific public violence shatters his safe existence, stripping away his mother and grandmother in a single afternoon. Left entirely alone, Yunjae must figure out how to exist in a reality that is as terrifying as it is incomprehensible.

The author’s literary style is spare, lucid, and deceptively simple. Avoiding melodramatic flourishes, Sohn writes with a clinical precision that mirrors Yunjae’s internal landscape. Yet, beneath the flat affect of the narration pulses a profound emotional resonance. Yunjae does not lament his condition; he observes. This deadpan sincerity makes his narration profoundly disarming and deeply moving. Every sentence feels deliberate, stripping away narrative fat to expose the bare bones of human survival and adaptation.

The novel’s narrative trajectory shifts when Yunjae encounters Gon, a troubled, hot-tempered teenager whose life has been defined by abandonment and abuse. Gon is essentially all fire; Yunjae is ice. Their unlikely friction forms the emotional backbone of the book. As they forge a complex bond—alongside a blossoming connection with a track star named Dora—Yunjae’s rigidly guarded interior begins to crack. Sohn avoids easy, sentimental tropes of miraculous healing. Instead, she presents a more arduous, realistic transformation: connection is not a cure, but a choice. It is a slow, often painful awakening to the risks of caring about another human being.

Ultimately, *Almond* asks a haunting question: What does it truly mean to be human? Is it the biological ability to weep and rage, or is it the conscious decision to step outside of oneself and protect another, even at great personal risk? Sohn Won-Pyung has crafted a compact masterpiece that lingers long after the final page, reminding us that empathy is a muscle, and that even the coldest hearts possess the capacity to learn how to feel.
