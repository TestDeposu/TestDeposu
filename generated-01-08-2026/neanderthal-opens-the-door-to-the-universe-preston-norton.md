# Neanderthal Opens the Door to the Universe

**Author:** Preston Norton  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

**Neanderthal Opens the Door to the Universe: A Heartfelt, Hilarious, and Unexpectedly Poignant Journey**

Preston Norton’s *Neanderthal Opens the Door to the Universe* is the kind of novel that sneaks up on you—first with its razor-sharp wit, then with its quiet, devastating honesty, and finally with a story so deeply human it lingers like a favorite song you can’t stop humming. At its core, this is a book about belonging, redemption, and the messy, beautiful ways we find our place in the world—even when the world seems determined to push us away.

The protagonist, Cliff Hubbard, is a towering, lovable disaster—a literal giant (6’6”, 250 pounds) who’s spent his life being underestimated, mocked, and forgotten. His nickname, "Neanderthal," isn’t just a jab at his size; it’s a cruel label for the way he’s been sidelined, as if his very existence is an afterthought. When his older brother’s suicide leaves him adrift, Cliff is at his lowest point—until he’s dragged into an unlikely mission by Aaron Zimmerman, the school’s golden-boy quarterback. Aaron, convinced he’s been divinely tasked with "making Happy Valley High suck less," insists Cliff is the only one who can help. What follows is a wild, heartfelt odyssey through the absurdities and cruelties of high school life, where the line between heroism and chaos blurs delightfully.

Norton’s writing is a masterclass in balancing humor and heartache. The novel crackles with the kind of sharp, observational comedy that makes you laugh out loud—whether it’s Cliff’s deadpan reactions to the world’s indignities or the absurdity of Aaron’s "divine mission." But beneath the jokes lies a profound emotional weight. Norton doesn’t shy away from the pain of grief, the sting of loneliness, or the way trauma can warp a person’s sense of self. Cliff’s journey isn’t just about fixing a school; it’s about learning to see himself as someone worthy of love, of purpose, and of belonging. The scenes where he confronts his past—especially his brother’s suicide—are raw and moving, proving that Norton has a rare ability to make vulnerability feel powerful rather than maudlin.

What makes *Neanderthal* truly special is how it turns a clichéd "underdog story" into something fresh and unexpected. Cliff isn’t just a brute with a heart of gold; he’s a deeply flawed, introspective character whose growth is as much about self-acceptance as it is about changing his world. Aaron, for all his arrogance and divine delusions, is equally compelling—a flawed hero whose journey mirrors Cliff’s, forcing him to question his own identity and the role he’s played in the school’s dysfunction. Together, they navigate a landscape of bullies, cults, and corrupt authority figures, but the real battle is internal: learning to trust, to forgive, and to believe that change—both in oneself and in the world—is possible.

Norton’s style is deceptively simple. His prose is clean, conversational, and effortlessly engaging, as if he’s sitting across from you, sharing a story over coffee. But there’s a depth to his writing that belies its accessibility. He excels at capturing the absurdity of adolescence—how petty rivalries can feel like wars, how a single moment can change everything, and how the people we least expect can become our saviors. The novel’s humor is infectious, but it’s never at the expense of its emotional core. Instead, Norton uses laughter as a shield, a way to make the heavier moments easier to bear.

By the end, *Neanderthal Opens the Door to the Universe* feels like a love letter to the outsiders, the misfits, and the dreamers who’ve ever felt like they didn’t belong. It’s a story about finding your tribe, about the power of second chances, and about the idea that even the most broken places can be mended—if you’re willing to open the door. Norton doesn’t offer easy answers, but he does give you something far more valuable: the courage to keep going, even when the world seems stacked against you.

In a genre often dominated by cynicism or saccharine sentimentality, *Neanderthal* stands out as a rare gem—funny, heartfelt, and utterly unforgettable. It’s the kind of book that makes you laugh, cry, and cheer for its characters long after the last page. If you’re looking for a story that opens doors to the universe—both literally and metaphorically—this is it.
