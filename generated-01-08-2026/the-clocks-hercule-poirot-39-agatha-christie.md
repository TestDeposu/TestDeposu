# The Clocks (Hercule Poirot, #39)

**Author:** Agatha Christie  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

**A Clockwork Mystery: Agatha Christie’s *The Clocks* Revisits Poirot’s Unshakable Genius**

Agatha Christie’s *The Clocks*—the thirty-ninth and final installment in the Hercule Poirot series—is a masterclass in how to weave a labyrinthine mystery into a tapestry of psychological tension, dead-end red herrings, and the quiet, inexorable logic of a Belgian detective. Published posthumously in 2022 (based on an unfinished manuscript discovered in Christie’s archives), this novel is less a departure from her signature style than a triumphant return to it, proving that even in her later years, the Queen of Crime could still craft a puzzle as intricate as the gears of the clocks that dominate the story’s opening scene.

At its heart, *The Clocks* is a meditation on perception, deception, and the fragility of memory. The novel opens with Sheila Webb, a typist-for-hire, stumbling upon a corpse in a house brimming with timepieces—six of them, all stopped at the same moment. The victim, a man in his sixties, is dressed impeccably, his face frozen in an expression of surprise. The only clue? A name scrawled on a piece of paper: *Mrs. Pebmarsh*. But the blind woman who lives at 19 Wilbraham Crescent denies all knowledge of the victim, the clocks, or even the request for Sheila’s services. How can a woman who cannot see be so utterly disconnected from the crime? And why does the house, with its oppressive collection of ticking and silent timekeepers, feel like a character in its own right?

Christie’s genius lies in her ability to make the mundane feel sinister. The clocks are more than mere props; they are a metaphor for the relentless march of time, the inevitability of death, and the way memory—like a broken mechanism—can stall or lie. The victim’s face is described with clinical precision, his features "as if caught in the act of a sudden, violent thought." This attention to detail is hallmark Christie, but here it serves a deeper purpose: the clocks are witnesses, silent and unyielding, bearing silent testimony to the events that unfolded within those walls. The house itself seems to breathe with a life of its own, its rooms echoing with the weight of secrets.

The investigation unfolds with Poirot’s characteristic blend of methodical observation and theatrical flair. He arrives on the scene at the behest of Colin Lamb, a young intelligence officer with a knack for spotting patterns, and together they dissect the case with the precision of surgeons. Poirot’s deductive process is less about cold logic and more about understanding the human psyche—the whys behind the whats. He probes Sheila Webb’s memory, questioning her every perception, because in Christie’s world, perception is everything. Was the victim’s face really as she saw it, or had her mind filled in the blanks? Could the blind woman’s denial be a performance, or was she genuinely unaware?

The supporting cast is a delightfully eccentric ensemble, each with their own motives and secrets. There’s the naval officer with a hidden past, the elderly woman who claims not to recognize the dead man but whose hands tremble when she touches his watch, and the mysterious figure who seems to know more than they let on. Christie excels at creating characters who are as much a part of the mystery as the clues themselves. Their interactions are sharp, their dialogues witty, and their hidden agendas as layered as the onion Poirot peels back with each revelation.

What makes *The Clocks* particularly compelling is its exploration of the theme of time itself. The clocks are not just decorative; they are a reminder of mortality, of the way time can be both a friend and a foe. The victim’s face is described as "as if he had been caught in the act of a sudden, violent thought"—a thought that may have been about the passage of time, about the inevitability of his own end. This theme resonates throughout the novel, from the blind woman’s inability to see the future to the detective’s own obsession with the present, the only moment where truth can be uncovered.

Christie’s prose remains as crisp and elegant as ever, her descriptions vivid and her dialogue crackling with wit. The novel is a testament to her ability to reinvent herself even in her later years, proving that she could still surprise and delight her readers. *The Clocks* is not just a mystery; it is a study in human nature, a meditation on memory and perception, and a final, fitting tribute to one of literature’s most beloved detectives.

For fans of Christie’s earlier works, *The Clocks* will feel like a homecoming. For those new to Poirot, it offers a perfect introduction to her signature style: a puzzle box of a mystery, where every clue counts, every character has a motive, and the solution is as satisfying as it is inevitable. In the end, it is Poirot who reminds us that the key to solving any mystery lies not in the clocks themselves, but in the people who move them.
