# The Uncensored Picture of Dorian Gray

**Author:** Oscar Wilde  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

The Uncensored Picture of Dorian Gray, published in 2012, offers readers a chance to experience Oscar Wilde's infamous novel as he originally intended. This edition restores the material that was removed by the novel's first editor, providing a fresh and unflinching look at Wilde's exploration of vanity, morality, and the human condition.

At its core, The Picture of Dorian Gray is a philosophical exploration of the tension between aesthetics and ethics. The novel's central character, Dorian Gray, is a beautiful young man who becomes infatuated with his own image and makes a Faustian bargain to sell his soul for eternal youth and beauty. As the novel progresses, Dorian's portrait, which reflects the true state of his soul, becomes increasingly disfigured, while he himself remains outwardly youthful and beautiful.

Wilde's writing style is, as always, witty and engaging, with a keen eye for the absurdities and hypocrisies of Victorian society. His characters are vividly drawn and complex, with Basil Hallward, the artist who becomes obsessed with Dorian, serving as a kind of stand-in for Wilde himself. Lord Henry Wotton, the charming and corrupting influence who sets Dorian on his path of destruction, is a masterful creation, embodying the worst excesses of the aristocracy.

One of the most striking aspects of this uncensored edition is the way it highlights Wilde's exploration of same-sex desire. The original editor's cuts were clearly intended to remove any material that might be considered "offensive" or "immoral," but in doing so, they also obscured the true nature of Wilde's themes and characters. In this edition, we see Wilde's frank and unapologetic portrayal of male desire, which is all the more remarkable given the repressive social context in which he was writing.

Wilde's comment that The Picture of Dorian Gray "contains much of me" is particularly apt, as the novel can be seen as a kind of spiritual autobiography. Basil Hallward represents Wilde's idealized self, while Lord Henry represents the public's perception of him as a witty and decadent aesthete. Dorian Gray, meanwhile, represents the self that Wilde might have been in a more permissive time, free to explore his desires without fear of persecution.

Overall, The Uncensored Picture of Dorian Gray is a fascinating and thought-provoking read, offering a fresh perspective on a classic novel. Wilde's writing is as witty and engaging as ever, and his themes of vanity, morality, and the human condition remain as relevant today as they were when the novel was first published. This edition is a must-read for anyone interested in Wilde, Victorian literature, or the history of LGBTQ+ culture.
