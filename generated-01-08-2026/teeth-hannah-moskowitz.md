# Teeth

**Author:** Hannah Moskowitz  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

**Teeth by Hannah Moskowitz: A Haunting Fairy Tale of Love, Lies, and the Cost of Survival**

Hannah Moskowitz’s *Teeth* is a dark, hypnotic fairy tale for the modern age—a story that twists the familiar contours of coming-of-age narratives into something far more unsettling. With the same raw, poetic intensity that defined her earlier works (*Break*, *Gone, Gone, Gone*), Moskowitz crafts a tale where love and violence are not just intertwined but indistinguishable, where the line between salvation and damnation blurs with every whispered secret. This isn’t just a story about a boy’s first crush or the isolation of adolescence; it’s a brutal exploration of what we’re willing to sacrifice to protect those we love, and the terrifying ways in which desire and destruction become one.

At its heart, *Teeth* is a story about belief—what we choose to see, what we refuse to acknowledge, and the consequences of those choices. Rudy, the novel’s protagonist, is a boy drowning in grief and guilt, his family’s move to a remote island a desperate, last-ditch effort to save his younger brother from an illness that medicine has failed to cure. The island is a character in itself—a place where the ocean’s whispers feel like warnings, where the air is thick with the scent of salt and something darker, something *wrong*. Here, Rudy is adrift, his nights filled with the sound of the sea and the gnawing certainty that his family’s fragile stability is a house of cards, one wrong breath away from collapse.

Then comes Diana, a girl who arrives like a breath of fresh air, her presence a stark contrast to the suffocating weight of Rudy’s isolation. She is warmth in the cold, a spark in the dark, and for the first time, Rudy feels something akin to hope. But hope, Moskowitz reminds us, is a fragile thing—especially when it’s built on lies. Because Diana is not the only new arrival on the island. There is also Teeth, a boy whose name alone carries a weight of dread, whose presence is both magnetic and menacing. Teeth is a paradox: charming, dangerous, and utterly compelling. He is the kind of person who makes Rudy question everything he thought he knew about morality, about trust, about what it means to be human. And it is Teeth who introduces Rudy to the island’s darkest secret—the one that will force him to confront the most painful truth of all: that love, in its most extreme forms, can be both a lifeline and a noose.

Moskowitz’s prose is lush and lyrical, but never sentimental. She writes with the precision of a surgeon, carving out each moment with deliberate, almost surgical clarity. The island is alive with sensory detail—the crack of waves against the shore, the taste of salt on the tongue, the way the wind carries voices that aren’t quite human. Yet for all its beauty, there is an undercurrent of unease, a sense that something is *off*, that the island’s charm is a mask for something far more sinister. Moskowitz’s style is a masterclass in atmospheric tension, where the ordinary becomes ominous and the extraordinary feels just within reach.

What makes *Teeth* so gripping is its refusal to offer easy answers. Rudy’s journey is not one of triumph, but of reckoning. He is forced to grapple with the idea that his brother’s life might depend on choices that feel morally bankrupt, that his own happiness might come at a cost he isn’t prepared to pay. Moskowitz doesn’t shy away from the brutality of her themes; she presents them with a stark honesty that lingers long after the final page. The novel is a meditation on the lengths to which we go for those we love, on the ways in which love can distort our perception of right and wrong, and on the terrifying realization that sometimes, the things we do to save someone are the very things that destroy them.

At its core, *Teeth* is a story about the power of belief—and the danger of unquestioning faith. Rudy’s arc is one of disillusionment, as he learns that the world is not as black-and-white as he once thought. Diana’s love is real, but it is not enough. Teeth’s allure is intoxicating, but it is also a trap. And the island’s secrets are not just personal—they are ancient, primal, and inescapable. Moskowitz weaves these elements together with a skill that borders on the supernatural, creating a narrative that feels both deeply personal and universally haunting.

For readers who crave stories that are equal parts romantic and horrifying, that balance beauty with brutality, *Teeth* is a must-read. It is a novel that lingers in the mind long after the last page, a tale that asks uncomfortable questions and delivers no easy resolutions. Moskowitz’s writing is a reminder that fairy tales are not just for children—they are warnings, and *Teeth* is one of the most chilling of them all.
