# Wandfasted (The Black Witch Chronicles, #0.5)

**Author:** Laurie Forest  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

**Wandfasted: A Prequel That Weaves Magic, Mystery, and the Weight of Destiny**

Laurie Forest’s *Wandfasted*—the prequel to *The Black Witch Chronicles*—is a spellbinding dive into the fractured soul of Erthia, a world where magic is both salvation and curse. This collection of interconnected stories, set twenty years before the events of *The Black Witch*, unfolds like a slow-burning ritual, revealing the roots of power, betrayal, and the dangerous allure of prophecy. Forest’s prose is lush yet precise, her storytelling a masterclass in atmospheric tension, where every word feels like a whispered secret in a storm.

At its core, *Wandfasted* explores the cost of destiny. Tessla Harrow, the first story’s protagonist, is a refugee fleeing the Realm War, only to stumble upon a power she never knew she possessed—and a connection to Vale Gardner, the son of the legendary Black Witch. Their bond is electric, charged with the kind of fate that feels both inevitable and terrifying. Forest doesn’t shy away from the brutality of war or the fragility of hope; instead, she frames it with poetic intensity, making the reader feel the weight of every choice. The world of Erthia is alive with political intrigue, where magic is a currency and survival is a gamble. Tessla’s journey is one of discovery, but also of reckoning: she must decide whether to cling to the past or embrace the power that could either save her people or doom them.

The second story, *Light Mage*, introduces Sagellyn Gaffney, a rare female Light Mage in a world that has forgotten her kind. Her fastmate—a forced magical union—is a facade, and when she uncovers the truth, she must flee, carrying with her the White Wand, a relic as dangerous as it is sacred. Forest’s handling of Sagellyn’s arc is particularly compelling. She avoids the pitfall of making magic feel like a mere plot device; instead, it’s woven into the fabric of Erthia’s society, shaping its laws, its fears, and its hierarchies. The tension between tradition and rebellion is palpable, and Sagellyn’s defiance resonates as a quiet revolution.

What makes *Wandfasted* so gripping is Forest’s ability to balance grand themes with intimate character moments. The world-building is immersive, but never overwhelming; instead, it’s revealed through the eyes of flawed, human-like figures who grapple with their roles in a world that demands perfection. The prose is evocative, with a rhythm that mirrors the ebb and flow of magic itself. There’s a musicality to her writing—phrases linger like incantations, and the dialogue crackles with authenticity.

Yet, the book isn’t without its challenges. Some readers might find the pacing uneven, particularly in the transitions between stories, but this is less a flaw and more a reflection of the interconnectedness of the narratives. The prequel nature of *Wandfasted* means it’s best experienced as a companion piece to *The Black Witch*, offering depth without spoiling the main saga. For those who dive in cold, the payoff is in the world-building and the emotional resonance of its characters.

Ultimately, *Wandfasted* is a testament to Forest’s skill in crafting a world where magic is both a gift and a burden. It’s a story about the cost of prophecy, the strength of defiance, and the quiet courage it takes to rewrite your own fate. If you’re a fan of richly detailed fantasy with morally complex characters and a touch of gothic romance, this collection will enchant you. It’s not just a prelude—it’s a necessary chapter in the saga of Erthia, one that lingers long after the last page.
