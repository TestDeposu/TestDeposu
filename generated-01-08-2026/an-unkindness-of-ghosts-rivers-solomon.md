# An Unkindness of Ghosts

**Author:** Rivers Solomon  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

An Unkindness of Ghosts by Rivers Solomon arrives as a searing, lyrical detonation in contemporary speculative fiction, marrying the visceral urgency of slave narratives with the cold, mechanized dread of a generation‑ship odyssey. Set aboard the HSS Matilda—a vessel that reproduces the social hierarchies of the antebellum South in the void of space—the novel follows Aster, a dark‑skinned, neurodivergent healer dwelling in the ship’s lowdeck slums. Through her eyes, Solomon interrogates how systems of oppression are not merely reproduced but re‑engineered when humanity carries its worst instincts into the stars. The central theme, therefore, is the inescapability of hierarchy: even when the Earth is a distant memory, the patterns of racism, classism, and gendered violence persist, reshaped by the ship’s rigid caste structure and the myth of a Promised Land that serves as both hope and control.

Solomon’s style is both brutal and beautiful, a prose that reads like a wound sutured with poetry. She employs a fragmented, almost stream‑of‑consciousness narrative that mirrors Aster’s fractured perception—her autism renders the world in heightened sensory detail, and the text accordingly lingers on smells, textures, and bodily sensations that most novels would gloss over. This immersive immediacy forces the reader to feel the grit of the lowdeck’s filth, the sting of the overseer’s whip, and the quiet terror of forbidden knowledge. Yet amid the visceral description, Solomon inserts moments of startling lyricism: a description of the ship’s engine as “a heart beating in the dark,” or Aster’s internal monologue that flickers like a candle in a storm. These flashes of lyricism do not soften the critique; they amplify it, reminding us that even in the most dehumanizing contexts, humanity’s capacity for wonder and resistance endures.

The novel’s strength lies in its unflinching confrontation of intersecting oppressions. Aster’s identity as a Black, disabled, queer woman places her at the nexus of multiple vectors of domination, and Solomon refuses to treat any axis as secondary. The overseer, Lieutenant‑Commander Theo, embodies the toxic masculinity and sadistic authority that uphold the ship’s order, while the religious doctrine that governs Matilda—centered on a sterile, puritanical vision of purity—functions as a spiritual justification for cruelty. Solomon shows how ideology, biology, and technology co‑produce a regime where bodies become commodities and deviation is pathologized. Aster’s nascent rebellion is not a grand, heroic uprising but a series of intimate, subversive acts: sharing forbidden medicine, decoding the ship’s true purpose, and nurturing clandestine bonds of kinship across decks. Her resistance is quietly revolutionary, suggesting that change often germinates in the shadows before it can blaze into open conflict.

What makes An Unkindness of Ghosts particularly resonant today is its Afrofuturist sensibility. Solomon does not merely transplant historical trauma onto a sci‑fi backdrop; she reimagines the future as a space where Black futures are both imagined and contested. The mythic Promised Land becomes a metaphor for the elusive promise of liberation that haunts marginalized communities—a destination perpetually deferred by those who benefit from the status quo. Yet the novel also plants seeds of hope: Aster’s connection to the ship’s ecological systems, her ability to heal, and her yearning for knowledge point toward an alternative ethos rooted in care rather than conquest. This tension between despair and possibility gives the work its emotional heft; readers are left not with a tidy resolution but with a lingering question about what it will take to dismantle the architectures of oppression that we carry with us, wherever we go.

In terms of craft, Solomon’s debut exhibits a maturity that belies its status as a first novel. She balances world‑building with character intimacy, never allowing the intricacies of the Matilda’s social machinery to overshadow the personal stakes. The pacing is deliberate, mirroring the slow, inexorable grind of systemic injustice, while bursts of action—such as the climactic confrontation in the ship’s hydroponics bay—serve as cathartic releases that feel earned rather than sensational. Her dialogue crackles with subtext, each exchange layered with power dynamics that reveal character motivations without exposition. Moreover, the novel’s structure—shifting between Aster’s present‑tense ordeal and fragmented flashbacks to her mother’s stories—creates a palimpsest of memory that underscores how history lives in the body, shaping identity and informing resistance.

Ultimately, An Unkindness of Ghosts is a tour de force that uses the speculative lens to lay bare the continuities of violence and the stubborn flicker of defiance. Solomon’s prose is as unsettling as it is enchanting, compelling readers to sit with discomfort while simultaneously feeling the pulse of hope that refuses to be extinguished. For anyone seeking a story that marries the intellectual rigor of Octavia Butler with the emotive force of contemporary literary fiction, this novel is indispensable—a stark reminder that the futures we imagine are inseparable from the pasts we confront.
