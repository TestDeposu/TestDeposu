# Existentialism is a Humanism

**Author:** Jean-Paul Sartre  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

**Existentialism Is a Humanism: Sartre’s Bold Defiance of Fate and the Call to Radical Freedom**

Jean-Paul Sartre’s *Existentialism Is a Humanism* is not merely a lecture—it is a manifesto, a clarion call to the modern soul. Written in 1945, just as Europe was emerging from the ashes of war, Sartre’s words cut through the philosophical jargon of his time to deliver a message as urgent as it is timeless: *we are free, and that freedom is both our burden and our salvation.* This new translation, paired with Sartre’s analysis of Camus’s *The Stranger* and a reflective essay by Annie Cohen-Solal, offers a rare glimpse into the mind of the man who reshaped how we understand existence, morality, and human agency.

At its core, *Existentialism Is a Humanism* is a corrective—a response to the misconceptions that had already begun to cloud Sartre’s philosophy. Critics and the public alike had reduced existentialism to a bleak, nihilistic doctrine, a philosophy of despair. Sartre, ever the provocateur, dismantles this myth with razor-sharp precision. He begins by asserting that existentialism is, above all, *a humanism*—not because it denies the human condition but because it embraces it fully. For Sartre, humanism is not about comforting illusions; it is about confronting the raw, unvarnished truth of our existence: *we are condemned to be free.*

The lecture’s central thesis—*existence precedes essence*—is both radical and liberating. Unlike traditional philosophy, which often begins with abstract concepts (e.g., "what is man?"), Sartre flips the script. He argues that we are not defined by a predetermined nature or divine plan; instead, we are *nothingness* before we choose to be something. This "nothingness" is not a void but a canvas upon which we paint our lives through our actions. There is no grand design, no cosmic script—only the relentless, terrifying, and exhilarating fact that *we are responsible for who we become.*

Sartre’s style is as uncompromising as his philosophy. He writes with the clarity of a preacher and the intensity of a revolutionary. There is no fluff, no philosophical detour—just direct, unfiltered truth. His language is vivid, almost poetic at times, yet grounded in the brutal honesty of human experience. When he describes the "contingency of death," he doesn’t offer solace; he forces the reader to stare into the abyss and recognize that death is not an escape but the ultimate affirmation of our freedom. To live is to choose, and to choose is to confront the fact that our decisions echo beyond ourselves, binding us to all humanity.

The inclusion of Sartre’s analysis of Camus’s *The Stranger* adds another layer to this collection. In his discussion of Meursault, the novel’s detached, indifferent protagonist, Sartre illuminates the existential dilemma: how do we reconcile our freedom with the world’s demands? Meursault’s indifference is not apathy but the raw, unfiltered experience of a man who refuses to be defined by others’ expectations. Sartre sees in him both the absurdity of existence and the courage of authenticity—a man who, in his very indifference, asserts his freedom.

Annie Cohen-Solal’s accompanying essay provides historical and biographical context, reminding us that Sartre was not just a philosopher but a man deeply engaged with the political and moral crises of his time. His existentialism was never abstract; it was a call to action. The Q&A with Sartre, included in this edition, further sharpens the focus, revealing how he grappled with the implications of his own ideas—how could one live authentically in a world that demanded conformity? His answers are as defiant as his philosophy: *we must choose, even when the choice is painful.*

What makes this book so compelling is its relevance. In an era of algorithmic lives, where identity is often dictated by likes, trends, and external validation, Sartre’s message feels like a wake-up call. He doesn’t offer easy answers; he demands that we *become* our answers. The freedom he describes is not a license for selfishness but a summons to responsibility—to ourselves, to others, and to the world. It is a philosophy for those who refuse to be passive spectators in their own lives.

Yet, there is a risk in reading Sartre. His ideas can feel overwhelming, even paralyzing. The weight of absolute freedom is not for the faint of heart. But that is precisely the point. Existentialism is not a comfort zone; it is a crucible. And if *Existentialism Is a Humanism* does nothing else, it invites us to step into that crucible and ask ourselves: *What will I choose?*

For anyone seeking to understand the foundations of modern thought—or simply to grapple with the meaning of their own existence—this book is essential. It is a masterclass in philosophical clarity, a challenge to complacency, and a reminder that the most human thing we can do is to live with unflinching honesty. Sartre didn’t just write about freedom; he embodied it. And in doing so, he gave us the tools to do the same.
