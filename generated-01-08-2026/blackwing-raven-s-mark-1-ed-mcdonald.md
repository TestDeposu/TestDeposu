# Blackwing (Raven's Mark, #1)

**Author:** Ed McDonald  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

Ed McDonald’s 2017 debut, Blackwing, does not merely invite readers into a new fantasy world; it drags them face-down through the radioactive, ash-choked soil of a frontier torn apart by incomprehensible horrors. Described by Anthony Ryan as a brilliant collision between the inventive, weird-fiction worldbuilding of China Miéville and the unrelenting, pulse-pounding heroics of David Gemmell, Blackwing carves out its own distinct, blood-soaked niche in the grimdark genre. At its core, the novel is a masterclass in atmospheric worldbuilding and cynical, hard-boiled survivalism, operating as a dark fantasy neo-noir where the detectives wear armor, the witnesses are psychotic sorcerers, and the city streets are constantly threatened by an apocalyptic wasteland.

The central theme of Blackwing is the heavy, suffocating toll of systemic corruption and the psychological weight of surviving a war that never truly ended. The setting itself, the Misery, is not just a backdrop; it is a malevolent character. This scarred, blighted expanse—the lingering fallout of a cataclysmic war against the immortal Deep Kings—functions as a brutal metaphor for generational trauma. Nearly a century after the war, the people living on this post-apocalyptic frontier are protected only by the Engine, a terrifying and unstable superweapon. McDonald uses this premise to explore how societies function when perpetually standing on the precipice of annihilation. The institutions meant to protect humanity are rotten to the core, the gods are distant, capricious, or dead, and the citizens are left to rot in the dust of divine indifference. 

Enter Ryhalt Galharrow, the novel’s battered, weary protagonist. Galharrow is the quintessential noir detective transplanted into a high-fantasy nightmare. As a "Blackwing"—an agent of the Nameless, the corrupt and ruthless rulers of the republic—he is a bounty hunter of heretics and smugglers, a man who has breathed Misery dust for two bitter decades. Galharrow is an exceptionally striking character precisely because he is deeply flawed, cynical, and utterly tired. He knows he is a blunt instrument of a broken system, yet he persists out of sheer stubbornness and a desperate, buried code of honor. When an assignment to track down a masked noblewoman, Lady Ezabeth Tanza, spirals into a catastrophic revelation—that the Deep Kings may no longer fear the Engine—Galharrow’s world view is violently upended. 

McDonald’s style is where Blackwing truly transcends standard grimdark tropes. His prose is punchy, visceral, and unyielding, drenched in a grit that feels earned rather than performant. He writes with a rhythmic cadence that mimics the harsh breathing of a man stumbling through toxic dust. The action sequences are choreographed with kinetic fury, fast-paced and brutal, yet they are always anchored in the emotional and physical exhaustion of the characters. McDonald has a rare talent for making magic feel genuinely dangerous and high-cost. In the Misery, magic is not a neat, academic discipline; it is a warping, radioactive poison that eats away at the mind and body. The Deep Kings and their twisted wraiths are genuinely terrifying, looming over the narrative as an existential dread that cannot simply be negotiated or fought away with a clever sword swing.

Furthermore, the relationship dynamics in the novel elevate the plot beyond a simple monster-of-the-week routine. Galharrow’s reunion with Ezabeth—a shadow from his grim past—injects the narrative with a layer of complex melancholy. Their shared history is complicated by shifting loyalties, dark secrets, and the uncomfortable reality that the blood Galharrow has spilled in the name of his gods might have all been for a lie. The conspiracy at the heart of the novel untangles slowly, keeping the reader hooked as the illusion of fragile peace peels away to reveal something far more sinister.

In conclusion, Blackwing is a remarkably assured and striking debut that succeeds by taking familiar elements—the world-weary antihero, the post-apocalyptic frontier, the corrupt regime—and executing them with blistering style and razor-sharp prose. Ed McDonald crafts a world so vividly bleak and compelling that readers will find themselves hopelessly drawn into its ash and shadows. It is a must-read for fans of dark, inventive fantasy who appreciate characters with bruised knuckles, cynical hearts, and a refusal to stay down when the sky begins to wail.
