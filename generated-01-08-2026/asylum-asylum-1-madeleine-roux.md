# Asylum (Asylum, #1)

**Author:** Madeleine Roux  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Madeleine Roux’s *Asylum* opens with the promise of a privileged summer program and quickly subverts it into a claustrophobic descent into forgotten madness. Sixteen‑year‑old Dan Crawford expects scholarly camaraderie at New Hampshire College Prep, but the shuttered housing forces him and his new friends Abby and Jordan into the decaying Brookline Dorm—a former sanatorium that once housed the criminally insane. The building itself becomes a character, its peeling wallpaper, echoing corridors, and hidden basement whispering secrets that refuse to stay buried.

The novel’s central theme is the way trauma lingers—not just in individuals but in the spaces they inhabit. Roux deftly shows how the asylum’s dark past seeps into the present, influencing the teens’ thoughts, dreams, and ultimately their sense of self. Dan’s personal quest for belonging mirrors the institution’s attempt to erase and control its patients, creating a resonant parallel between internal and external confinement. As the friends uncover old patient records, cryptic photographs, and unsettling occurrences, the narrative interrogates how societies marginalize the mentally ill and how those silenced voices echo through architecture and memory.

Roux’s style is both atmospheric and accessible, a hallmark of effective YA horror. She blends crisp, present‑tense narration with vivid sensory details that make the dorm’s chill palpable. The inclusion of real found photographs—grainy portraits of former patients, stark institutional interiors—adds a documentary layer that blurs fiction and reality, heightening the uncanny feeling that the horror could be true. This visual element also serves as a pacing tool; each image acts as a beat, prompting the reader to pause and imagine the stories behind the faces before plunging back into the prose.

The author’s prose leans into a restrained, almost journalistic tone when describing the asylum’s history, then shifts to a more lyrical, almost dream‑like register during the characters’ hallucinations and flashbacks. This tonal swing mirrors the thin line between genius and insanity that the book promises to tread. While the plot occasionally leans on familiar haunted‑house tropes—secret doors, whispered warnings, a reluctant skeptic—Roux compensates with strong character dynamics. Dan, Abby, and Jordan each embody a different response to the past: curiosity, fear, and a desperate need to prove themselves. Their friendship grounds the supernatural elements, making the stakes feel personal rather than merely gothic spectacle.

One critique worth noting is that the novel’s reliance on the found‑photo motif, while effective, sometimes feels slightly underutilized; a few images appear more decorative than integral to the mystery. Additionally, the resolution leans toward a tidy explanation that may leave readers craving deeper ambiguity, a hallmark of the best psychological horror. Nevertheless, for a debut YA thriller, Roux delivers a compelling blend of suspense, historical intrigue, and emotional resonance that will linger long after the last page.

*Asylum* is an ideal pick for readers who enjoy the eerie, photo‑laden atmosphere of *Miss Peregrine’s Home for Peculiar Children* paired with the moody introspection of dark academia. It invites teens to contemplate how places remember what people forget—and what happens when those memories finally demand to be heard.
