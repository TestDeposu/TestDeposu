# Impossible (Impossible, #1)

**Author:** Nancy Werlin  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Abby North’s world shifts on the scar that blooms across her father’s abdomen after a routine kidney surgery—a thin, pink line that soon becomes the visible sign of a far deeper wound: cancer. Nancy Werlin’s *Impossible* does not dramatize illness with melodrama; instead, it lets the disease seep quietly into the everyday rhythms of a twelve‑year‑old’s life, turning ordinary concerns—annoying siblings, unrequited crushes, the shifting terrain of friendship—into a backdrop for a much larger, more intimate battle. The novel’s strength lies in its steadfast focus on Abby’s internal landscape, allowing readers to feel the weight of each whispered fear, each forced smile, and each quiet moment of hope as she learns to navigate a family tilted on its axis.

Werlin’s prose is both restrained and luminous. She writes in a close, first‑person voice that feels like a confession slipped into a diary, capturing Abby’s precocious observation without ever tipping into precociousness. Sentences are often short, crisp, and grounded in sensory detail—the smell of antiseptic in the hospital hallway, the way her mother’s tears muffle the sound of the shower, the faint hum of the refrigerator that becomes a metronome for sleepless nights. Yet within this economy of language, Werlin manages to evoke expansive emotional terrain. She lets the reader sit with Abby’s confusion, her anger at the unfairness of “It,” and her gradual, painful acceptance that love does not disappear when a body fails. The author’s style is marked by an honest, almost journalistic clarity that refuses to sentimentalize grief; instead, she honors it by showing how grief reshapes identity, how it forces a child to confront adult realities while still clinging to the fragments of childhood that make her who she is.

A central theme that threads through the narrative is the search for personal peace amid external chaos. Abby’s journey is not about finding a cure or a tidy resolution; it is about discovering an inner steadiness that can coexist with sorrow. Werlin illustrates this through small, symbolic gestures—the way Abby begins to draw the scar as a map rather than a wound, how she learns to listen to her father’s exhausted breaths as a kind of lullaby, and how she redefines her relationship with Spence, moving from a crush rooted in fantasy to a friendship grounded in mutual support. The novel’s hopeful ending feels earned because it does not deny the pain; rather, it suggests that peace can be forged in the act of bearing witness, in the willingness to let love persist even as it changes shape.

What makes *Impossible* resonant beyond its specific medical context is its universal exploration of how families reorganize themselves around loss. Werlin avoids the trap of making the illness a mere plot device; instead, the cancer becomes a catalyst for Abby’s self‑discovery, prompting her to question what she values, what she fears, and what she can control. The author’s deft handling of voice ensures that Abby never feels like a passive victim; she is an active participant in her own healing, even when her actions are as simple as choosing to sit quietly beside her father’s bed or as bold as confronting her mother’s silent grief.

In a literary landscape where YA fiction often leans on high‑concept fantasies or dystopian thrillers, Werlin’s quiet, character‑driven approach stands out. Her style—clear, compassionate, and meticulously attuned to the subtleties of adolescent emotion—invites readers to linger in the spaces between words, to feel the ache of a scar that is both physical and emblematic. *Impossible* is a testament to the resilience of the human spirit, a reminder that even when the world feels irrevocably altered, there remains room for growth, for connection, and for a kind of peace that is hard‑won but profoundly real.
