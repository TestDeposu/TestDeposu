# A History of Wild Places

**Author:** Shea Ernshaw  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

**A History of Wild Places by Shea Ernshaw: Where Fairy Tales Rot Beneath the Surface**

Shea Ernshaw’s *A History of Wild Places* is a haunting, atmospheric descent into a commune that exists beyond the law of time—and sanity. Part psychological thriller, part dark fairy tale, this novel doesn’t just explore the mysteries of a vanished community; it dissects the way fear, memory, and isolation warp the human mind. Ernshaw’s prose is lush yet unsettling, weaving a narrative that feels both intimate and vast, like a story whispered in the dark by someone who knows too much.

At its core, the book is a meditation on the allure and danger of the "wild places" we create—or are forced into. Pastoral, the reclusive commune at the heart of the story, is a place that should have faded into myth by now. Founded in the 1970s as a utopia of simplicity and connection, it has since become a sealed-off enclave, its members convinced they are protecting themselves from an unseen threat: *rot*, a disease that spreads like a creeping dread, eroding trust and sanity. When outsider Travis Wren stumbles upon Pastoral and vanishes, the community’s carefully constructed illusion begins to unravel. Years later, Theo—a lifelong resident—discovers Travis’s abandoned truck, and with it, a trail of secrets that threaten to consume him, his wife Calla, and her sister Bee.

Ernshaw’s genius lies in her ability to make the supernatural feel deeply human. The *rot* isn’t just a physical plague; it’s a metaphor for the decay of truth, the way lies fester in families, and the way fear can turn a sanctuary into a prison. The novel’s structure—jumping between past and present, between the outsider’s perspective and the insider’s—creates a sense of unease that lingers long after the final page. You’re never sure what’s real, what’s remembered, and what’s been fabricated to protect the fragile illusion of safety.

Themes of fairy tales and the fear of the dark are woven throughout, but Ernshaw doesn’t rely on clichés. Instead, she subverts expectations, making the "monsters" of the story not just external but internal. The commune’s members are neither villains nor victims; they are flawed, desperate people who have built a world where the rules of the outside no longer apply. This moral ambiguity makes the story far more compelling than a simple horror tale. It’s a story about the stories we tell ourselves to survive—and the cost of believing them.

Ernshaw’s writing is immersive, her descriptions vivid enough to make the mist-choked woods of Pastoral feel like a living entity. Her characters are deeply flawed, their motivations complex, and their relationships fraught with tension. Theo’s journey from reluctant participant to reluctant truth-seeker is particularly compelling, as is the dynamic between Calla and Bee, whose bond is both a shield and a source of conflict. The novel’s pacing is deliberate, allowing the dread to build slowly, like the creeping fog that seems to envelop the commune.

What makes *A History of Wild Places* truly stand out is its refusal to offer easy answers. The "mystery" isn’t just about what happened to Travis or Maggie St. James; it’s about the nature of memory, the power of narrative, and the ways in which we all construct our own versions of reality. Ernshaw doesn’t just tell a story; she invites the reader to question their own perceptions, to wonder how much of their own past they’ve sanitized for their own peace of mind.

In the end, *A History of Wild Places* is a chilling exploration of the places we go to escape—and the ways those places, in turn, escape us. It’s a novel that lingers in the mind long after the last page, a reminder that the darkest fairy tales are often the ones we tell ourselves.
