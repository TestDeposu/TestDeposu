# Faerie Tale

**Author:** Raymond E. Feist  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1988

When we think of Raymond E. Feist, our minds immediately wander to the sprawling, high-fantasy vistas of Midkemia, a realm built on traditional heroic tropes, epic warfare, and grand magics. Yet, with his 1988 standalone novel Faerie Tale, Feist proves that his narrative prowess cuts just as sharply when he turns his gaze away from the medieval and fixes it squarely on the contemporary world. This is not the sanitized, shimmering folklore of children's picture books; rather, Feist reaches back into the grim, primeval roots of European mythology to deliver a chilling, sophisticated dark fantasy that exposes the terrifying truth behind ancient legends.

The central theme of Faerie Tale pivots on the violent collision between modern suburban comfort and ancient, untamed malice. Phil Hastings, a successful screenwriter, believes he has struck gold by relocating his family to an idyllic estate in rural New York. It is the quintessential American dream, complete with money, a loving wife, and three children. However, Feist uses this domestic bliss as fertile ground for horror, demonstrating how quickly civilization peels away when confronted by primordial forces. The house and its surrounding woods are not merely a picturesque backdrop; they are a threshold. Through the Hastings family, Feist explores the vulnerability of modern man—blinded by rationalism and comfort—when forced to reckon with forces that predate humanity entirely. 

What makes the book compelling is Feist’s distinct authorial style. He masters the slow-burn transition from mundane realism to surreal dread. The exposition is rich and grounded, allowing readers to settle into the rhythms of the Hastings family life before subtly introducing the uncanny. When the supernatural finally breaches the family's reality, it arrives with a disorienting, suffocating weight. Feist injects the narrative with a heavy dose of psychological suspense and stylish sensuality, elevating the material above standard creature features. The entities that haunt the margins—the Bad Thing, the Faerie, and the enigmatic Fool—are not whimsical tricksters of modern invention. They are cruel, capricious, and driven by a cold, alien logic that defies human empathy. 

Furthermore, Feist brilliantly subverts the romanticized notion of the fairy tale. He reminds his audience that original folklore was designed to warn children away from dark woods and dangerous strangers, born from a primal fear of being hunted and consumed. In Faerie Tale, the woods are indeed deep and dark, and the ancient war resurrected by the family's intrusion is one humanity is woefully unequipped to fight. The prose is atmospheric, vividly painting the oppressive shadows of the forest and the suffocating terror of a nightmare made waking fact. 

Ultimately, Faerie Tale stands as a high-water mark for 1980s dark fantasy. It challenges the reader to look past the manicured lawns of contemporary life and remember the monsters that still wait in the dark. It is absorbing, thought-provoking, and deeply unsettling—a masterclass in modern horror that lingers long after the final page is turned. Read it, if you dare, but perhaps keep the lights on.
