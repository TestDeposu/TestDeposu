# Iphigenia in Aulis (Plays for Performance Series)

**Author:** Euripides  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

**A Bloodstained Sacrifice: Euripides’ *Iphigenia in Aulis* Reimagined in Merwin and Dimock’s Brilliant Translation**

Euripides’ *Iphigenia in Aulis* is not merely a tragedy—it is a searing indictment of the human capacity for cruelty, a play where the gods’ whims and mortal ambition collide in a storm of violence and moral bankruptcy. Now, in a fresh translation by W.S. Merwin and George E. Dimock Jr., this ancient Greek masterpiece resurfaces with a raw, modern urgency, stripping away the veneer of myth to expose the timeless horror of political expediency and the sacrifice of the innocent. This edition, part of the *Plays for Performance Series*, is more than a scholarly exercise; it is a visceral re-creation of Euripides’ unflinching critique of war, power, and the dehumanizing consequences of blind obedience.

At its core, *Iphigenia in Aulis* is a play about the cost of war—specifically, the cost of the Trojan War, which Euripides frames as a grotesque farce of ambition. The Greek army, led by Agamemnon, is trapped at Aulis, unable to set sail for Troy until the winds comply. The gods demand a sacrifice: Iphigenia, Agamemnon’s daughter, must be offered to Artemis to appease her wrath. What follows is not just a ritual murder but a descent into madness, where men—supposedly civilized—conspire to betray their own kin, their own humanity. The play’s genius lies in its refusal to romanticize heroism. Instead, Euripides lays bare the rot beneath the armor: the generals’ cowardice, the soldiers’ complicity, and the gods’ indifference. The translation by Merwin and Dimock captures this with a poetic precision that feels both ancient and urgently contemporary.

Merwin and Dimock’s approach is particularly striking. Their language is lyrical yet unflinching, avoiding the melodrama of some modern translations while retaining the emotional weight of Euripides’ original. The dialogue crackles with tension—Agamemnon’s justifications for the sacrifice sound like the hollow rhetoric of any warlord, while Iphigenia’s final moments are rendered with a haunting tenderness that makes the audience wince. The translation also excels in its handling of the chorus, which shifts between despair and defiance, mirroring the play’s broader themes of collective guilt and resistance. The introduction and notes provide essential context, but the real strength lies in the text itself, which feels alive, as if the play were being performed in the present moment.

Euripides’ critique of Greek culture is razor-sharp. The play doesn’t just condemn the Trojan War—it exposes the war machine itself as a monstrous cycle of vengeance and greed. Agamemnon, a man who once boasted of his wisdom, now grovels before the gods and his own men, his pride reduced to a pathetic plea for survival. The soldiers, who should be the protectors of their homeland, are revealed as a mob, their loyalty bought with promises of plunder. Even the gods are complicit, their demands serving as a convenient excuse for human malice. The translation doesn’t shy away from this darkness; instead, it amplifies it, making the audience confront the uncomfortable truth that little has changed since the time of Euripides.

What makes this edition particularly valuable is its accessibility. The glossary of mythical and geographical terms ensures that readers unfamiliar with Greek mythology can still follow the narrative, while the comprehensive notes offer deeper insights into Euripides’ political and philosophical context. Yet, the real power of the book lies in its immediacy. The themes—war, betrayal, the sacrifice of the innocent—resonate across centuries. In an era where political leaders still justify atrocities in the name of "necessity," where wars are waged on the backs of the vulnerable, *Iphigenia in Aulis* feels less like a relic of antiquity and more like a warning.

Merwin and Dimock’s translation is not just a faithful rendering of Euripides’ text; it is a reimagining that breathes new life into an ancient tragedy. It is a play that demands to be heard, not just read—one that should be performed, debated, and, above all, remembered. In a world where the line between sacrifice and slaughter is often blurred, Euripides’ *Iphigenia in Aulis* remains a necessary reminder of the price of war, and the cost of surrendering to the will of tyrants, whether they wear crowns or command armies.
