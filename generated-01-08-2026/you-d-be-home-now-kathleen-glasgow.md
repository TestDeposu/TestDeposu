# You'd Be Home Now

**Author:** Kathleen Glasgow  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Title: The Weight of Expectations: Kathleen Glasgow’s Striking Exploration of Recovery and Selfhood

In the landscape of contemporary young adult literature, few authors capture the raw, unvarnished agony of adolescent trauma with the piercing precision of Kathleen Glasgow. Following her critically acclaimed debut *Girl in Pieces*, Glasgow turns her formidable empathetic lens toward the opioid epidemic in her 2021 novel, *You’d Be Home Now*. Rather than viewing the crisis through the sterile lens of statistics or distant headlines, Glasgow plunges the reader directly into the suffocating intimacy of a small town drowning in grief, prescription pills, and silent complicity. The result is an impossibly moving, deeply human story that demands to be felt just as much as it is read.

At the heart of the narrative is Emory Ward, a teenager trapped inside a rigid casting call of familial and societal roles. To her affluent Michigan hometown of Mill Haven, she is the legacy—the great-great-granddaughter of the mill’s founder, born into a wealth that feels less like a privilege and more like a gilded cage. At school, she is merely Maddie Ward’s younger, less luminous sister. And within the fragile ecosystem of her own home, she is assigned the heaviest burden of all: the good girl, the eternal caretaker, the designated babysitter for her older brother, Joey. 

Emory’s carefully constructed existence fractures the night she and Joey are involved in a devastating car accident that claims the life of a local girl, Candy MontClaire. The tragedy acts as a brutal catalyst, ripping the veil off Joey’s severe opioid addiction and exposing the rot beneath Mill Haven’s picturesque facade. When the novel opens, four months have passed. Joey is tentatively returning home from rehab, and the town is still vibrating with judgment and pain. Everyone—from grieving parents to predatory gossips—is eager to assign blame, write the narrative, and force Emory right back into her old, accommodating box. 

Yet, Glasgow’s central thesis is that survival requires reinvention. As Emory navigates her junior year, she begins the agonizing process of shedding the identities others have mapped onto her. Through her evolving relationships—with a recovering brother who is far from "cured," a complex next-door neighbor, and the marginalized "ghostie" addicts who haunt the periphery of Mill Haven—Emory learns to look past the superficial labels that define her town. She realizes that people, much like communities, are layered, fractured, and desperately complex.

Glasgow’s literary style is the true anchor of the novel. Her prose is kinetic, lyrical, and intensely visceral. She writes with a rhythmic urgency that mirrors the chaotic, racing thoughts of a traumatized teenager. The structure of the book—punctuated by brief, poetic vignettes and stark, emotional truths—captures the feeling of walking on thin ice. Glasgow refuses to offer easy, Hollywood resolutions. Recovery is depicted not as a miraculous finish line, but as a daily, grueling negotiation with relapse, resentment, and forgiveness. The opioid crisis is not used merely as a dramatic backdrop; it is rendered as an insidious fog that alters the atmosphere, seeping into the floorboards of homes and poisoning the collective empathy of a town.

What makes *You’d Be Home Now* so striking is its profound compassion. Glasgow does not villainize those struggling with addiction, nor does she romanticize the collateral damage inflicted upon the families left in their wake. Instead, she shines a glaring, compassionate light on the invisible labor of caretakers and the heavy price of being labeled the strong one. 

Ultimately, Kathleen Glasgow has crafted a novel that is both a cautionary tale and a tender reclamation of self. It is a story about the courage it takes to step out of the shadows of other people's expectations and finally decide who you are. For anyone who has ever felt suffocated by the role they were assigned to play, *You’d Be Home Now* offers a heartbreaking, ultimately luminous reminder that home isn't a place dictated by others—it is a space where you are finally seen, flaws and all.
