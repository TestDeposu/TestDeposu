# Breath: The New Science of a Lost Art

**Author:** James Nestor  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

The way we breathe is a forgotten art, and modern humanity has largely lost the ability to do it correctly. This is the arresting premise at the heart of James Nestor’s bestselling exploration, Breath: The New Science of a Lost Art. Born out of a personal struggle with recurring respiratory ailments like bronchitis and pneumonia, Nestor’s investigation transforms from a desperate quest for personal healing into a fascinating, eye-opening exposé on human anatomy, evolutionary biology, and forgotten medical history. 

Nestor approaches his subject not as a dispassionate academic, but as an adventurous science journalist willing to subject his own body to extreme experimentation. Throughout the book, he teams up with researchers to test radical hypotheses—such as plugging his nose with silicone for ten days to study the detrimental physiological effects of mouth breathing. This hands-on, gonzo-journalism style gives the book a visceral, urgent energy. Readers are not just reading about science; they are experiencing the trials alongside the author, making complex anatomical concepts startlingly immediate and personal.

The central theme of Breath challenges the conventional wisdom of modern health. Nestor argues that somewhere along the evolutionary timeline—spurred by the industrial revolution, processed foods, and the resulting changes in our jaw structure—humans stopped breathing correctly. We over breathe, we breathe through our mouths instead of our noses, and we take shallow chest breaths that starve our cells of optimal oxygen. By drawing on ancient breathing practices like Pranayama, Tummo, and the methods of physical culturists like George Catlin, alongside cutting-edge pulmonology, Nestor bridges the gap between ancient mysticism and hard science. He demonstrates how conscious breathwork can alleviate sleep apnea, boost athletic performance, straighten crooked teeth, and fundamentally alter our autonomic nervous system.

What makes Nestor’s writing style so compelling is his ability to distill dense medical data and historical archives into a narrative page-turner. He introduces readers to eccentric characters—from punk-rock orthodontists to operatic breathing coaches—who have dedicated their lives to restoring this lost art. The prose is witty, accessible, and relentlessly engaging, stripping away clinical jargon without ever sacrificing intellectual rigor. 

In a world increasingly preoccupied with wellness, immune health, and stress management—especially in the shadow of global respiratory crises—Breath feels less like a mere recommendation and more like an essential manual for survival. Nestor reminds us that the most vital act we perform twenty-five thousand times a day is also the one we understand the least. By the final page, the reader is left with a profound sense of revelation and a newly sensitized awareness of every single inhalation and exhalation. It is a striking, transformative read that fundamentally changes how you inhabit your own body.
