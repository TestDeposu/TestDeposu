# Doctor Glas

**Author:** Hjalmar Söderberg  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2002

Doctor Glas, first published in 1905 and reintroduced to English‑speaking readers with an incisive introduction by Margaret Atwood, remains a startlingly modern meditation on the thin line between compassion and culpability. Hjalmar Söderberg’s slender novel packs a psychological punch that belies its modest length, offering a claustrophobic glimpse into the inner life of its titular protagonist—a city physician whose quiet routine is upended by a forbidden desire and a looming moral abyss.

At its core, Doctor Glas grapples with the ethics of intervention. The doctor’s revulsion toward the local pastor, a man whose piety masks a predatory sexuality, is not merely a matter of personal dislike; it becomes the catalyst for a contemplation of murder as a possible act of mercy. When the pastor’s young wife, haunted by her husband’s oppressive advances, seeks the doctor’s counsel, Glas finds himself torn between his professional oath to alleviate suffering and a burgeoning, almost erotic, fascination with the idea of eliminating the source of that suffering. Söderberg does not present this dilemma as a simple clash of good versus evil; instead, he immerses the reader in the doctor’s rationalizations, fantasies, and self‑loathing, forcing us to question whether any act born of compassion can ever be free of selfish motive.

The novel’s setting—fin‑de‑siècle Stockholm—functions as more than backdrop. Söderberg renders the city with a restrained, almost photographic precision: gaslit streets, austere interiors, the muffled hum of bourgeois respectability. This atmospheric detail amplifies the protagonist’s isolation; Glas wanders the same avenues day after day, his introspection mirrored in the city’s quiet, unyielding rhythm. The starkness of the prose mirrors the emotional sterility of his world, yet beneath the surface currents of repressed passion and violent impulse churn relentlessly.

Söderberg’s style is a hallmark of early psychological realism. He favors an economical, first‑person narrative that reads like a confidential diary, allowing the doctor’s voice to oscillate between clinical detachment and feverish confession. Sentences are often short, declarative, and devoid of ornamental flourish, yet they accumulate a cumulative tension that feels almost cinematic. This spare technique echoes the influence of August Strindberg’s interior dramas and anticipates the visual minimalism later championed by Ingmar Bergman: each line is a carefully framed shot, each paragraph a lingering close‑up on a twitching conscience.

What makes Doctor Glas especially compelling is its refusal to offer easy absolution. The doctor’s internal monologue is riddled with self‑justifications—he imagines the murder as a service to society, a way to free an innocent woman from tyranny—yet Söderberg constantly undercuts these rationalizations with flashes of guilt, fear, and the unsettling awareness that the act would also gratify his own repressed hostility. The novel thus becomes a study in the psychology of obsession: how a single fixation can warp perception, how moral boundaries blur when desire and duty intertwine, and how the mind can construct elaborate narratives to sanction the unsanctionable.

Margaret Atwood’s introduction situates the work within a broader literary tradition, noting its affinities with Balzac’s Eugénie Grandet and Henry James’s Washington Square while highlighting its distinctly Nordic timbre—an amalgam of naturalistic detail and existential dread that feels remarkably contemporary. Her commentary underscores the novel’s enduring relevance: the questions it raises about power, consent, and the limits of empathy resonate in today’s conversations about agency and abuse.

In sum, Doctor Glas is a masterclass in psychological intensity delivered through austerely beautiful prose. Söderberg compels us to inhabit the mind of a man teetering on the edge of action, urging us to examine our own capacities for justification and condemnation. The novel’s brevity belies its depth; it lingers long after the final page, a haunting reminder that the most dangerous battles are often fought silently within.
