"The Watchmaker of Filigree Street" opens with a quiet, almost domestic miracle: a gold pocket watch appearing on Thaniel Steepleton’s pillow, its ticking a promise that will later pull him from the devastation of a Scotland Yard bombing. Natasha Pulley’s debut novel immediately situates the reader in a Victorian London that feels both familiar and uncanny, a city where gas lamps flicker beside brass gears and where the boundaries between science, superstition, and craftsmanship blur. The central theme that threads through the narrative is the negotiation of time—not merely as a measurable quantity but as a fluid, personal experience shaped by memory, choice, and the hidden mechanisms that govern our lives. Pulley treats time as both a literal object (the intricate watchworks of Keita Mori) and a metaphor for the ways individuals negotiate their pasts, their loyalties, and the inevitable march toward an uncertain future.

From the outset, Pulley’s style is marked by a lyrical precision that echoes the ticking of Mori’s creations. Her sentences are measured, often employing a restrained cadence that mirrors the deliberate motions of a clockmaker’s hands. Yet within this restraint she allows moments of luminous description—“the fog clung to the cobblestones like a half‑remembered dream”—to surface, giving the novel a texture that is both tactile and ethereal. This balance of restraint and lyricism serves the novel’s dual focus on historical veracity and magical intrusion. Pulley researches the period with evident care: the smog‑filled streets of London, the rigid hierarchies of the Metropolitan Police, the nascent fervor of Japanese immigration after the Meiji Restoration, and the intellectual ferment of Oxford’s physics laboratories are rendered with a historian’s eye. Yet she never lets the detail overwhelm the story; instead, the historical scaffolding becomes a stage upon which her fantastical elements—most notably the clockwork octopus that watches over Thaniel—can perform with uncanny plausibility.

The motif of the clockwork octopus is more than a whimsical gadget; it embodies the novel’s exploration of hybridity. Originating from Mori’s Japanese heritage yet fabricated in the smoky workshops of London, the creature signals a synthesis of East and West, tradition and innovation. Pulley uses this hybrid to interrogate the immigrant experience: Mori, a gentle, lonely watchmaker, carries the weight of a homeland in flux, his craft a silent testament to a culture that values patience, precision, and an almost spiritual attunement to the passage of time. In contrast, Thaniel represents the grounded, pragmatic Englishman whose loyalty to duty and reason is continually tested by the inexplicable grace of Mori’s inventions. Their friendship becomes a conduit for examining how trust is built across cultural divides when the underlying mechanisms of each world remain partially opaque.

Grace Carrow, the Oxford physicist, introduces a second axis of tension: the clash between empirical rationality and the wonder that defies measurement. Pulley treats Grace not merely as a love interest or a foil but as a embodiment of the era’s scientific optimism—her belief that every phenomenon can be reduced to equations and observable data. When Grace’s experiments inadvertently disturb Mori’s delicate temporal devices, the narrative thrusts the protagonists into a moral quandary: should they protect the mysterious, potentially dangerous knowledge Mori harbors, or submit to the enlightened imperative of disclosure? Pulley handles this dilemma with subtlety, allowing the reader to feel the weight of each character’s convictions without resorting to didacticism. The result is a nuanced meditation on the limits of knowledge and the ethical responsibility that accompanies discovery.

A recurring stylistic device in Pulley’s prose is the use of sensory detail to anchor the fantastical. The scent of oil on brass, the faint chime of a hidden gear, the taste of green tea served in a cramped flat—these minutiae ground the reader in the characters’ immediate experiences, making the surreal moments feel earned rather than arbitrary. When the clockwork octopus unfurls its metallic limbs across a fog‑laden alley, the description is both precise (“each joint articulated with the delicacy of a watchspring”) and evocative (“its eyes glimmered like polished onyx, reflecting the gaslight in fractured shards”). This attention to the tactile world reinforces the novel’s central idea that time, though abstract, is always felt through the body and the objects we inhabit.

Pulley’s narrative pacing mirrors the slow, inexorable turn of a mainspring. Early chapters unfold with a deliberate, almost meditative rhythm, allowing the reader to acclimate to the atmospheric setting and the subtle oddities that pepper Thaniel’s day‑to‑day life. As the plot accelerates—triggered by the Scotland Yard explosion and the subsequent revelations about Mori’s past—the prose tightens, sentences gaining a slight urgency that mimics the ticking of a watch winding down to a critical moment.
