# Take a Hint, Dani Brown (The Brown Sisters, #2)

**Author:** Talia Hibbert  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

Take a Hint, Dani Brown crackles with the kind of smart, sexy energy that has become Talia Hibbert’s trademark. The second installment in the Brown Sisters series throws us into the world of Danika “Dani” Brown—a bisexual PhD candidate who has deliberately shelved romance in favor of academic glory and occasional, no‑strings‑attached hookups. When a clumsy fire‑drill leaves her stranded in the office building, brooding security guard Zafir Ansari swoops in like a knight in a high‑visibility vest, and a spontaneous video of the rescue rockets to viral fame. Overnight, the internet dubs them #DrRugbae, and Zaf, desperate for publicity for his youth sports charity, convinces Dani to stage a fake relationship.

What could have been a routine faux‑mance premise blossoms into a nuanced exploration of how we protect ourselves from vulnerability. Dani’s initial request—a friend‑with‑benefits who knows the score and the bedroom—feels like a defensive maneuver, a way to keep intimacy transactional and therefore safe. Hibbert flips that script by letting Zaf’s quiet, earnest romanticism seep through Dani’s armor. Their banter crackles with wit, yet beneath the laughter lies a genuine tension: Dani’s fear that love will derail her career, and Zaf’s struggle to reconcile his public persona as a stoic ex‑rugby player with his private yearning for emotional openness.

Hibbert’s style shines in the way she balances humor with heart. Dialogue snaps—Dani’s sarcastic asides are razor‑sharp, while Zaf’s gruff charm is delivered in low, endearing murmurs. The narrative never lingers too long on exposition; instead, it trusts the reader to infer backstory through small gestures—a lingering glance, a shared joke over lukewarm coffee, the way Dani’s fingers trace the spine of a textbook when she’s nervous. This economy of language makes the novel feel brisk without sacrificing depth; every scene serves dual purpose, advancing plot while deepening character.

Representation is woven seamlessly into the fabric of the story. Dani’s bisexuality is presented matter‑of‑factly, free from the tropes of confusion or experimentation that often plague queer characters in romance. Her attraction to both men and women is simply part of who she is, and the novel treats her sexuality with the same respect it gives her ambition. Likewise, Zaf’s British‑Pakistani heritage informs his worldview without becoming a caricature; his family’s expectations, his community’s pride in his charitable work, and his own internalized pressure to be “strong” add layers to his emotional barriers.

The fake‑dating trope, a staple of contemporary romance, feels fresh here because Hibbert uses it as a mirror rather than a mere plot device. The publicity stunt forces Dani and Zaf to confront the dissonance between their public personas and private desires. As the charade deepens, the line between performance and authenticity blurs, prompting both characters to ask: What am I really protecting myself from? And, more importantly, what am I willing to risk for a chance at genuine connection?

Sex scenes are rendered with a refreshing candor that never feels gratuitous. Hibbert writes intimacy as an extension of emotional truth—each encounter reveals another facet of Dani’s guardedness and Zaf’s longing to be seen beyond his musculature. The chemistry is palpable, but it’s the emotional vulnerability that lingers after the pages turn.

Ultimately, Take a Hint, Dani Brown is a celebration of letting go of the self‑imposed scripts that keep us safe but stagnant. Hibbert’s voice—sharp, compassionate, and unapologetically feminist—invites readers to laugh, swoon, and perhaps reconsider their own “hints” about love and ambition. It’s a romance that satisfies the craving for steamy fun while delivering a thoughtful reminder that the most rewarding relationships often begin when we stop pretending and start listening.
