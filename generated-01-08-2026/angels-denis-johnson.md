# Angels

**Author:** Denis Johnson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

Denis Johnson’s 2007 National Book Award-winning novel, *Tree of Smoke*, stands as a monumental achievement in contemporary American literature—a sprawling, hallucinatory descent into the moral quagmire of the Vietnam War. Though the provided synopsis references *Tree of Smoke*, it introduces the book through its iconic framing and characters, specifically Skip Sands, a CIA spy-in-training navigating the psychological labyrinth of the conflict, and the drifting Houston brothers. Johnson, celebrated for his unflinching portrayals of America’s disenfranchised, takes the grand canvas of the Vietnam War and shrinks it down to the raw, pulsating nerve endings of individual human desperation. 

At the heart of the novel lies a devastating exploration of identity and disillusionment. The narrative’s opening confession—tracing the protagonist’s devolution from the idealistic "Wise American" to the crushing realization of being merely the "F*****g American"—encapsulates the overarching theme of the book: the erosion of national innocence. Johnson strips away the geopolitical abstraction of the Cold War and the Vietnam conflict, replacing it with a fog of disinformation, delusion, and existential dread. The war is not merely a historical event here; it is a spiritual wasteland where characters search frantically for meaning, redemption, or simply an end to their profound loneliness. Whether they seek solace in sex, violence, religious fervor, or the ultimate release of death, Johnson’s characters are united by a desperate yearning for grace in a world that has fundamentally broken.

Stylistically, Johnson writes with a gritty, unsparing lyricism that is entirely his own. His prose is both brutal and tender, capable of capturing the harsh glare of the Arizona desert or the oppressive humidity of Southeast Asia with equal vividness. There is a cinematic quality to his pacing, moving effortlessly from high-stakes intelligence operations to quiet, devastating moments of human connection and failure. Johnson avoids the traditional traps of war fiction—there is no glorification, no neat moral arc, and no comforting resolution. Instead, he embraces ambiguity, reflecting the chaotic, unfathomable nature of the era he depicts. 

*Tree of Smoke* is not an easy read, but it is an essential one. It demands patience and emotional investment, rewarding the reader with a visceral portrait of a nation losing its soul. Denis Johnson doesn't just tell a story about the Vietnam War; he holds up a cracked mirror to the American psyche, exposing the tragic gap between our highest ideals and our basest realities. It remains a towering, unforgettable masterpiece of modern fiction.
