# The Reformatory

**Author:** Tananarive Due  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2023

Tananarive Due’s *The Reformatory* is a masterclass in historical horror, a searing monument to historical trauma, and a deeply emotional story of survival. Set in the Jim Crow South of 1950s Florida, this award-winning novel is both a ghost story and a harrowing reckoning with American history. Rooted in the tragic legacy of the notorious Dozier School for Boys—a real-life institution where Black children suffered unimaginable cruelties—Due has crafted a work that vibrates with righteous fury, profound empathy, and supernatural dread.

At the heart of the novel is twelve-year-old Robbie Stephens, Jr. Banished to the Gracetown School for Boys—the titular reformatory—for the simple crime of defending his older sister against a wealthy white boy, Robbie enters a literal chamber of horrors. The reformatory is a place where young Black boys are worked to the bone, beaten mercilessly, and subjected to the casual, sadistic cruelty of a racist system designed to erase them. But Robbie possesses a unique and terrifying gift: he has always been able to see haints, the lingering spirits of the dead. 

While the living boys at the reformatory face daily brutalities, it is the dead who tell the true, unvarnished story of the school. The haints whisper of those who went missing, of bodies buried in the dense Florida brush, and of the systemic evil that pervades every square inch of the grounds. Through the guidance of fellow inmates Redbone and Blue, Robbie learns the agonizing rules of survival. Simultaneously, back in the real world, his fierce sister Gloria refuses to let him become just another statistic, launching a relentless campaign to bring him home before it is too late.

Due’s literary style is nothing short of transcendent. She effortlessly bridges the gap between historical fiction and supernatural horror, proving that the ghosts of our past are often far less terrifying than the living people who created them. Her prose is deceptively accessible yet relentlessly gripping, drawing the reader into the sweltering, oppressive heat of Jim Crow Florida with visceral sensory details. She handles the heavy subject matter with immense grace, refusing to exploit the trauma of her characters, but rather honoring their resilience, humanity, and deep bonds of kinship. 

The main theme of *The Reformatory* explores the weight of memory and the imperative duty to bear witness. By weaving folklore and the supernatural into a meticulously researched historical framework, Due confronts the ghosts of America’s racial history head-on. She shows how institutional violence echoes across generations and argues that true justice requires remembering those whom society has tried to erase. The haints in the novel are not merely cheap horror tropes; they are symbols of stolen futures, speaking truth to power from beyond the grave.

Stephen King called it a book you cannot put down, and Joe Hill hailed it as a masterpiece. They are not exaggerating. *The Reformatory* will break your heart before it fills you with awe. It is a stunning, unforgettable triumph from a master storyteller at the absolute peak of her powers, ensuring that the forgotten boys of the past finally have their stories told.
