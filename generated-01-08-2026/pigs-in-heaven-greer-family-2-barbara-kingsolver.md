# Pigs in Heaven (Greer Family, #2)

**Author:** Barbara Kingsolver  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Pigs in Heaven picks up where The Bean Trees left off, thrusting Taylor Greer and her fiercely observant six‑year‑old daughter, Turtle, back into the tangled currents of love, loss, and belonging. When Turtle witnesses a miraculous rescue at Hoover Dam, her innocent certainty becomes the catalyst that pulls her mother, their eclectic circle of friends, and the wider Cherokee community into a struggle that stretches from the dusty backroads of Kentucky to the sun‑baked streets of Tucson and finally to the sacred grounds of Heaven, Oklahoma. The novel’s core theme is the elasticity of family—how bonds are forged, tested, and re‑defined across blood, law, and culture. Kingsolver asks what it means to be a parent when the state, the tribe, and the heart all lay claim to a child, and she answers with a nuanced portrait that refuses easy villains or saintly heroes.

Kingsolver’s style is both lyrical and grounded, a hallmark that lets her swing from vivid, almost cinematic descriptions of the Southwest landscape to intimate, dialogue‑driven scenes that feel like overheard kitchen conversations. She weaves multiple narrative strands—legal proceedings, tribal politics, personal memoirs—into a tapestry that never feels forced; each shift in perspective deepens the reader’s understanding of the characters’ motivations. Her prose is accessible yet layered, peppered with humor that softens the weight of the subject matter while never undermining its gravity. The result is a novel that reads like a heartfelt conversation with a wise friend who knows exactly when to linger on a detail and when to let the story sweep you forward.

What makes Pigs in Heaven resonate long after the final page is its refusal to simplify the complexities of identity and allegiance. Kingsolver treats the Cherokee Nation not as a backdrop but as a living, breathing presence whose traditions, laws, and spiritual worldview intersect with the protagonists’ lives in ways that are both enlightening and unsettling. The novel’s title itself—a wry nod to the idea of “heaven” as a place of hope and a satirical comment on the absurdities of bureaucratic “salvation”—encapsulates the book’s balance of earnestness and wit.

In short, Pigs in Heaven is a compelling continuation of Taylor and Turtle’s journey, showcasing Kingsolver’s gift for blending social critique with deeply human storytelling. It challenges readers to expand their notions of family, to listen to the quiet truths children often see more clearly than adults, and to appreciate the redemptive power of love that persists even when the odds are stacked against it.
