# Remarkably Bright Creatures

**Author:** Shelby Van Pelt  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

Shelby Van Pelt’s debut novel, Remarkably Bright Creatures, arrives as a refreshing balm for readers weary of cynicism, offering a masterclass in how to construct a narrative about grief without letting it curdle into despair. At its core, the novel examines the profound architecture of loneliness—how it isolates us, how we build fortresses of routine to keep it at bay, and, most importantly, how easily those walls can be cracked open by the most unexpected of connections. 

The story anchors itself around Tova Sullivan, a widow whose life is rigidly defined by nocturnal labor and decades-old absence. Ever since her eighteen-year-old son, Erik, vanished mysteriously on a boat in Puget Sound over thirty years ago, Tova has coped the only way she knows how: by keeping busy, cleaning the floors of the Sowell Bay Aquarium, and burying her accumulated sorrows beneath an impenetrable veneer of stoicism. 

Enter Marcellus. A giant Pacific octopus residing in a tank at the aquarium, Marcellus is not merely a clever set piece or a whimsical literary device. Van Pelt boldly grants him a distinct, highly perceptive narrative voice—a curmudgeon of the deep who views his human captors with a mixture of detached amusement and mild disdain. Marcellus has a lifespan measured in months rather than decades, a keen intellect, and a profound capacity for observation. When his path crosses with Tova’s, a silent, cross-species kinship blooms, anchored by midnight cleaning shifts, shared silences, and a mutual understanding of confinement. 

What makes Van Pelt’s stylistic approach so striking is the delicate balancing act she performs between the whimsical and the deeply grounded. Giving a cephalopod a point of view is a high-risk literary gamble that could easily veer into the absurd or overly saccharine. Yet, Van Pelt anchors Marcellus’s perspective with a dry, observational wit that grounds the fantasy in emotional truth. He is cynical, observant, and fiercely loyal in his own peculiar way. Through his eyes, human absurdities are laid bare, and Tova’s quiet grief is elevated from a private tragedy to something witnessed, acknowledged, and ultimately challenged.

Van Pelt’s prose is characterized by its gentle momentum and understated grace. For fans of character-driven contemporary fiction in the vein of A Man Called Ove, the novel offers a similar brand of grumpy-yet-tender redemption. There is an economy to Van Pelt’s writing style that serves the material well; she avoids melodramatic flourishes, trusting the quiet accumulation of detail to carry the emotional weight. The pacing mirrors the rhythmic, nocturnal flow of Tova’s life—measured, methodical, and occasionally disrupted by currents she never saw coming.

As Marcellus acts as an unlikely detective, piecing together the fractured history of the night Erik disappeared, the novel shifts from a quiet character study into a compelling exploration of reckoning and hope. Van Pelt reminds us that the past, no matter how securely locked away in the vaults of memory, demands to be examined if we are ever to inhabit the future. The mystery of the vanishing son provides the narrative drive, but the true beating heart of the book remains the transcendent bond between a grieving human and a brilliant, captive invertebrate.

Remarkably Bright Creatures succeeds because it dares to find profound empathy in unlikely places. It is a testament to Shelby Van Pelt’s skill that she can make a reader care as deeply for an octopus as she does for her human protagonists. Ultimately, the novel serves as a gentle, stirring reminder that healing often begins not with grand gestures, but with the slightest, most unexpected touch from another living thing.
