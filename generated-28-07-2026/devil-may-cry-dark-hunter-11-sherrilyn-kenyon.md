# Devil May Cry (Dark-Hunter, #11)

**Author:** Sherrilyn Kenyon  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

Sherrilyn Kenyon’s Devil May Cry, the eleventh installment in her phenomenal Dark-Hunter series, proves once again why she remains an undisputed powerhouse of paranormal romance. Published in 2007, this searing, action-packed novel takes readers deeper into the mythological shadows, blending high-stakes apocalyptic dread with the intoxicating, sharp-tongued chemistry that has defined Kenyon’s career. At its core, the book explores a profound and deeply human theme: the agony of chronic betrayal and the terrifying, redemptive leap of faith required to love after your world has been shattered. 

The narrative centers on Sin, an ancient Sumerian god stripped of his divinity and left for dead by the fickle goddess Artemis. For millennia, Sin’s existence has been fueled by a singular, consuming fire: absolute, unforgiving vengeance. Kenyon brilliantly uses Sin to examine the psychological weight of trauma. He is a man—and an immortal—who knows only betrayal, building impenetrable walls around his soul to survive. Yet, Kenyon’s signature style shines brightest when she strips her larger-than-life characters down to their raw, vulnerable cores. Sin’s journey is not merely about clawing back his lost power; it is about the agonizing dismantling of his defenses when the fate of humanity demands that he trust the very type of person most likely to destroy him.

Enter the female lead, whose identity and sharp edge keep both Sin and the reader on high alert. The enemies-to-lovers dynamic is executed with Kenyon’s trademark razor-sharp dialogue, relentless pacing, and palpable sexual tension. She masterfully weaves ancient mythologies into a contemporary, gritty landscape, ensuring that the supernatural elements never feel dusty or academic. Instead, the Sumerian lore breathes with urgent, terrifying life, particularly through the resurrection of the lethal gallu—demons hungry for human flesh and bent on biblical annihilation. 

Kenyon’s prose is fast, muscular, and unapologetically emotional. She balances laugh-out-loud, snarky banter with moments of profound, gut-wrenching anguish. The world-building is expansive, yet it never bogs down the central romance or the frantic urgency of the plot. As Sin discovers that the woman threatening to kill him has instead inadvertently stolen his heart, the narrative raises the emotional stakes to a fever pitch. Will she save him, or deliver him to the monsters? That delicious ambiguity drives the pages forward at a breathless speed.

Ultimately, Devil May Cry is a triumph of urban fantasy and paranormal romance. It delivers everything series fans crave—dark heroes, fierce heroines, high-octane battle sequences, and a scorching romance born in the fires of impending doom. Kenyon proves that even characters who have been broken beyond repair can still find a reason to fight for tomorrow. For readers willing to dive into the dark, sensual, and dangerous underworld of the Dark-Hunters, this novel is an unforgettable, pulse-pounding ride.
