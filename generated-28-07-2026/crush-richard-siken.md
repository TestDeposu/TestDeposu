# Crush

**Author:** Richard Siken  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

**War of the Foxes: Richard Siken's Masterful Exploration of Creation and Perception**

Richard Siken's long-awaited second collection, "War of the Foxes," is a dazzling exploration of the human psyche, art, and the intricate dance between the two. Siken, whose debut "Crush" was hailed as a masterpiece, returns with a work that is both deeply introspective and universally resonant.

At the heart of "War of the Foxes" lies a profound interrogation of creation and representation. Siken, a poet, painter, and filmmaker, draws on his multidisciplinary background to weave a tapestry of poems that are as much about the act of making art as they are about the human condition. He asks, in his signature cinematic style, "Why paint a bird?" and in doing so, invites us to question our own motivations, our desires, and our perceptions.

Siken's territory, as the New York Times noted, is where passion and eloquence collide and fuse. His poems are not merely words on a page; they are experiences, visceral and immediate. He paints with language, creating vivid, often disorienting landscapes that reflect the chaos and beauty of the human mind.

The collection opens with "The Museum," a poem that sets the stage for the explorations to come. Two lovers stand before a painting, one lost in its gaze, the other impatient. The moment captures the essence of Siken's project: the tension between the subjective experience of art and the objective reality of its creation.

Throughout "War of the Foxes," Siken uses a variety of forms and techniques, from spare, haunting lyrics to sprawling, narrative-driven poems. Yet, his voice remains consistent: urgent, unflinching, and deeply human. He writes about love, desire, violence, and eroticism with a cinematic brilliance and urgency, as the Huffington Post noted. But he also writes about the mundane, the everyday, imbuing it with a profound sense of wonder and longing.

Siken's poems are not easy. They demand active engagement, a willingness to be disoriented, to be challenged. But they reward that engagement with moments of stunning clarity and beauty. They are, as Library Journal noted, "slippery, magnetic riffs" that complicate and deepen our understanding of the world.

In "War of the Foxes," Siken turns his gaze inward, interrogating not just the world but also his own role as an artist. He writes about the struggle to create, the doubt, the fear, the desperation. But he also writes about the joy, the exhilaration, the sense of purpose that comes from making something new.

"War of the Foxes" is a book about looking, about seeing, about the ways we create meaning from the world around us. It is a book about art, yes, but it is also a book about being human. It is a book that will haunt you, as NPR promised, with its beauty, its honesty, its unflinching gaze.
