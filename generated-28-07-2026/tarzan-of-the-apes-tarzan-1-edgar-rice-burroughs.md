# Tarzan of the Apes (Tarzan, #1)

**Author:** Edgar Rice Burroughs  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Wild, untamed, and relentlessly gripping, Edgar Rice Burroughs’ 1912 masterpiece Tarzan of the Apes remains a towering monument of early adventure fiction. Originally serialized in the pulp pages of All-Story Magazine before its enduring life in print, this foundational text is far more than a boy’s adventure story. It is a primal psychological exploration wrapped in the skin of a high-octane jungle epic, posing timeless questions about what it truly means to be civilized.

At the heart of the narrative lies Burroughs’ most arresting theme: the eternal clash between nature and nurture, savagery and civilization. Abandoned in the perilous African jungle as an infant following a brutal mutiny, the orphaned son of Lord and Lady Greystoke is adopted by a tribe of great apes and named Tarzan—white skin, gray eyes, and all. Burroughs constructs a fascinating juxtaposition between the feral existence of the beasts and the stiff, hypocritical veneer of Edwardian human society. Tarzan grows into an apex predator, possessing the physical perfection of a Greek god and the lethal instincts of a beast. Yet, deep within his bloodline, a mysterious nobility and an innate intellectual curiosity persist. When he eventually encounters civilized humans, Burroughs uses Tarzan as a mirror to reflect the hypocrisy, greed, and cruelty of the so-called civilized world, famously suggesting that the jungle is often more honest in its brutality than the drawing rooms of London.

Burroughs’ literary style is the pulsing engine that drives this classic forward. Writing in the breathless, action-oriented tradition of early twentieth-century pulp fiction, he relies on relentless pacing and high-stakes plotting. There is little room for quiet introspection; instead, the prose vibrates with visceral energy, cinematic descriptions of dense jungles, and heart-pounding confrontations with lions, apes, and hostile native tribes. While modern readers might occasionally find the prose melodramatic or culturally dated—reflecting the colonial attitudes of its era—Burroughs' storytelling craftsmanship cannot be denied. He possesses a rare myth-making ability, elevating Tarzan from a mere character into a universal archetype of the wild man, a romanticized fantasy of absolute freedom and physical mastery.

Tarzan of the Apes is a breathless sprint through the primal subconscious. It challenges our definitions of humanity, thrills the senses, and delivers an unforgettable cinematic reading experience that established a pop-culture icon for the ages.
