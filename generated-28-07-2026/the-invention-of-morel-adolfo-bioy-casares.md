**Page Count:** Unknown

**A Masterful Metaphysical Enigma: Adolfo Bioy Casares' "The Invention of Morel"**

Adolfo Bioy Casares' "The Invention of Morel" (2026) is a literary gem that sparkles with the allure of a perfect novel, as aptly described by Octavio Paz. This new translation by Margaret Jull Costa allows English readers to fully appreciate the Argentine master's metaphysical prowess, echoing the brilliance of Henry James' "The Turn of the Screw" and Jules Verne's "Journey to the Center of the Earth," as lauded by Jorge Luis Borges.

The narrative unfolds on a seemingly deserted island, where a fugitive writer finds refuge. However, the island's eerie inhabitants—well-heeled tourists with nightly rituals—challenge his understanding of reality. Casares masterfully weaves a tale that is as bewildering as it is captivating, drawing the reader into a labyrinth of perception and consciousness.

Casares' style is deceptively simple, yet profoundly evocative. He employs precise, unadorned language to convey the protagonist's disorientation and growing obsession with a mysterious woman. The author's fascination with Louise Brooks is palpable, as the enigmatic beauty haunts the pages, embodying the story's metaphysical core.

"The Invention of Morel" is not merely a suspenseful tale; it is a profound exploration of reality, memory, and the self. Casares' narrative anticipates the philosophical dilemmas of virtual reality, making this 1940 novel eerily prescient. The story's open-ended conclusion invites introspection, leaving readers to grapple with the unsettling possibility that our perceptions may not reflect objective truth.

Inspired by Casares' work, Alain Resnais and Alain Robbe-Grillet's "Last Year at Marienbad" forever changed cinema. Now, Costa's translation ensures that "The Invention of Morel" continues to influence and inspire, resonating with readers across generations.
