# Dealing with Dragons (Enchanted Forest Chronicles, #1)

**Author:** Patricia C. Wrede  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

A Delightful Subversion of Fairy Tale Tropes: A Review of Patricia C. Wrede’s Dealing with Dragons

For generations, fairy tales have served up a predictable menu of damsels in distress waiting passively for dashing princes to save the day. Enter Cimorene, a royal protagonist with zero interest in embroidery, proper curtseying, or marrying dull monarchs. Patricia C. Wrede’s timeless 1985 classic—re-released with a fresh authorial introduction—shatters these archaic conventions with humor, wit, and a brilliant reimagining of fantasy tropes. Dealing with Dragons, the sparkling first installment of the Enchanted Forest Chronicles, invites readers into a world where princesses rescue themselves and dragons defy patriarchal expectations.

At the heart of Wrede’s narrative is a magnificent thematic subversion. Cimorene is not merely a rebellious teenager; she is a pragmatist with a fierce appetite for independence and practical skills. Unimpressed by the rigid expectations of her kingdom, she deliberately runs away to become a dragon’s princess. Yet, this is no kidnapping in the traditional sense. Cimorene volunteers for the job, trading the suffocating boredom of royal court life for the intellectually stimulating, if hazardous, occupation of managing a dragon's household. 

Wrede uses this premise to brilliantly deconstruct fantasy genre conventions. Dragons are not mindless beasts to be slain by wandering knights; instead, they are bureaucratic, highly intelligent creatures navigating complex political landscapes. Kazul, the dragon Cimorene chooses to serve, is a compelling feminist icon in her own right, campaigning fiercely to become the king of the dragons in a system resistant to female leadership. Through Cimorene and Kazul, Wrede crafts a powerful commentary on agency, competence, and breaking free from societal pigeonholes. Being female, Wrede gently reminds us, is no barrier to ambition, whether that ambition involves mastering Latin, organizing a massive treasure hoard, or ascending to a throne.

The author’s style is one of the novel's greatest triumphs. Wrede writes with a light, sparkling touch that balances high-stakes adventure with sly, laugh-out-loud humor. The prose is deceptively smooth, effortlessly drawing younger readers in while offering enough clever winks and satirical nods to entertain adults. Rather than relying on heavy-handed moralizing, the feminist undertones of the story are woven seamlessly into the fabric of the plot. The empowerment here is earned through Cimorene's resourcefulness, intelligence, and her refusal to conform to stereotypes. She learns to fireproof herself, deals diplomatically with pesky, arrogant princes who insist on rescuing her against her will, and solves magical mysteries with a cool head and a sharp tongue.

Dealing with Dragons is a triumph of imagination and charm. It treats its audience with respect, presenting a protagonist who is tough-minded, capable, and entirely unforgettable. Whether you are revisiting the Enchanted Forest as an adult or discovering Cimorene’s adventures for the very first time, Wrede’s delightful masterpiece remains a shining beacon of subverted expectations. It is a brisk, highly entertaining read that proves happily ever after is something you build for yourself.
