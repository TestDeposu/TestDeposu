# The Little Sister (Philip Marlowe, #5)

**Author:** Raymond Chandler  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1988

In "The Little Sister," Raymond Chandler masterfully weaves a complex web of intrigue, deception, and corruption, set against the backdrop of the glamorous yet seedy underbelly of 1940s Hollywood. This iconic novel, first published in 1949, not 1988, marks Chandler's first foray into the world of Tinseltown, and it's a scathing critique of the industry's superficiality and the darker forces that drive it.

At the center of the novel is Philip Marlowe, Chandler's quintessential urban private eye, who is drawn into a world of movie stars, gangsters, and blackmailers when a young woman named Orfamay Quest walks into his office. Orfamay's sister, a movie starlet, has gone missing, and Orfamay is determined to find her. As Marlowe delves deeper into the case, he becomes embroiled in a complex network of secrets, lies, and deceit that threatens to destroy him.

Chandler's prose is, as always, a marvel of style and sophistication. His writing is a perfect blend of witty banter, vivid descriptions, and philosophical musings, which creates a sense of atmosphere and mood that's unparalleled in crime fiction. His depiction of Los Angeles as a city of broken dreams and shattered illusions is both poignant and prescient, and his portrayal of the Hollywood elite as a bunch of self-absorbed, narcissistic poseurs is both biting and hilarious.

One of the most striking aspects of "The Little Sister" is its exploration of the theme of identity. Marlowe, as always, is a man of mystery and ambiguity, a loner who operates on the fringes of society. But as he navigates the complex web of relationships and alliances in the novel, he begins to question his own identity and sense of purpose. Is he a detective, a knight-errant, or just a cynical opportunist? Chandler's exploration of these questions is both nuanced and profound, and it adds depth and complexity to the novel.

The characters in "The Little Sister" are, as always, a diverse and fascinating bunch. From the enigmatic and alluring Orfamay Quest to the sinister and menacing gangster, Steelgrave, each character is meticulously crafted to reveal the darker aspects of human nature. Chandler's portrayal of women, in particular, is noteworthy for its time, as he depicts them as strong, independent, and complex individuals, rather than mere objects of desire or damsels in distress.

In conclusion, "The Little Sister" is a masterpiece of crime fiction that showcases Chandler's unique style and thematic concerns. Its exploration of identity, corruption, and the human condition is both timeless and timely, and its depiction of 1940s Hollywood is a fascinating and scathing critique of the industry. If you're a fan of crime fiction, noir, or simply great writing, then "The Little Sister" is a must-read.
