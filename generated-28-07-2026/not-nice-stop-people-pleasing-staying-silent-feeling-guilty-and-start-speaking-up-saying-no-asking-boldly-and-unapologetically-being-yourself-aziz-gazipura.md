# Not Nice: Stop People Pleasing, Staying Silent, & Feeling Guilty... And Start Speaking Up, Saying No, Asking Boldly, And Unapologetically Being Yourself

**Author:** Aziz Gazipura  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

Not Nice: Stop People Pleasing, Staying Silent, & Feeling Guilty… And Start Speaking Up, Saying No, Asking Boldly, And Unapologetically Being Yourself tackles one of the most pervasive yet quietly destructive habits of modern life: the compulsion to accommodate others at the expense of one’s own well‑being. Aziz Gazipura, a clinical psychologist known for his direct, no‑nonsense approach to confidence and social anxiety, frames people‑pleasing not as a personality flaw but as a learned survival strategy that, over time, erodes authenticity, fuels resentment, and stifles personal growth. The book’s central thesis is simple and compelling: reclaiming voice and boundary‑setting is not selfish; it is the foundation of genuine connection and lasting self‑respect.

Gazipura’s style mirrors the book’s message—blunt, conversational, and peppered with real‑world vignettes that feel like therapy sessions transcribed onto the page. He avoids jargon, opting instead for plain language that makes psychological concepts accessible to readers who might be intimidated by more academic self‑help tomes. Each chapter opens with a relatable scenario—say, agreeing to extra work despite burnout, or smiling through a hurtful comment to keep the peace—before dissecting the underlying fear (often of rejection, conflict, or being perceived as “difficult”). He then walks the reader through a step‑by‑step process: identifying the automatic “yes” reflex, pausing to check in with one’s true desire, rehearsing a courteous but firm refusal, and finally reflecting on the outcome. These practical exercises are reinforced with short worksheets, journal prompts, and “challenge” actions that encourage immediate application, turning insight into habit.

One of the book’s strongest assets is its emphasis on self‑compassion. Rather than shaming readers for past people‑pleasing, Gazipura normalizes the behavior as a coping mechanism rooted in early conditioning—whether from overly critical parents, cultural expectations, or past trauma. By framing the shift toward assertiveness as a gradual rewiring of neural pathways, he reduces the pressure to be perfect and encourages a mindset of experimentation. This tone is both empowering and disarming; readers feel seen, not judged, which is crucial when tackling a topic that often triggers guilt and shame.

The author also excels at linking internal change to external impact. He argues that when individuals honor their own limits, they model healthier relational dynamics for others, thereby improving the quality of their friendships, romantic partnerships, and workplace interactions. The book does not promise that saying no will instantly solve all interpersonal problems; instead, it acknowledges that initial discomfort is normal and that mastery comes with repeated practice. Gazipura’s candid admission that he himself still struggles with occasional people‑pleasing moments adds authenticity and prevents the text from sounding like a prescriptive, one‑size‑fits‑all manual.

That said, the book’s brevity—while a virtue for busy readers—can leave some nuances underexplored. Readers seeking deeper dives into the sociocultural roots of compliance (e.g., gendered expectations, systemic power dynamics) may find the treatment somewhat surface‑level. Additionally, the reliance on anecdotal evidence, though engaging, occasionally substitutes for more rigorous empirical support. However, given Gazipura’s clinical background and the book’s explicit aim to be a pragmatic guide rather than an academic treatise, these limitations feel appropriate to its purpose.

Overall, Not Nice succeeds as a motivating, action‑oriented manual for anyone tired of feeling guilty for prioritizing their own needs. Gazipura’s blend of straight‑talk psychology, practical tools, and empathetic storytelling creates a roadmap that is both hopeful and realistic. By the final chapter, the reader is equipped not just with a set of techniques for saying no, but with a renewed sense of agency—a reminder that being “nice” does not have to mean being silent, and that true kindness begins with honoring oneself.
