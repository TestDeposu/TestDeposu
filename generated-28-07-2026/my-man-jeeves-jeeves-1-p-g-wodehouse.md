**Preview/Critique: My Man Jeeves by P.G. Wodehouse**

In the annals of English literature, few authors have matched P.G. Wodehouse's prowess in crafting comedic masterpieces. "My Man Jeeves," the inaugural collection of Jeeves and Wooster tales, is a testament to his unparalleled ability to weave humor from the most mundane aspects of life.

At the heart of Wodehouse's narrative genius lies the dynamic between the impetuous, feeble-minded Bertie Wooster and his unflappable, ingenious valet, Jeeves. Bertie, a wealthy, idle aristocrat, is a walking disaster, prone to impulsive decisions and social gaffes. Jeeves, on the other hand, is the epitome of cool-headed efficiency, his calm demeanor a stark contrast to Bertie's frantic energy. This class-based, temperament-driven duo forms the bedrock of Wodehouse's humor, with Jeeves's quiet manipulation of Bertie providing the comedic spark.

The stories in "My Man Jeeves" are a delightful blend of farce, wit, and social satire. From "Absent Treatment," where Jeeves cures Bertie's friend Freddie of his love for a woman by making him believe she's engaged, to "Bertie Changes His Mind," where Jeeves outmaneuvers Bertie to prevent him from donating a valuable painting, each tale is a masterclass in comedic timing and character development.

Wodehouse's style is as distinctive as it is delightful. His use of language is a joy, with clever wordplay and puns abounding. He has a knack for capturing the essence of the upper-class English idiom, making his characters and their world instantly recognizable. Yet, beneath the laughter, there's a subtle commentary on class, wealth, and the absurdities of societal norms.

"My Man Jeeves" is not just a collection of stories; it's an introduction to a world that, despite its idiosyncrasies, feels oddly familiar. It's a world where the absurd is commonplace, and the commonplace is absurd. It's a world where, despite their differences, Bertie and Jeeves are inseparable, bound by a bond that transcends class and temperament. It's a world that, as Wodehouse assures us, can never stale.
