# Killing Lincoln: The Shocking Assassination that Changed America Forever

**Author:** Bill O'Reilly  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

History has rarely felt this urgent, this breathless, or this lethal. In Killing Lincoln: The Shocking Assassination that Changed America Forever, co-authors Bill O’Reilly and Martin Dugard strip away the dry, academic dust traditionally clinging to the Civil War era and transform one of the darkest moments in American history into a pulse-pounding, cinematic thriller. 

Published in 2012, this runaway bestseller successfully bridges the often-dreaded gap between textbook history and mainstream entertainment. O’Reilly openly stated his ambition: to write a book that reads like a suspense novel, tailored specifically for readers who might not otherwise pick up a history book. By focusing intensely on the human element, the authors reveal what true leadership looks like under the crushing weight of a fractured nation. 

The most defining and polarizing stylistic choice in Killing Lincoln is its aggressive use of the present tense. As readers open the book, they are not merely looking back at the past; they are thrown directly into the capital alongside a visibly exhausted Abraham Lincoln, walking through the final weeks of his life with a target on his back. While purists might occasionally find the present-tense narrative intrusive—given that the events took place over a century and a half ago—it undeniably achieves its primary goal. It injects an immense sense of immediacy and heightens the pacing to a relentless degree. You know the tragic destination from page one, yet the authors masterfully trick your adrenaline into believing the outcome can still be altered.

The narrative thrives on the collision of two opposing forces: the benevolent, war-weary President racing against his fate, and John Wilkes Booth, the vain, handsome actor coiled like a spring and driven mad by Confederate defeat. The book meticulously details the grand, multi-pronged conspiracy. It tracks not just the fateful night at Ford’s Theatre, but the coordinated terror meant to decapitate the United States government all at once—from Lewis Powell’s brutal, bloody assault on Secretary of State William Seward to George Atzerodt losing his nerve to assassinate Vice President Andrew Johnson. 

True to O’Reilly’s penchant for dramatic flair, the book does not shy away from historical rabbit holes and juicy conspiracies. The authors dive straight into the lingering mysteries surrounding Secretary of War Edwin Stanton, the missing eighteen pages of Booth’s recovered diary, and the whispering campaigns of shadowy complicity. While O’Reilly and Dugard freely admit that these theories have been largely dismissed by mainstream historians, their inclusion adds a layer of investigative intrigue that keeps the pages turning rapidly. 

Critics might point out that the book is light on scholarly attribution, lacking the rigorous footnotes expected of a traditional historical monograph. However, judging Killing Lincoln by the strict standards of academic historiography misses the point entirely. This is narrative non-fiction designed for maximum impact. It strips away the pomp of nineteenth-century politics and exposes the raw, beating heart of a nation experiencing its greatest trauma. 

Ultimately, Killing Lincoln is a stunning success in popular history. It reminds us that historical figures were not marble statues, but flesh-and-blood human beings making split-second decisions in chaotic, terrifying times. For anyone looking to understand the dramatic twilight of the Civil War with the page-turning compulsion of a modern thriller, this book is an absolute must-read.
