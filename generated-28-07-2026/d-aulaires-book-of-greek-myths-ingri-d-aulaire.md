# D'Aulaires' Book of Greek Myths

**Author:** Ingri d'Aulaire  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1962

Generations of readers have traced their earliest literary awakenings to a single, dog-eared volume published in 1962: Ingri and Edgar d'Aulaire’s timeless masterwork, D'Aulaires' Book of Greek Myths. More than just a collection of ancient folklore, this book serves as an indispensable cultural gateway, capturing the grand, tempestuous, and endlessly fascinating world of Mount Olympus and making it entirely accessible to the youthful imagination. Long before children encounter Homer or Ovid in high school classrooms, the d'Aulaires provide a vibrant, pictorial introduction to the pantheon of gods, goddesses, heroes, and terrifying monsters that have shaped Western storytelling for millennia.

At the heart of the book lies a profound theme: the timeless humanity of the divine. The ancient Greeks did not worship distant, abstract forces; they envisioned deities driven by very human emotions—jealousy, vanity, boundless love, petty spite, and awe-inspiring wrath. The d'Aulaires capture this essence brilliantly. Zeus is not merely a ruler of the cosmos, but a notoriously fickle husband dodging the sharp gaze of Hera. Athena springs fully formed from a headache, and Prometheus steals fire with a rebellious courage that instantly captivates the young reader. By stripping away the academic dryness often associated with classical mythology, the authors present these figures as living, breathing entities whose triumphs and follies mirror our own. It is a brilliant exploration of how storytelling helps humanity make sense of the natural world, human nature, and the passage of time.

The stylistic brilliance of the volume is found in the inimitable, hand-lithographed artwork crafted by Ingri and Edgar d'Aulaire. Their visual style is chunky, textured, and deeply atmospheric, evoking the rustic warmth of Scandinavian folk art while capturing the sun-drenched majesty of the Mediterranean. Every page is a delicate balance of robust illustrations and lively, conversational prose. The text reads like a whispered fireside story, effortlessly guiding the reader through complex lineages, terrible curses, and epic quests without ever talking down to them. The monsters are suitably monstrous—Medusa’s serpents hiss with palpable menace, and the Minotaur prowls the shadowy depths of the labyrinth—yet they are rendered with an artistic restraint that sparks wonder rather than mere terror. 

Furthermore, the recent additions to this classic edition—including a moving afterword, rare sketchbook drawings, and family photographs—only deepen our appreciation for the immense labor of love the d'Aulaires poured into their craft. It is a testament to their enduring genius that authors, artists, and dreamers continually trace the roots of their creativity back to the pages of this singular book. 

D'Aulaires' Book of Greek Myths is much more than a children’s book; it is a cultural touchstone and a masterpiece of illustrated literature. It demands a place on the shelf of every home, ready to bewitch yet another generation with the immortal magic of the ancient world.
