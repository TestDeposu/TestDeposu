# Girls of Paper and Fire (Girls of Paper and Fire, #1)

**Author:** Natasha Ngan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

Opulence and oppression rarely dance so closely as they do in Natasha Ngan’s breakout 2018 fantasy, Girls of Paper and Fire. In this striking opening to a planned trilogy, Ngan crafts an Asian-inspired secondary world that is as breathtakingly gorgeous as it is brutally suffocating. Blending the lush world-building of palace intrigue with a searing narrative of survival and resistance, the novel establishes Ngan as a vital new voice in young adult fantasy. 

At the emotional center of the story is Lei, a young woman belonging to the Paper caste—the lowest, most marginalized tier of Ikhara society. Ngan wastes no time establishing the high stakes of this hierarchy. Marked by her striking golden eyes, Lei is ripped from her modest village life by royal guards, fated to become one of the Paper Girls, the king’s unwilling concubines. Through Lei’s perspective, Ngan explores the central theme of institutionalized dehumanization. The palace is depicted not as a sanctuary of fairytale dreams, but as a gilded cage built on the suffering of the lower castes. The author’s exploration of trauma is deeply resonant; Lei’s internal landscape is haunted by the memory of her mother’s abduction, making her forced entry into the royal court feel all the more violating.

Yet, what elevates Girls of Paper and Fire above standard dystopian or fantasy tropes is Ngan’s striking prose style. Her writing is sensory and evocative, painting Ikhara in vivid hues of silk, gold, and blood. Every room in the opulent palace drips with heavy descriptions that mirror the claustrophobia Lei feels. When the nine chosen girls undergo rigorous training to charm a tyrant, Ngan juxtaposes the dazzling exterior of courtly life with the psychological terror lurking beneath the surface. The pacing is adrenaline-soaked, effortlessly bridging quiet moments of profound vulnerability with sudden, heart-pounding danger.

Against this backdrop of systemic cruelty, Ngan introduces a spark of rebellion and hope: a forbidden romance. As Lei trains alongside her fellow consorts, she forms a deep, secret bond that eventually blossoms into love. This queer romance is handled with tenderness and fierce loyalty, acting as both an anchor for Lei's humanity and a catalyst for the story's explosive political intrigue. Ngan demonstrates that love in a world designed to break you is, in itself, a revolutionary act. 

Ultimately, Girls of Paper and Fire is a compelling critique of power, complicity, and the heavy cost of justice. Ngan does not offer easy answers or sugarcoat the brutality of her world, leaving her protagonist—and her readers—to grapple with how far one is willing to go for revenge and liberation. It is a mesmerizing, emotionally charged triumph that lingers long after the final page.
