# Losing It (Losing It, #1)

**Author:** Cora Carmack  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

Cora Carmack’s 2012 novel Losing It stands as a foundational pillar of the New Adult genre, capturing the frantic, vulnerable, and often hilarious transition from late adolescence into genuine adulthood. At its core, the novel explores the theme of bodily autonomy and the societal pressures surrounding virginity. Carmack uses her protagonist, Bliss Edwards, to dissect the anxiety of feeling left behind in a culture that hyper-sexualizes youth, while simultaneously subverting the very tropes she employs. Bliss’s quest to shed her virginity is less about physical desire and more about regaining control over a narrative she feels she is failing at. When her meticulously planned one-night stand goes disastrously awry—leaving a gorgeous stranger naked in her bed—Bliss is forced to confront her deep-seated fears of inadequacy, vulnerability, and exposure. 

The brilliance of Losing It lies in its stylistic balance between laugh-out-loud comedy and genuine emotional resonance. Carmack’s writing style is punchy, conversational, and relentlessly fast-paced, perfectly mirroring the chaotic inner monologue of a stressed college senior. The dialogue is snappy and laced with sharp wit, keeping the narrative engaging even during moments of heightened melodrama. Rather than relying on dark, brooding angst—a common pitfall in the romance genre—Carmack infuses her storytelling with self-aware humor. The ultimate humiliation of discovering that the one-night stand she fled from is actually her new theater professor elevates the dramatic irony and creates a high-stakes dynamic that drives the plot forward with relentless momentum. 

Yet, beneath the sparkling surface humor and the delicious tension of the forbidden professor-student trope, Carmack weaves a thoughtful critique of shame. Bliss’s virginity is not treated as a holy grail to be fiercely protected, nor is it depicted as a shameful burden to be discarded recklessly. Instead, Carmack frames it as a personal milestone that should be approached on one’s own terms, free from the peer pressure and timelines dictated by society. The slow unraveling of Bliss’s defenses allows the romance to develop on a foundation of mutual respect and emotional stripping away of masks, paralleling her theatrical studies. 

Losing It is a striking, deeply entertaining preview of what New Adult fiction can achieve when it refuses to take itself too seriously while still honoring the profound heartbreaks of youth. Carmack crafts a world where mistakes are messy, apologies are necessary, and love rarely follows the syllabus. It is a triumphant debut that proves the best coming-of-age stories are the ones where the characters stumble awkwardly, laugh at themselves, and finally figure out how to lose it all in order to find themselves.
