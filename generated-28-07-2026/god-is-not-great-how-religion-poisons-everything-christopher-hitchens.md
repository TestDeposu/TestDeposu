# God Is Not Great: How Religion Poisons Everything

**Author:** Christopher Hitchens  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

Review: God Is Not Great: How Religion Poisons Everything by Christopher Hitchens

In the pantheon of modern polemic literature, few works ignite the intellect and provoke as immediate a reaction as Christopher Hitchens’s 2007 tour de force, God Is Not Great: How Religion Poisons Everything. As one of the formidable "Four Horsemen" of the New Atheism movement, Hitchens did not merely question faith; he launched a relentless, brilliantly articulated assault against the institutionalized dogmas that have shaped—and scarred—human history. This manifesto is not written for the timid believer or the complacent agnostic. It is a fiery, uncompromising challenge to the intellectual laziness of religious faith, demanding that humanity finally grow up and embrace the harsh, beautiful light of reason.

The central theme of the book is captured with brutal succinctness in its subtitle: religion poisons everything. Hitchens meticulously unpacks this thesis across geopolitics, personal morality, and human history. He argues that religion is a man-made construct, born out of our primitive fears and our early, desperate attempts to understand a cosmos too vast for our infancy. Rather than offering a divine moral compass, Hitchens contends that religion actively subverts morality, demanding obedience over ethics and encoding ancient prejudices into sacred texts that continue to oppress modern societies. With sharp historical acumen, he exposes how holy books are riddled with absurdities, contradictions, and endorsements of atrocities, from slavery to holy war. 

What elevates this critique above standard secular pamphleteering is Hitchens’s inimitable literary style. He writes not merely as a debater, but as a man of letters deeply steeped in history, literature, and philosophy. His prose is a masterclass in eloquence, biting wit, and uncompromising clarity. Hitchens balances high-brow cultural references with devastating satirical jabs, making the complex demolition of theological arguments read with the pacing of a thriller. He does not whisper his disagreements; he articulates them with the righteous fury of a journalist who has witnessed firsthand the sectarian bloodshed fueled by religious fanaticism across the globe.

Yet, for all its iconoclasm, God Is Not Great is ultimately an optimistic book. Hitchens does not leave a void where faith used to be. Instead, he passionately champions a secular life rooted in science, art, and critical inquiry. He invites the reader to trade the archaic myths of the desert for the staggering realities revealed by the Hubble Telescope, and the primitive narratives of creation for the elegant symmetry of the double helix. In Hitchens’s view, reality is far more wondrous, complex, and awe-inspiring than anything concocted by ancient priests. 

Whether you view it as a dangerous heresy or a long-overdue public service, God Is Not Great remains a defining cultural artifact of the twenty-first century. Hitchens forces believers and non-believers alike to defend their epistemological foundations. It is a dazzling, provocative, and deeply humanistic read that challenges us to cast aside dogma and dare to think for ourselves.
