# Ghosts

**Author:** Dolly Alderton  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Anatomy of Disappearance: Why Dolly Alderton’s Ghosts Hits Uncomfortably Close to Home

In her fiction debut, Ghosts, bestselling author Dolly Alderton turns her sharp, empathetic eye from the memoiristic reflections of her breakout hit, Everything I Know About Love, toward the messy, labyrinthine reality of modern womanhood. Published in 2021, the novel centers on Nina Dean, a successful food writer on the cusp of her thirty-second year. Nina has built a life she is genuinely proud of: she owns a charming apartment in London, boasts a bustling social life lubricated by good wine and loyal friends, and maintains an admirably civilized friendship with an ex-boyfriend. Yet, as the clock ticks toward thirty-two, Nina finds herself standing at the uncomfortable intersection of youth and middle age, where the rules of romance, friendship, and family responsibilities are being furiously rewritten.

Enter Max, a man discovered on a dating app who defies all statistical probability by actually being wonderful. Handsome, attentive, and effortlessly charming, Max sweeps Nina into a whirlwind romance defined by instant chemistry, shared eccentricities, and late-night laughter. But just as the narrative arc seems poised to follow the well-worn grooves of a traditional romantic comedy—culminating in Max uttering those three fateful words—the rug is violently pulled out. Max does not just leave; he evaporates. He ghosts. 

Yet, to categorize Ghosts merely as a novel about modern dating would be to miss its beating, bruised heart. Alderton uses the sudden, disorienting disappearance of Max not as the entirety of the plot, but as a catalyst that triggers a domino effect across every other facet of Nina’s life. With her romantic validation abruptly revoked, Nina is forced to confront the slow-motion tragedy unfolding at home. Her brilliant, eccentric father is succumbing to the cruel, creeping fog of early-onset dementia, while her mother retreats into a fortress of exhausting, desperate denial. Simultaneously, her oldest childhood friend is drifting away into the insular, baffling world of new motherhood, and professional pressures loom large. 

What makes Ghosts so striking is Alderton’s uncanny ability to capture the specific, low-grade anxiety of being a woman in your early thirties. The author masterfully dissects the societal expectations that dictate how a woman should feel about aging, fertility, singlehood, and familial duty. Nina is not a damsel in distress waiting to be saved; she is competent, witty, and fiercely independent. Yet, she is deeply vulnerable to the fractures appearing in her foundational relationships. Alderton explores the exhaustion of caring for aging parents while trying to maintain a semblance of a vibrant social life, capturing the poignant role reversal of children suddenly becoming the caretakers of their heroes.

Stylistically, Alderton’s prose is a masterclass in tonal balance. She is wickedly funny, peppering the narrative with sharp observations about dating app culture, hipster pretension, and the absurdities of contemporary London life. Her dialogue crackles with authenticity, feeling less like scripted fiction and more like an overheard conversation between close friends. However, beneath the sparkling wit lies a profound, unyielding sincerity. Alderton does not shy away from the darker, less Instagrammable aspects of life. The scenes detailing the father’s cognitive decline are rendered with a devastating, quiet tenderness that will leave readers reaching for the nearest tissue. It is this friction between cynicism and profound empathy that elevates the novel above standard chick-lit fare, grounding it in a reality that feels uncomfortably close to home.

Ultimately, Ghosts is a triumph of contemporary storytelling because it refuses to offer easy resolutions. Life does not tie itself up in a neat bow, and neither does Alderton’s narrative. Instead, she delivers a moving, clear-eyed meditation on resilience, grief, and the quiet courage it takes to keep showing up for yourself and others when the world seems determined to fade away. It is smart, sexy, and shatteringly relatable—a book that feels less like reading a story and more like having a deeply comforting, brutally honest conversation with your wisest friend.
