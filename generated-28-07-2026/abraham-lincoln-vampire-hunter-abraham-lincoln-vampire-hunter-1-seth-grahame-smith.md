# Abraham Lincoln: Vampire Hunter (Abraham Lincoln: Vampire Hunter, #1)

**Author:** Seth Grahame-Smith  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

History is rarely what it seems on the surface, and Seth Grahame-Smith reminds us of this truth with delightfully bloody audacity in Abraham Lincoln: Vampire Hunter. Following his breakout success with Pride and Prejudice and Zombies, Grahame-Smith applies his unique brand of mash-up literature to the annals of American history, transforming the 16th President of the United States from a standard-issue historical bronze monument into a brooding, ax-wielding avenger of the night. 

At its core, the novel explores a fascinating and surprisingly resonant central theme: the intersection of human cruelty and supernatural monstrosity. Grahame-Smith uses the institution of slavery and the horrors of the antebellum South not merely as historical backdrops, but as the primary feeding grounds for an entrenched aristocracy of vampires. By framing the Civil War not just as a political and moral battle to preserve the Union and end human bondage, but as an apocalyptic war against the undead, the author crafts a biting metaphor. In this alternate history, the vampires represent the ultimate parasitic elite, feeding quite literally off the blood and labor of the marginalized. Abraham Lincoln’s lifelong crusade against these creatures evolves in tandem with his political awakening, brilliantly fusing his personal quest for vengeance with a broader, national destiny to purge the country of its darkest evils.

Grahame-Smith’s stylistic choice is perhaps the book’s most impressive and convincing trick. Eschewing the fast-paced, action-heavy prose one might expect from a schlocky creature-feature, the author adopts the gravitas, meticulous pacing, and solemn tone of a traditional historical biography. Written in the grand, authoritative style of master historians like Doris Kearns Goodwin or David McCullough, the narrative treats the existence of vampires with absolute, deadpan seriousness. The framing device—wherein Grahame-Smith supposedly discovers and transcribes Lincoln’s long-lost secret journals—lends the proceedings an aura of authenticity that makes the utterly absurd premise feel strangely plausible. 

The prose is stark, atmospheric, and deeply immersive. When nine-year-old Abe kneels at his mother’s bedside in an Indiana cabin, the grief is rendered with genuine emotional weight, rooting the fantastic elements in authentic human tragedy. This emotional anchor prevents the novel from devolving into a mere novelty act. Lincoln is portrayed as a complex, melancholic figure burdened by the weight of a nation and a secret war fought entirely in the shadows. When he wields his legendary silver-bladed ax, the action scenes are visceral and cinematic, yet they are always counterbalanced by political intrigue, speeches, and the quiet loneliness of the presidency.

Ultimately, Abraham Lincoln: Vampire Hunter succeeds because it commits entirely to its own high-concept premise. It is a wildly entertaining, fast-moving epic that cleverly subverts the historical canon while maintaining a surprising amount of reverence for its subject. Grahame-Smith has crafted a brilliant historical fantasy that asks readers to look at the American Civil War—and the man who led the nation through it—through a wildly distorted, blood-soaked lens that is impossible to look away from.
