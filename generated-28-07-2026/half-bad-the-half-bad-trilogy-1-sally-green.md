# Half Bad (The Half Bad Trilogy, #1)

**Author:** Sally Green  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

Half Bad bursts onto the page with a raw, almost visceral urgency that feels less like a conventional YA fantasy and more like a desperate sprint through a war‑torn alley. Sally Green drops us straight into Nathan’s bruised reality—a sixteen‑year‑old half‑blood witch whose very existence threatens the fragile balance between the White and Black witch covens that secretly co‑exist with modern England. From the opening scene of Nathan shivering in a cramped cage, Green’s prose is tight, clipped, and unflinchingly present, mirroring the protagonist’s hyper‑awareness of every sound, every threat, and every fleeting chance at freedom.

The novel’s beating heart is the theme of alienation forged by identity. Nathan is neither wholly accepted by the White witches who see his Black heritage as a contaminant, nor trusted by the Black witches who fear his potential as a weapon. This duality forces him into a perpetual outsider status, and Green exploits that liminality to explore what it means to survive when you belong nowhere. The relentless countdown to Nathan’s seventeenth birthday—a deadline that promises either empowerment or death—acts as a metronome for the narrative, tightening the tension with each chapter. Green’s style thrives on this pressure: short, punchy sentences interspersed with stark, sensory details that make the magic feel dangerous and immediate rather than whimsical. Her world‑building is economical; she reveals the lore through Nathan’s fragmented memories and the brutal tactics of his captors, allowing readers to piece together the witch society as he does, which deepens the sense of disorientation and urgency.

What sets Half Bad apart from its Harry Potter‑inspired peers is the refusal to romanticize magic. Here, witchcraft is a tool of oppression and survival, stained with blood and betrayal. Green’s narrative voice—often verging on first‑person immediacy even when the text shifts to third—captures Nathan’s internal monologue with a rawness that feels almost confessional. The romance that threads through the plot, particularly his connection with the enigmatic Annalise, offers fleeting tenderness but never distracts from the overarching drive to escape and understand his lineage.

In sum, Half Bad is a gripping, unrelenting thriller that uses the witch‑war backdrop to examine the corrosive effects of being caught between worlds. Sally Green’s spare, kinetic prose propels the reader forward, making every page feel like a step closer to Nathan’s elusive freedom—or his inevitable demise.
