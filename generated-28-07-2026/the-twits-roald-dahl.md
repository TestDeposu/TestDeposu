# The Twits

**Author:** Roald Dahl  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1991

Roald Dahl has long been celebrated as the master of the macabre for children, a writer who respects the young reader enough to acknowledge that the world can be delightfully dreadful. Published in 1980 and enduring strongly through later editions like the 1991 printing, The Twits stands as one of Dahl's most concentrated and gleefully grotesque achievements. At first glance, it is a brisk, slapstick comedy about two thoroughly repulsive human beings. Yet, beneath the worm-infested spaghetti and the sticky traps of birdlime lies a sharp, subversive thesis on the nature of ugliness, beauty, and the moral imperative of rebellion.

The central theme of The Twits is the direct equation between inner malice and physical decay. Mr. and Mrs. Twit are universally unpleasant, but Dahl is careful to construct their repulsiveness as a moral failing rather than a genetic misfortune. Mr. Twit’s legendary unwashed beard—caked with old cornflakes, tinned sardines, and cheese—is a living ecosystem that mirrors the rot of his character. Mrs. Twit, possessing a glass eye and a face frozen in a perpetual sneer, proves that bitterness literally warps the flesh over time. Dahl brilliantly posits a philosophical rule for his young audience: if a person has nasty thoughts, they begin to show on the face, and pretty soon, they will look as ghastly inside as they are on the outside. Conversely, a kind heart produces sunshine. It is a bold, uncompromising moral framework delivered with the subtlety of a falling anvil, perfectly calibrated to appeal to a child's sense of absolute justice.

Stylistically, Dahl operates at the absolute peak of his anarchic powers here. Working with illustrator Quentin Blake—whose scratchy, kinetic drawings capture the chaotic energy of the text—Dahl crafts a narrative driven by rhythm, repetition, and deliberate grossness. He knows precisely what makes children giggle, weaponizing bodily functions, dirt, and cruelty into a carnival of the absurd. The dialogue snaps with theatrical malice, and the escalation of their pranks against one another—from stretching Mrs. Twit with gas balloons to dropping frogs in her bed—escalates into pure cartoon physics. 

Yet, the book transcends mere schoolyard sadism when the perspective shifts to the captive Muggle-Wumps and the wild birds. Here, Dahl introduces his recurring thematic preoccupation with the tyranny of the powerful over the powerless. The caged monkeys and the tormented birds are innocent victims of small-minded despots. The genius of the climax lies not just in the poetic justice of the Twits shrinking away into nothingness—a literal implosion of their toxic identities—but in the collective action of the oppressed. The Muggle-Wumps do not merely escape; they outwit and humiliate their captors, delivering a potent lesson in solidarity and resistance.

The Twits is a masterclass in economy. In just a handful of chapters, Dahl builds a complete, morally charged universe that remains as startling and hilarious today as it was decades ago. It is a book that encourages children to laugh at authority, despise cruelty, and remember that ugliness is a choice. A deliciously nasty bite of a book, it proves that sometimes, the only way to deal with terrible people is to laugh them right out of existence.
