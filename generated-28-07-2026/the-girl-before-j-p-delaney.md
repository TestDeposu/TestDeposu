# The Girl Before

**Author:** J.P. Delaney  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

Architectural Minimalism Meets Psychological Terror: A Review of J.P. Delaney’s The Girl Before

In the realm of domestic noir, the setting is rarely just a backdrop; it is usually an active, breathing participant in the deception. Yet, few novels elevate architecture to a character quite as chillingly as J.P. Delaney’s 2017 global bestseller, The Girl Before. At once a sleek meditation on minimalism and a relentless exercise in psychological dread, Delaney’s novel asks a deceptively simple question: How much of ourselves are we willing to surrender for perfection? 

The narrative unfolds through a clever, alternating dual timeline centered on a single address: One Folgate Street. Designed by the brilliant, enigmatic, and fiercely controlling architect Edward Monkford, the house is a masterclass in pale stone, plate glass, and soaring, sterile spaces. But residency comes at a steep price. Applicants must submit to a bizarre, intrusive questionnaire listing their essential possessions and agree to an oppressive manifesto of house rules. No books. No throw pillows. No clutter, no photos, no personal effects. The minimalist design is intended to transform its occupant—and, as the novel chillingly demonstrates, it does.

We first meet Emma, a woman reeling from a traumatic break-in, desperate for safety and a fresh start. She accepts the draconian terms, seduced by the promise of sanctuary, unaware of the psychological cage she is entering. Then comes Jane, moving into the very same house years later following her own personal tragedy. Instantly drawn to the austere beauty of the space—and to its aloof, seductive creator—Jane soon uncovers a terrifying truth. The previous tenant, a woman eerily similar to Jane in age, appearance, and past trauma, died mysteriously on the property. As Jane settles in, she realizes she is walking a predetermined path, unwittingly mirroring the habits, choices, and ultimately the terror of the girl before.

Delaney’s style is as sharp and polished as the glass walls of One Folgate Street. The author employs a taut, propulsive narrative rhythm that makes the book famously difficult to put down, capturing the glossy, high-stakes atmosphere popularized by thrillers like Gone Girl and The Girl on the Train, yet carving out a distinct identity through its focus on design and control. One of the most ingenious structural choices in the book is the inclusion of the architect’s ethical and psychological questions interspersed throughout the chapters—interrogations left starkly unanswered, forcing the reader to mentally fill in the blanks and become complicit in the psychological experiment. 

At its core, The Girl Before is a profound exploration of trauma, obsession, and the illusion of safety. It investigates how grief strips us down to our core, leaving us vulnerable to reinvention, even when that reinvention is dictated by a megalomaniac. Edward Monkford’s house functions as a physical manifestation of emotional trauma: cold, unyielding, and demanding the erasure of the past in exchange for a sterile, manufactured future. 

Delaney crafts a brilliant puzzle box of duplicity, death, and deception. It is a stylish, deeply unsettling thriller that forces us to look at our own lives and question the very objects, memories, and attachments we hold dear. If minimalism is the art of subtraction, Delaney reminds us that subtracting too much of our humanity leaves a void waiting to be filled with fear.
