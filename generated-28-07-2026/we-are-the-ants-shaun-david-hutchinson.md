# We Are the Ants

**Author:** Shaun David Hutchinson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

If the end of the world came down to a simple, binary choice—push a button or let it burn—what would you choose? For sixteen-year-old Henry Denton, the protagonist of Shaun David Hutchinson’s 2016 critically acclaimed novel We Are the Ants, that question is far from hypothetical. Abducted by extraterrestrials on a regular basis, Henry is given an ultimatum: the world will end in 144 days, and only he has the power to stop it. Yet, as a young man drowning in grief, isolation, and unrelenting trauma, Henry is far from convinced that humanity is worth saving. 

We Are the Ants is not your average science fiction narrative. Instead of focusing on interstellar warfare or cosmic exploration, Hutchinson uses the absurd premise of alien abduction as a brilliant, melancholic metaphor for the crushing weight of depression and the existential dread of modern adolescence. The science fiction elements serve as a canvas for a much deeper, deeply human exploration of grief and survival. Henry’s life is an avalanche of tragedy. His boyfriend, Jesse, died by suicide a year prior, leaving Henry to navigate a labyrinth of guilt and lingering affection. At home, his mother chain-smokes her way through financial ruin, his brother is an aimless dropout facing impending fatherhood, and his grandmother’s mind is slowly fading into the fog of Alzheimer’s disease. To top it all off, Henry endures a degrading, secret sexual relationship with his primary tormentor, a high school bully. 

When the aliens present him with the glowing red button to wipe the slate clean, destruction looks less like a catastrophe and more like a merciful release. 

Hutchinson’s authorial style is the driving force behind the novel's profound impact. He writes with a voice that is equal parts razor-sharp, sarcastic, and devastatingly tender. Henry is a deeply cynical narrator, employing dark humor as a defense mechanism against a world that has repeatedly broken him. Yet beneath the biting sarcasm lies a profound vulnerability. Hutchinson refuses to sanitize teenage pain. He tackles heavy themes—suicide, bullying, homophobia, and terminal illness—with an unflinching honesty that respects the emotional intelligence of young adult readers. The prose moves fluidly between the absurd surrealism of Henry’s alien encounters and the gritty, agonizing reality of his everyday life, creating a jarring yet cohesive reading experience.

At its core, the central theme of We Are the Ants is the radical, terrifying act of choosing hope in the face of insurmountable despair. The title itself is a poignant reflection of this theme. Humans are infinitesimal, like ants crawling across a microscopic speck of dust in an indifferent universe. To the aliens, we are insignificant. But Hutchinson argues that our insignificance is precisely what gives us freedom and meaning. If the universe doesn't care about us, we are entirely responsible for creating our own purpose. We must find beauty in the mundane, forgiveness in betrayal, and connection through our shared brokenness. 

As the 144-day countdown ticks away, Henry embarks on a scientific assessment of humanity, weighing the pros and cons of existence through his interactions with an eclectic cast of characters, including a brilliant new boy who challenges his worldview. Hutchinson masterfully leads the reader to the precipice of the ultimate question, making us feel the crushing gravity of Henry’s choice in our own bones. 

We Are the Ants is a striking, unforgettable triumph of contemporary young adult fiction. It is a book that doesn't offer easy answers or sugarcoat the darkness, but instead shines a flashlight into the shadows, revealing that even the smallest moments of human connection are worth fighting for. Hutchinson has crafted a masterpiece about the heavy burden of living and the revolutionary power of love.
