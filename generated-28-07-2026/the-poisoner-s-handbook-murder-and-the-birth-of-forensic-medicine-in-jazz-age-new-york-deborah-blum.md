# The Poisoner's Handbook: Murder and the Birth of Forensic Medicine in Jazz Age New York

**Author:** Deborah Blum  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

The Poisoner's Handbook: Murder and the Birth of Forensic Medicine in Jazz Age New York, written by Deborah Blum, is a gripping and meticulously researched account of the dawn of forensic medicine in the early twentieth century. This book masterfully weaves together the threads of true crime, history, and science to create a narrative that is both fascinating and unsettling. Blum's writing style is akin to a masterful storyteller, making the complex and often dry world of toxicology and forensic science come alive with vivid descriptions and a sense of urgency.

At the heart of the book are the pioneering figures of Charles Norris and Alexander Gettler, who, despite the rampant corruption and lack of scientific rigor in the coroner's office, dared to challenge the status quo. Their groundbreaking work in the field of forensic chemistry not only helped to solve some of the most baffling murder cases of the time but also laid the foundation for the modern justice system. Blum's portrayal of these two men is nothing short of captivating, bringing to light their unwavering dedication, unrelenting passion, and unshakeable commitment to the pursuit of truth and justice.

One of the most striking aspects of The Poisoner's Handbook is its ability to transport the reader to the Jazz Age, a time of great social change, cultural upheaval, and scientific discovery. Blum's vivid descriptions of the era's excesses, from the opulent parties to the dark underbelly of organized crime, create a rich and immersive backdrop against which the story of Norris and Gettler unfolds. The author's use of language is evocative and engaging, making it easy to become fully invested in the world she has created.

Throughout the book, Blum also explores the darker aspects of human nature, delving into the world of poisoners and murderers who exploited the lack of scientific understanding to commit the perfect crime. The cases she presents are both shocking and thought-provoking, highlighting the cunning and ruthlessness of those who would stop at nothing to achieve their goals. Yet, even in the face of such darkness, the book remains a testament to the power of human ingenuity, perseverance, and the unwavering commitment to justice.

The Poisoner's Handbook is a book that will appeal to a wide range of readers, from true crime enthusiasts to history buffs and science aficionados. Blum's writing is accessible and engaging, making complex scientific concepts easy to understand and relate to. The book's pacing is well-balanced, moving seamlessly between the historical context, the scientific discoveries, and the personal stories of the protagonists.

In conclusion, The Poisoner's Handbook is a captivating and expertly crafted book that sheds light on a fascinating era in American history. Deborah Blum's masterful storytelling and meticulous research have resulted in a narrative that is both informative and entertaining. This book is a must-read for anyone interested in the history of forensic medicine, true crime, or the Jazz Age, and is sure to leave a lasting impression on its readers.
