# Making Money (Discworld, #36; Moist Von Lipwig, #2)

**Author:** Terry Pratchett  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Terry Pratchett’s *Making Money* is a rollicking, razor‑sharp satire that takes the seemingly mundane subject of currency and turns it into a playground for absurdity, intrigue, and surprisingly earnest commentary on human nature. Set in the bustling, smog‑choked streets of Ankh‑Morpork, the novel follows Moist von Lipwig—former confidence‑trickster turned Postmaster General—as he is reluctantly recruited by Lord Vetinari to resuscitate the failing Royal Mint and introduce the city’s first paper money. What begins as a bureaucratic rescue mission quickly spirals into a high‑stakes game where vampires, crossbow‑wielding aristocrats, and alchemical experiments lurk in the shadows, all eager to protect—or exploit—the newfound power of cash.

At its core, *Making Money* explores the illusion of value. Pratchett dissects how society collectively agrees that a slip of paper, a stamped coin, or a digital entry holds worth, and how that agreement can be manipulated, abused, or reinvented. Moist’s journey is less about mastering ledgers than about understanding the stories people tell themselves about wealth: the greed that drives the old guard, the fear that fuels resistance to change, and the odd, often endearing ingenuity that emerges when ordinary folk are forced to confront a system they never designed. The novel’s humor springs from the collision of these lofty ideas with Pratchett’s trademark slapstick—a runaway printing press, a vampire with a penchant for accounting, and a cadre of golems who take their jobs a little too literally.

Stylistically, Pratchett shines as a master of tonal juggling. His prose is crisp and witty, peppered with footnote‑like asides that feel like conspiratorial whispers from a narrator who knows every joke before‑you‑real‑was‑a technique keeps readers constantly one step ahead, delighted by the inevitability of each twist. Characters are rendered with affectionate exaggeration: Moist’s smooth‑talking charm masks a genuine, if reluctant, sense of responsibility; Lord Vetinari’s omnipresent calm is both menace and mentorship; even the minor players—a nervous cashier, an overzealous clerk—are given distinct voices that echo the larger themes of loyalty, ambition, and the occasional absurdity of duty.

What makes *Making Money* especially resonant is its timelessness. Though rooted in a fantastical city where dragons occasionally nap in the sewers, the novel’s meditation on trust, value, and the social contracts that underpin economies mirrors contemporary debates about cryptocurrency, inflation, and financial reform. Pratchett never preaches; instead, he lets his characters stumble through the mess, laugh at their own follies, and occasionally stumble upon a truth that feels both hard‑won and oddly comforting.

In short, *Making Money* is a brilliant blend of farce and insight—a Discworld romp that asks us to look at the money in our pockets and wonder what stories we’re telling ourselves about it. Pratchett’s deft hand ensures that the satire never overwhelms the heart, and the humor never obscures the message: money may be a human invention, but the values we attach to it are profoundly, hilariously human.
