# A Good Girl's Guide to Murder (A Good Girl's Guide to Murder, #1)

**Author:** Holly Jackson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

Holly Jackson’s *A Good Girl’s Guide to Murder* is a masterclass in modern YA mystery—sharp, suspenseful, and relentlessly addictive. Published in 2020, this debut novel didn’t just ride the wave of the genre’s popularity; it carved out its own space with a protagonist whose determination and vulnerability make her impossible to ignore. The book’s premise is deceptively simple: a high school senior reopens a five-year-old murder case, only to uncover truths that could shatter her town’s complacency—and put her in the crosshairs of a killer. But Jackson’s genius lies in how she transforms a familiar trope into something fresh, with a narrative voice that’s equal parts witty and haunting.

At its core, *A Good Girl’s Guide to Murder* is about the power of doubt—not just in others, but in oneself. Pippa Fitz-Amobi, our protagonist, is the kind of heroine who feels achingly real: a straight-A student with a meticulous planner, a protective older brother, and a quiet obsession with justice. When she chooses Sal Singh’s case for her final project, she’s not just challenging a closed investigation; she’s confronting the idea that the people we trust can be capable of terrible things. The book forces readers to question their own assumptions, too. How well do we really know the people around us? Can a town’s collective memory be trusted when it’s built on tragedy?

Jackson’s style is a breath of fresh air in the YA mystery space. Pip’s narration is conversational, peppered with pop culture references and dry humor that keeps the tone from feeling too heavy, even as the stakes escalate. But beneath the snark lies a simmering tension. The book’s structure—interspersed with Pip’s case notes, interview transcripts, and her own reflections—mimics the detective work itself, pulling the reader into the investigation alongside her. It’s a technique that could easily feel gimmicky, but Jackson executes it flawlessly, making the format feel organic rather than forced.

What truly sets this book apart, however, is its refusal to give the reader easy answers. The mystery unfolds in layers, with each revelation complicating the narrative rather than simplifying it. Pip’s journey isn’t just about proving Sal’s innocence; it’s about confronting the messy, uncomfortable truths that lie beneath the surface of small-town life. The supporting cast—from Pip’s skeptical best friend, Cara, to the enigmatic newcomer, Ravi Singh (Sal’s younger brother)—add depth and moral ambiguity, ensuring that no character is purely good or evil.

Jackson’s prose is clean and propulsive, but it’s her ability to balance humor with genuine suspense that makes this book so compelling. The pacing is relentless, with cliffhangers that demand to be read in one sitting. Yet, even as the plot twists and turns, the emotional core of the story remains Pip’s struggle to reconcile her idealism with the harsh realities of the world. By the final pages, the reader is left breathless—not just because of the shocking conclusion, but because of the emotional weight Jackson has woven into every clue and confession.

*A Good Girl’s Guide to Murder* isn’t just a mystery; it’s a meditation on memory, guilt, and the stories we tell ourselves to make sense of the past. It’s the kind of book that lingers long after the last page, leaving you to question not just the characters’ motives, but your own. Holly Jackson has crafted a debut that’s as clever as it is compulsive, proving that YA mystery can be both wildly entertaining and thought-provoking.
