# Anna and the French Kiss (Anna and the French Kiss, #1)

**Author:** Stephanie Perkins  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

Anna and the French Kiss is a delightful, sunlit novel that captures the intoxicating allure of first love and the transformative power of place. Stephanie Perkins crafts a story that feels like a warm breeze drifting through the cobbled streets of Paris—gentle, romantic, and impossible to resist.

At its heart, the book is a coming-of-age tale about Anna Oliphant, a seventeen-year-old girl uprooted from her comfortable life in Atlanta and deposited into the elegant, foreign world of the School of America in Paris. Forced into a year abroad, Anna resists at first, clinging to her familiar routines and her crush back home. But Paris, with its golden light and whispered promises, has other plans. It doesn’t just change her surroundings—it changes her. The city becomes a character in its own right, a living, breathing entity that teaches Anna to see the world—and herself—differently.

The central theme is the tension between expectation and reality, especially in love. Anna arrives in Paris believing she knows what she wants: a steady boyfriend, a predictable future, and the comfort of the familiar. But the city, with its art, its history, and its people, challenges those assumptions. She meets Étienne St. Clair, the charming, blue-eyed British-American boy with a French name and a complicated love life. He’s taken—by his long-term girlfriend, Meredith—but that doesn’t stop the spark between him and Anna. Their friendship blossoms into something deeper, something charged with longing and possibility. Perkins masterfully explores the agony of unrequited love, the thrill of almost-kisses, and the quiet devastation of missed connections.

What makes this novel so compelling is Perkins’ style—effortlessly readable, witty, and deeply immersive. Her prose is clean and unpretentious, yet it carries a rhythmic charm that makes you feel as though you’re walking beside Anna through the Latin Quarter or sharing a café crème in a sunlit square. The dialogue crackles with authenticity; Anna’s inner voice is sharp, self-deprecating, and full of heart. She’s not a perfect heroine—she’s flawed, stubborn, and sometimes petty—but that’s what makes her so relatable. She’s a girl trying to figure herself out while navigating the minefield of teenage emotions.

Perkins also excels at creating a rich tapestry of supporting characters. From the eccentric, free-spirited Meredith to the loyal and hilarious Josh, Anna’s roommate, each character feels fully realized. Even the secondary figures, like the enigmatic and aloof Meredith or the sweet but awkward Elijah, add depth to the story. The dynamics between them are what make the book so engaging—there’s humor, drama, and a sense of camaraderie that feels genuine.

The romance in Anna and the French Kiss is slow-burning and achingly sweet. It’s not about grand gestures or instant passion; it’s about the quiet moments—the stolen glances, the shared secrets, the way Anna’s heart races when Étienne brushes her hand. Perkins understands that the best love stories aren’t about perfection but about imperfection, about the way love can both comfort and wound.

Ultimately, Anna and the French Kiss is a love letter to Paris and to the messy, beautiful process of growing up. It’s a story about finding yourself in unexpected places and learning that sometimes the things you resist the most end up being the very things that change your life. Perkins’ writing is like a first sip of wine—smooth, intoxicating, and impossible to put down.

If you’ve ever dreamed of Paris, of love, or of that moment when everything shifts and you realize you’re not the same person you were a year ago, this book will resonate with you. It’s a novel that lingers, like the scent of rain on cobblestones or the echo of a song you can’t quite place.
