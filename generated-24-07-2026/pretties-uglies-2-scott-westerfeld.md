# Pretties (Uglies, #2)

**Author:** Scott Westerfeld  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2008

Scott Westerfeld’s *Pretties* is a masterclass in dystopian storytelling, a sequel that deepens the unsettling allure of its predecessor while sharpening its critique of beauty, conformity, and the cost of happiness. Where *Uglies* introduced us to a world where society enforces physical perfection at 16, *Pretties* drags its protagonist—Tally Youngblood—into the glittering but hollow existence of the "Pretty" class, exposing the rot beneath the glamour. This isn’t just a tale of rebellion; it’s a psychological unraveling, a story about what happens when you’re given everything you thought you wanted and realize it was never yours to begin with.

Westerfeld’s genius lies in his ability to make the dystopian feel intimate. The shift from Tally’s "ugly" life to her "pretty" one is rendered with almost cinematic precision—her new body, her designer clothes, her effortless popularity—all designed to lull the reader into complacency before the rug is pulled out from under them. The prose is sleek, almost seductive, mirroring the superficial perfection of Tally’s new world. But beneath the surface, the language becomes jagged, fragmented, mirroring Tally’s growing unease. When she starts questioning the system, the narrative itself feels like it’s resisting her, as if the very words are trying to keep her in line. It’s a brilliant stylistic choice that underscores the book’s central theme: the insidious nature of control.

At its core, *Pretties* is about the illusion of choice. Tally believes she’s free—she can party, she can fly, she can love—but every pleasure is a carefully constructed illusion. The "Pretties" are not just physically altered; they’re psychologically conditioned to avoid anything that disrupts their contentment. The book’s most chilling moments come when Tally realizes that her friends, her boyfriend, even her own memories, have been altered to fit the narrative of perfection. Westerfeld forces us to ask: How much of our happiness is real, and how much is just another form of control?

The antagonist, Dr. Cable, is a particularly terrifying figure—not because she’s overtly villainous, but because she’s terrifyingly reasonable. She doesn’t need to resort to brute force; she’s already won by convincing people that their oppression is liberation. Her dialogue is laced with the language of progress and happiness, making her arguments almost persuasive. It’s a reflection of how real-world systems of control often work: not through fear, but through the promise of comfort.

Westerfeld also excels in exploring the theme of identity. Tally’s journey is one of self-discovery, but it’s complicated by the fact that her very sense of self has been manufactured. The book forces us to confront the idea that identity isn’t just about who we are, but who we’re allowed to be. The "Pretty" operation isn’t just cosmetic; it’s a psychological reset, erasing the past to ensure compliance. When Tally starts remembering fragments of her old life, it’s not just nostalgia—it’s a rebellion against the erasure of her true self.

The pacing of *Pretties* is relentless, mirroring Tally’s own frantic search for truth. The first half of the book is a dizzying descent into hedonism, making the eventual awakening all the more jarring. The second half is a race against time, as Tally fights to expose the truth before the system silences her. Westerfeld balances action with introspection, never letting the story feel like it’s just a series of set pieces. Even in the most high-stakes moments, Tally’s internal struggle remains at the forefront.

One of the book’s most haunting elements is its exploration of memory. Memory, in *Pretties*, is both a weapon and a vulnerability. Tally’s fragmented recollections of her past are what drive her to act, but they’re also what make her a target. The book suggests that memory is the key to true freedom—that without it, we’re just puppets dancing to someone else’s tune. It’s a theme that resonates deeply in an age where social media and algorithms shape our perceptions, where our pasts are constantly being edited and curated.

Westerfeld’s world-building is, as always, impeccable. The city of New Pretty Town is a glittering nightmare, a place where luxury is the ultimate form of control. The hoverboards, the morpho-pills, the ever-present surveillance—all of it feels both futuristic and eerily familiar. The book’s dystopia isn’t some distant, abstract threat; it’s a reflection of our own world, where beauty standards, social media validation, and the pressure to conform are just as insidious as any government mandate.

In the end, *Pretties* is a story about the cost of happiness. It asks whether a life of superficial perfection is worth the price of losing yourself. Tally’s journey is one of disillusionment, but it’s also a journey toward authenticity. The book doesn’t offer easy answers—just a mirror held up to the reader, forcing us to confront our own illusions.

Scott Westerfeld has crafted a sequel that’s as thought-provoking as it is thrilling. *Pretties* isn’t just a dystopian novel; it’s a warning. And in a world where we’re constantly told to chase perfection, that warning feels more urgent than ever.
