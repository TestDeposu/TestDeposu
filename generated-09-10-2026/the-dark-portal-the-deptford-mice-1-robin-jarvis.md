# The Dark Portal (The Deptford Mice, #1)

**Author:** Robin Jarvis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2025

Beneath the bustling streets of London lies a subterranean labyrinth where the ordinary rules of nature and morality do not apply. In The Dark Portal, the inaugural volume of Robin Jarvis’s celebrated Deptford Mice trilogy, the author constructs a richly layered, deliciously macabre world that redefines the boundaries of children’s fantasy. Released in a fresh edition, Jarvis’s classic tale proves just as spellbinding, atmospheric, and bracingly dark today as it was upon its original publication, offering young readers a sophisticated alternative to the typically sanitized tropes of animal-centric fiction.

At the heart of Jarvis’s narrative style is a striking juxtaposition between comforting domesticity and visceral, primeval terror. The novel opens in an idyllic sanctuary: an abandoned London house where a thriving community of mice has established a peaceful, sheltered existence. Free from the traditional threats of human traps and prowling felines, these creatures enjoy a deceptively cozy routine nestled within the skirting boards. Yet, Jarvis masterfully disrupts this complacency. The central theme of the novel hinges on the danger of complacency and the high cost of forbidden curiosity, embodied by the unfortunate mouse Albert Brown, whose ill-fated descent into the sewers shatters the illusion of eternal safety.

What elevates The Dark Portal far above standard anthropomorphic adventure is Jarvis’s unapologetic embrace of dark fantasy and horror elements. When Albert ventures into the dank, subterranean depths, he discovers a terrifying underworld governed by the grotesque cult of the sewer rats. These wretched creatures cower in the presence of a mysterious, malevolent entity that demands blind worship and blood sacrifice. Jarvis does not shy away from the grim realities of his fictional ecosystem; the rats are genuinely menacing, driven by foul sorcery and a bloodthirsty hunger that threatens to spill upward into the light. This creates a palpable tension that runs through the narrative like a live wire, making the struggle of the Deptford mice a genuinely desperate, high-stakes battle for survival.

Jarvis’s prose style is both lyrical and sharply cinematic, painting the sewers with shades of ooze, decay, and ancient gloom, while contrasting it with the warm, hearth-like camaraderie of the mice. He treats his youthful audience with immense respect, refusing to sugarcoat the themes of mortality, courage, and corruption. The mice are not magical heroes endowed with supernatural powers; rather, they are ordinary, fragile beings who must summon an extraordinary inner fortitude to confront horrors beyond their comprehension. This grounded heroism makes their plight deeply moving and intensely relatable.

Ultimately, The Dark Portal is a triumphant exercise in atmospheric world-building and suspense. It captures the imagination with its seamless blend of folklore, urban grit, and high-stakes adventure. For readers aged nine to twelve—and for any adult willing to revisit the shadows of childhood reading—Jarvis’s subterranean epic remains an essential, spine-chilling masterpiece. It is a stark reminder that even the smallest creatures can be called upon to stand against eternal darkness, and that the deepest courage is often born in the face of absolute terror.
