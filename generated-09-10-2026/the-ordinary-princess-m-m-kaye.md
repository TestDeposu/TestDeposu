# The Ordinary Princess

**Author:** M.M. Kaye  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2002

In a literary landscape overflowing with glittering tiaras, damsels in distress, and predestined royalty, M.M. Kaye’s *The Ordinary Princess* arrives as a breath of fresh, unpretentious air. Originally published in 2002, this enchanting fairy tale brilliantly subverts the traditional tropes of children's literature, offering a timeless meditation on the true nature of beauty, individuality, and self-determination. 

At its thematic core, the novel champions the radical notion that being ordinary is not a curse, but a profound liberation. Princess Amy, the youngest of seven royal sisters in the fantastical realm of Phantasmorania, is dealt a seemingly unfortunate hand at her christening. While her siblings are bestowed with the predictable fairy gifts of absolute beauty, wit, charm, health, and courage, a mischievous and perhaps wiser fairy gifts Amy with "Ordinariness." As she grows, Amy does not possess the golden curls or porcelain skin expected of a princess; instead, she sports a dusting of freckles, mousy brown hair, and a stubborn refusal to conform to courtly expectations. She would rather skin her knees exploring the countryside, climb trees, and seek out genuine adventures than sit demurely playing the harp or embroidering endless tapestries. 

M.M. Kaye, renowned globally for her sweeping historical fiction such as *The Far Pavilions*, demonstrates her mastery of narrative pacing and rich world-building even within the compact confines of a children's fable. Her writing style is delightfully witty, laced with a gentle, self-aware irony that appeals as much to adult readers reading aloud as it does to young independent readers. Kaye uses humor as a narrative weapon to dismantle the rigid vanity of the royal court. The King and Queen’s frantic attempts to mold Amy into a marketable, crown-ready commodity serve as a sharp, satirical critique of societal pressures to conform. 

When her parents attempt to force her into a loveless, high-stakes marriage, Amy does what any sensible, spirited heroine would do: she escapes. Because of her defining trait—her astonishing ordinariness—she slips away unnoticed and easily secures employment as the fourteenth assistant kitchen maid at a neighboring palace. It is here that Kaye’s narrative truly shines, shifting from a critique of upper-class superficiality to a celebration of honest labor, grassroots camaraderie, and quiet resilience. 

Without spoiling the charming twists that follow, Amy’s eventual encounter with a prince who is equally ordinary—and equally exceptional in his authenticity—cements the novel's central thesis. Kaye reminds us that real worth is not measured by royal decrees, magical blessings, or societal expectations, but by character, capability, and the courage to forge one's own path. 

*The Ordinary Princess* is neither ordinary in its execution nor forgettable in its impact. It is a sparkling, subversive gem that encourages every reader—young or old—to embrace their unique imperfections and reject the gilded cages built by others.
