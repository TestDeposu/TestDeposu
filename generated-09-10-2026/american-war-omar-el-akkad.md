# American War

**Author:** Omar El Akkad  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

What happens when the machinery of empire, so long calibrated to crush distant foreign lands, finally turns inward? Omar El Akkad’s harrowing 2017 debut, American War, poses this terrifying question with unrelenting force. Set in a fractured United States beginning in 2074, the novel chronicles a second civil war sparked by the stubborn refusal of southern states to abandon fossil fuels in the face of climate catastrophe and federal mandate. But this is not a romanticized tale of noble rebels and heroic generals. Instead, El Akkad strips away patriotic mythmaking to present a chillingly realistic portrait of total collapse, where ideological purity has been replaced by sheer, grinding survival.

At the heart of the narrative is Sarat Chestnut. When the war erupts, Sarat is just a young girl growing up in a Louisiana that is half-drowned by rising seas and patrolled by relentless unmanned drones. After her father dies and her family is shoved into Camp Patience—a bleak, sprawling detention center for displaced persons—Sarat’s childhood is effectively erased. Here, El Akkad's thematic brilliance shines brightest. He explores how radicalization is not born in a vacuum, but is instead cultivated through trauma, humiliation, and institutional cruelty. Under the tutelage of a mysterious operative known only as Albert, Sarat is meticulously hollowed out and refashioned into a living weapon. 

El Akkad’s prose is sparse, muscular, and devastatingly austere, reminiscent of Cormac McCarthy’s post-apocalyptic landscapes, yet grounded in the sobering geopolitics of our modern era. He refuses to offer easy moral binaries. The federal "Free Southern State" coalition and the northern "Blue" authorities are both guilty of atrocities, utilizing famine, biological warfare, and psychological terror. By mirroring the tactics of twenty-first-century asymmetrical warfare—refugee camps, suicide bombings, drone strikes, and radical insurgencies—the author forces American readers to look in the mirror. He suggests that the horrors typically exported abroad are already latent within the nation's own soil, waiting for the right catalyst to erupt.

American War is ultimately a tragedy of scale and intimacy. It zooms out to show the sweeping, generational consequences of national self-destruction, while zooming in painfully close on the ruination of a single human soul. Sarat’s trajectory from an innocent child chasing butterflies to a hardened instrument of vengeance is a masterclass in character development. El Akkad has crafted a modern classic of speculative fiction that feels less like a distant warning and more like an inevitable reckoning. It is a brilliant, haunting reminder that civil wars are never truly won; they are merely survived, leaving behind a scarred landscape where the ghost of a once-great nation haunts the ruins.
