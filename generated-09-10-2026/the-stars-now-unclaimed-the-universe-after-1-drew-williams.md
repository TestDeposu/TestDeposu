# The Stars Now Unclaimed (The Universe After #1)

**Author:** Drew Williams  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

If you like your science fiction served at a frantic, adrenaline-fueled velocity with the dial permanently cranked to eleven, Drew Williams’s debut novel, The Stars Now Unclaimed, demands a prime spot on your bookshelf. Launching the sweeping Universe After series, this 2018 space opera strips away the brooding melancholy often found in modern speculative fiction and replaces it with the pure, unadulterated joy of Saturday morning serials mixed with modern, high-stakes world-building. As Becky Chambers aptly noted, if you come for the exploding spaceships, you will undoubtedly stay for the intricate universe—and Williams delivers both in spades.

At the narrative and thematic heart of the novel is the tension between authoritarian control and fragile hope, manifested through a galaxy scarred by a cataclysmic event known as the Pulse. This phenomenon previously plunged countless worlds into technological and societal dark ages, and the threat of its return hangs over humanity like a guillotine. The primary vehicle for this theme is Jane Kamali, a battle-hardened agent of the Justified. Jane’s mission is both noble and ethically precarious: she scours the galaxy to recruit children possessing miraculous, unexplained gifts, gambling that these gifted youths are the galaxy’s only safeguard against another devastating Pulse. 

Contrasting this desperate bid for survival is the Pax, a looming, monolithic faction of fascist zealots who operate under the insidious belief that they are the rightful, divine rulers of the cosmos. Unscathed by the Pulse, the Pax view Jane’s mission not as salvation, but as an existential threat to their totalizing hegemony. This ideological clash elevates the book beyond a mere shoot-first-ask-questions-later adventure. Williams uses the pursuit of young Esa—a powerful telekinetic girl rescued by Jane—to explore the ethics of weaponizing children, the weight of generational trauma, and what it truly means to fight for a future you may never live to see. 

Stylistically, Williams writes with a cinematic eye and a relentless pace. The prose is punchy, action-oriented, and unapologetically direct. There is little wasted breath on flowery, overly dense exposition; instead, the universe unfolds organically through movement, dialogue, and environmental storytelling. Remnants of ancient, incomprehensible technology litter the backdrop, giving the galaxy a rich, lived-in patina of a post-collapse civilization trying to piece together the bones of its ancestors. The dialogue snaps with sharp wit and military camaraderie, grounding the high-concept science fiction in very human, relatable emotions. 

The character work anchors the cosmic chaos. Jane Kamali is a compelling protagonist—tough as nails, deeply pragmatic, yet harboring a fierce, protective core that makes her instantly endearing. When she is forced to team up with a handful of ghosts from her past and the formidable young Esa, the resulting dynamic provides the emotional glue that keeps the book from spinning entirely out of orbit. The bonds forged in the crucible of relentless pursuit give the narrative its beating heart, ensuring that readers care just as much about the quiet moments of reflection as they do about the cataclysmic space battles erupting outside the viewport.

The Stars Now Unclaimed is a triumphant, high-octane opening salvo for a universe begging to be explored. It successfully blends the grandiose scope of classic space opera with the breathless momentum of a modern thriller. Drew Williams has crafted a debut that rewards readers with relentless thrills, memorable characters, and a universe so brimming with danger and wonder that turning the final page feels like only the beginning of the journey.
