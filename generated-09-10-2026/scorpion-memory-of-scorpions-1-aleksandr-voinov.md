# Scorpion (Memory of Scorpions, #1)

**Author:** Aleksandr Voinov  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

Scorpion (Memory of Scorpions, #1) by Aleksandr Voinov thrusts readers into a shattered world where the remnants of an elite fighting unit cling to survival against a backdrop of looming empire‑building and personal vendetta. The novel’s central thread is the relentless drive to rebuild what has been lost—whether that be a brotherhood, a love, or a sense of self—while navigating a treacherous landscape where loyalty is bartered and every alliance may conceal a dagger. Kendras, the wounded Scorpion protagonist, embodies this theme: his broken foot mirrors the fissures in his past, yet his unyielding resolve to find the last of his brothers and the enigmatic leader who once held his heart fuels a narrative that feels both intimate and expansive.

Voinov’s style is lean and propulsive, mirroring the mercenary ethos of his characters. He opens with stark, sensory details— the clang of steel, the scent of antiseptic, the ache of a splintered bone— immediately grounding the reader in Kendras’s physical and emotional state. This economy of language does not sacrifice depth; rather, it amplifies it. By stripping away extraneous description, Voinov allows the inner life of his protagonist to surface through action and terse dialogue. Kendras’s thoughts are revealed not in lengthy introspection but in the split‑second decisions he makes when a mercenary named Steel offers medicine and shelter in exchange for submission and a secret task. The tension between Kendras’s innate Scorpion code—never surrender, never abandon his tribe—and the pragmatic need to accept Steel’s help creates a compelling push‑pull that drives the plot forward.

The author excels at weaving political intrigue into a personal quest. As Kendras navigates courts, camps, and clandestine meetings, Voinov paints a panorama of shifting allegiances where a smile can mask a betrayal and a whispered promise can seal an empire’s fate. The world feels lived‑in: factions have histories, motives are layered, and the stakes escalate from personal rescue to the birth of an empire. Voinov handles this expansion without losing the novel’s emotional core; each political maneuver is filtered through Kendras’s yearning to reunite with his lost lover and to resurrect the Scorpions. This dual focus prevents the story from becoming a mere chess game of power and keeps the reader invested in the human cost of ambition.

Romance and sexuality are treated with the same frankness as the battle scenes. Voinov does not shy away from depicting Kendras’s attraction to both Steel and the mysterious Scorpion leader, allowing the love triangle to evolve organically rather than as a gratuitous subplot. The intimacy scenes are rendered with a quiet intensity that mirrors the restraint of Kendras’s own demeanor—passion is felt in the brush of a hand, the shared breath before a strike, rather than in overt melodrama. This subtlety reinforces the novel’s theme that love, like loyalty, can be both a weapon and a sanctuary.

One of Voinov’s greatest strengths is his ability to balance fast‑paced action with moments of reflective stillness. Battle sequences are choreographed with a kinetic clarity that lets readers feel each parry and thrust, yet they are interspersed with quieter passages where Kendras tends to his wound, watches a sunrise over a camp, or recalls a Scorpion oath. These pauses serve as narrative breathing rooms, deepening our understanding of the protagonist’s psyche and highlighting the cost of constant warfare. The prose in these interludes often adopts a lyrical quality, using metaphor sparingly but effectively—comparing Kendras’s resolve to a scorpion’s sting, both defensive and lethal.

Despite its relatively modest length (noted as a 70,000‑word novel), Scorpion manages to feel expansive. Voinov achieves this by implying a larger world through concise world‑building: references to past campaigns, the structure of the Scorpion hierarchy, and the vague but looming threat of an emerging empire are dropped like breadcrumbs, inviting the reader to infer the rest. This technique respects the audience’s intelligence and keeps the narrative tight, avoiding the exposition dumps that can bog down fantasy epics.

Critically, the novel’s primary weakness lies in its occasional reliance on familiar tropes—the wounded hero, the possessive mercenary, the hidden leader with a mysterious past. However, Voinov mitigates this by infusing these archetypes with specific emotional truths and by grounding them in Kendras’s unwavering sense of identity as a Scorpion. The result feels less like a cliché rehash and more like a homage to the genre’s strengths, refreshed by authentic character voice and a clear thematic focus.

In sum, Aleksandr Voinov’s Scorpion is a striking debut that blends gritty action, political machination, and a poignant exploration of love and loyalty. Its main theme—the unyielding fight to rebuild what war has shattered—is resonant and universally compelling, while the author’s crisp, evocative style ensures that every page pulses with urgency and heart. Readers who crave a story where honor is tested on the blade’s edge and where personal redemption intertwines with the fate of nations will find much to admire here.
