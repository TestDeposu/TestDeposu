# Star Wars: The Evil Experiment (Jedi Apprentice, #12)

**Author:** Jude Watson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

In Star Wars: The Evil Experiment, the twelfth installment of the acclaimed Jedi Apprentice series by Jude Watson, the galaxy far, far away undergoes a microscopic examination of the fragile bonds between mentor and student. While the original synopsis highlights a narrative bridge between the prequel films, Watson accomplishes something far more profound within these pages. She strips away the sprawling cosmic politics of the Republic and traps her characters in a crucible of survival, creating an intimate, high-stakes psychological drama disguised as middle-grade adventure.

The central theme of the novel is the friction of transition—the painful, necessary evolution from isolated individualism to collaborative trust. Watson explores this primarily through the tumultuous relationship between the newly paired Obi-Wan Kenobi and his fiercely independent, exceptionally powerful Padawan, Anakin Skywalker. At fourteen, Anakin is a paradox of legendary capability and profound emotional vulnerability. He possesses the raw power to alter destiny, yet he lacks the foundational patience of the Jedi Code. When a routine mission goes catastrophically wrong and the duo is violently separated, Watson uses physical isolation as a brilliant metaphor for emotional detachment. 

Deprived of his Master’s stabilizing presence, Anakin is forced into an uneasy alliance with his peers, most notably his sharp-tongued rival, Ferus Olin. Watson’s thematic brilliance shines brightest here, as she dissects the poisonous nature of pride and competition among the young Jedi hopefuls. The evil experiment of the title is not merely the literal, sinister biological threat engineered by the book's antagonists, but rather the crucible in which Anakin’s character is tested to its absolute limits. Will his ego consume him, or will he learn the humbling truth that the Force is not just a personal weapon, but a connective tissue binding all living things? Watson answers this with gripping narrative tension, forcing Anakin to realize that survival depends as much on humility as it does on a lightsaber.

Watson’s writing style is a masterclass in economy and pacing. Longtime fans of the expanded universe know her as an author who respects her young audience without ever talking down to them. Her prose is razor-sharp, favoring brisk, cinematic action sequences that propel the plot forward without sacrificing emotional resonance. In The Evil Experiment, she adopts a breathless, claustrophobic tone that mirrors the harrowing conditions her characters endure. Every corridor feels tighter, every threat looms larger, and every choice carries the weight of a potential fall to the dark side. 

Furthermore, Watson excels at internal characterization. Even within a fast-paced action framework, she successfully captures the simmering inner turmoil of young Anakin Skywalker—a boy constantly wrestling with the specter of his own extraordinary destiny. She paints a nuanced portrait of a protagonist who is simultaneously brilliant and reckless, heroic and deeply flawed. The dialogue crackles with youthful angst, yet remains grounded in the philosophical traditions of the Jedi Order, contrasting Obi-Wan’s measured wisdom with Anakin’s impulsive defiance.

Ultimately, Star Wars: The Evil Experiment is much more than a nostalgic tie-in or a quick diversion for young readers. It is a vital character study that deepens our understanding of the prequel era's most tragic figures. Jude Watson delivers a striking, atmospheric preview of the trials that forge legends, proving that the greatest battles are often fought not against external enemies, but against the darkness within oneself. For anyone looking to understand the complex architecture of Anakin Skywalker’s fall and Obi-Wan’s agonizing dedication, this book is an indispensable, thrilling read.
