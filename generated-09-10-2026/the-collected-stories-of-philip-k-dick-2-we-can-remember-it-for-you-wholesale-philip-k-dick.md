# The Collected Stories of Philip K. Dick 2: We Can Remember it for You Wholesale

**Author:** Philip K. Dick  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Philip K. Dick’s *The Collected Stories of Philip K. Dick 2: We Can Remember It for You Wholesale* is a masterclass in existential dread, technological paranoia, and the fragile nature of human identity—all wrapped in the razor-sharp prose of a writer who saw the future before it arrived. This collection isn’t just a showcase of Dick’s most celebrated tales; it’s a mirror held up to the anxieties of the modern age, where reality is fluid, memory is malleable, and the line between illusion and truth blurs with terrifying ease.

At the heart of Dick’s fiction lies a profound interrogation of what it means to be human in a world increasingly shaped by technology, corporate manipulation, and the illusions we create to cope with chaos. Stories like *The Minority Report*—the one that inspired Spielberg’s chilling dystopia—explore the ethical nightmares of predictive policing, where free will is sacrificed on the altar of "preventive justice." Here, Dick doesn’t just warn us about the dangers of surveillance; he forces us to confront the moral ambiguity of a system that assumes it knows better than the individuals it seeks to control. The story’s tension isn’t just about the future; it’s about the present, about how easily we might trade autonomy for the illusion of safety.

Then there’s *We Can Remember It for You Wholesale*, the tale that became *Total Recall*, where memory itself becomes a commodity. Dick doesn’t just ask whether we can fake experiences—he asks whether we *should*. The story’s brilliance lies in its refusal to offer easy answers. Is the protagonist’s "false" vacation a scam, or is it a liberation? Is reality something to be clung to, or is it a cage? Dick’s genius is in making us question the very act of questioning, in turning a sci-fi premise into a philosophical minefield.

But it’s not all about grand dystopias. *The Days of Perky Pat* is a haunting meditation on escapism, where people retreat into the lives of dolls, preferring the artificial perfection of their playthings to the messiness of real existence. It’s a story about how we manufacture happiness, how we outsource our emotions to things we can control. Meanwhile, *Autofac* delivers one of Dick’s most visceral critiques of industrialization, where machines—supposedly benign—become the oppressors, reducing human life to mere components in a system designed to maximize efficiency. The story’s horror isn’t in the technology itself but in the dehumanizing logic that justifies it.

Dick’s style is as distinctive as his themes. His prose is precise, almost clinical at times, but beneath the surface lies a simmering unease. He doesn’t rely on flashy descriptions or over-the-top action; instead, he builds tension through psychological depth and moral ambiguity. His characters are often ordinary people thrust into extraordinary circumstances, and their struggles feel painfully real. Dick’s worldbuilding is subtle but devastating—he doesn’t need to spell out the dystopia; he lets the implications sink in. Take *I Hope I Shall Arrive Soon*, where a man’s reality is called into question, and the reader is left wondering: How do you know you’re not living in someone else’s nightmare?

What makes this collection so compelling is its range. Dick’s stories span decades of his career, from the early paranoia of *Adjustment Team*—where a man accidentally steps out of his own time and into a future where his past self is erased—to the later, more philosophical works like *The Builder* and *The Hanging Tree*, where themes of identity and predestination take center stage. Yet despite the diversity, there’s a unifying thread: a deep skepticism about the nature of truth, the reliability of perception, and the fragility of human agency in a world that often feels like it’s rigged against us.

Dick’s influence on sci-fi is undeniable, but his stories endure because they’re not just about the future—they’re about the present. They’re about the ways we manipulate reality, the ways we’re manipulated, and the ways we might just be living in a simulation without realizing it. In an era where deepfakes, AI-generated content, and corporate-controlled narratives blur the lines between fact and fiction, Dick’s warnings feel more urgent than ever.

This collection is essential reading for anyone who wants to understand the philosophical underpinnings of modern sci-fi, or who simply enjoys stories that make them question the world around them. Dick doesn’t just entertain; he unsettles, provokes, and challenges. And in a time when reality is increasingly constructed, curated, and contested, that’s a gift we can’t afford to ignore.
