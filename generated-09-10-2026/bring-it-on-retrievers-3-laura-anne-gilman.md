# Bring It On (Retrievers, #3)

**Author:** Laura Anne Gilman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

**A High-Stakes Dance of Duty and Defiance in *Bring It On***

Laura Anne Gilman’s *Bring It On*—the third installment in *The Retrievers* series—is a masterclass in balancing political intrigue with personal stakes, where the line between hero and pawn blurs with every page. Wren Valere, the lonejack par excellence, has spent years operating in the shadows, adhering to the first rule of her kind: *don’t get involved*. But Gilman, ever the architect of morally complex protagonists, refuses to let her heroine stay that way. Here, Wren’s carefully constructed detachment is tested not just by the Mage Council’s relentless persecution, but by the fracturing alliances in her city, where humans and non-humans teeter on the brink of open conflict. The result is a story that’s as much about the cost of silence as it is about the reckoning of action.

What makes *Bring It On* compelling isn’t just the escalating tension—though Gilman delivers that in spades—but the way she weaves Wren’s internal conflict into the fabric of the plot. Wren’s relationship with Sergei, her partner both in business and in life, serves as a microcosm of her broader struggle: she wants stability, safety, the quiet life she’s built. But the world around her is unraveling, and her refusal to look away becomes a kind of quiet rebellion. Gilman’s writing shines in these moments of tension, where Wren’s internal monologue crackles with the weight of her choices. There’s a raw honesty to her voice, a refusal to romanticize heroism, that grounds the story in something visceral. You don’t just *read* Wren’s dilemma—you *feel* it, because Gilman makes her relatable without ever making her weak.

The political undercurrents here are equally gripping. The Mage Council’s tightening grip on the city mirrors real-world themes of institutional power and resistance, but Gilman’s touch is uniquely her own. She doesn’t just present a binary conflict between oppressors and the oppressed; instead, she layers in shades of gray, showing how even the most well-intentioned actions can have unintended consequences. The rising tensions between human Talents and non-humans, for instance, aren’t just a plot device—they’re a reflection of Wren’s own internalized biases and fears. Gilman forces her readers to sit with the discomfort of moral ambiguity, asking whether Wren’s involvement will make things better or worse. It’s a question that lingers long after the last page.

Gilman’s signature style—sharp, introspective, and steeped in atmospheric detail—is on full display here. The world of *The Retrievers* is rich with texture, from the neon-lit streets of the city to the eerie, otherworldly undertones of its supernatural elements. But what truly sets this series apart is Gilman’s ability to blend urban fantasy with grounded emotional realism. The magic system, while intricate, never overshadows the human drama. Instead, it’s woven seamlessly into the narrative, enhancing the stakes without ever feeling like a gimmick. Whether it’s the subtle hints of Wren’s latent abilities or the looming threat of the Mage Council’s experiments, Gilman keeps the tension simmering just beneath the surface.

One of the most striking aspects of *Bring It On* is how it plays with the trope of the reluctant hero. Wren isn’t a classic damsel in distress, nor is she a fearless warrior—she’s a woman who’s spent years protecting herself, only to realize that protection sometimes means doing nothing. Gilman’s portrayal of her is nuanced; Wren’s growth isn’t about becoming a different person, but about learning when to step forward and when to hold back. It’s a subtle but powerful message about agency and the limits of control, especially in a world where power is often wielded by those who refuse to answer for it.

The pacing of *Bring It On* is another strength. Gilman balances action-packed sequences with quieter, introspective moments, ensuring that the emotional core of the story never gets lost in the chaos. The job Wren takes on—one she shouldn’t have—serves as a catalyst for the entire narrative, forcing her to confront not just external threats, but the quiet, gnawing fear that she’s been avoiding for too long. The result is a story that feels both urgent and deeply personal, where every decision Wren makes carries weight, and every consequence ripples outward in unexpected ways.

For fans of Gilman’s work, *Bring It On* is a natural evolution of the series, deepening the lore while keeping the focus on Wren’s journey. But it’s also a standout in its own right, offering a fresh take on the "hero’s reluctant involvement" trope. Gilman’s writing is as sharp as ever, her characters as layered, and her world as immersive. If you’ve followed Wren’s story from the beginning, you’ll be rewarded with a satisfying continuation. But even if you’re new to *The Retrievers*, *Bring It On* delivers a compelling entry point—a story about the cost of silence, the burden of choice, and the messy, beautiful complexity of being human in a world that wants to control you.

In the end, *Bring It On* is more than just another installment in a series; it’s a testament to Gilman’s ability to craft stories that are as much about the internal struggle as they are about the external conflict. It’s a book that lingers, not just because of its twists or its world-building, but because of the way it makes you question what you’d do when the world demands you step up—and whether you’d have the courage to say no.
