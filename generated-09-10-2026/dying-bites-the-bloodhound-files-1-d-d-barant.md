# Dying Bites (The Bloodhound Files, #1)

**Author:** D.D. Barant  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

**Dying Bites: Where the FBI Meets the Undead—and the Killer is Human**

D.D. Barant’s *Dying Bites* isn’t just another urban fantasy romp through a world of vampires and werewolves—it’s a razor-sharp thriller that flips the script on the genre itself. At its core, this is a story about isolation, the fragility of sanity, and the terrifying idea that the real monster might not be the one with fangs or fur. Set in a parallel universe where humans are a rare minority, *Dying Bites* follows FBI profiler Jace Valchek, a woman whose expertise in unraveling the minds of serial killers suddenly becomes the key to saving a world that doesn’t understand madness.

The brilliance of Barant’s world-building lies in its subversion of expectations. Most urban fantasies focus on the supernatural conflicts—vampires vs. werewolves, ancient curses, and political intrigue among the undead. But here, the real threat isn’t the supernatural at all. It’s a human serial killer, a man who sees himself as a god, hunting down vampires and werewolves not out of malice, but because he believes he’s performing a divine purge. The horror isn’t just in the violence; it’s in the realization that in a world where most beings are immortal, a single human mind could unravel everything. Jace, stranded in this bizarre reality, must navigate a landscape where Bela Lugosi is a cultural icon, full moons are celebrated like Mardi Gras, and the NSA is run by a vampire. Her job—tracking the fractured minds of killers—suddenly takes on existential stakes.

Barant’s prose is tight, witty, and relentlessly engaging. The dialogue crackles with sharpness, whether it’s the dry professionalism of Jace’s FBI training or the eerie detachment of the supernatural beings she encounters. The pacing is relentless, with each chapter peeling back another layer of the mystery while keeping the tension high. There’s a cleverness to the way the story balances the procedural elements of a serial killer hunt with the broader stakes of a collapsing world. Jace isn’t just a detective; she’s an outsider in every sense, and her struggle to adapt to this new reality—where her own humanity is both a weapon and a liability—adds depth to the narrative.

What makes *Dying Bites* stand out is its fresh take on the urban fantasy formula. Instead of another love triangle or a battle between factions of the supernatural, Barant delivers a story that’s as much about psychological horror as it is about monsters. The killer’s motives are chillingly logical, and the way the supernatural beings react to his actions—some horrified, others fascinated—creates a rich, morally complex landscape. It’s a world where morality is fluid, where power is measured in centuries, and where a single human mind could tip the balance of existence.

The book’s strength lies in its protagonist, Jace Valchek. She’s not a damsel in distress or a reluctant heroine; she’s a woman who’s seen too much, who trusts her instincts, and who refuses to back down from a fight. Her relationship with David Cassius, the vampire NSA director, is particularly compelling—equal parts professional partnership and uneasy alliance. Their dynamic adds layers of tension, as trust is hard-won and betrayal is always a possibility.

For fans of urban fantasy who crave something more than just another vampire romance or werewolf drama, *Dying Bites* is a breath of fresh air. It’s a story that blends the procedural elements of a crime thriller with the supernatural intrigue of urban fantasy, all while keeping the focus on the human element—both in the killer and in Jace herself. The result is a book that’s as thought-provoking as it is entertaining, a tale where the real monsters might not have fangs, but their minds are just as terrifying.

If you’re looking for a story that subverts expectations, delivers sharp writing, and keeps you guessing until the very end, *Dying Bites* is a must-read. It’s a testament to Barant’s skill in crafting a world that feels both alien and eerily familiar, where the line between hero and villain is blurred, and where the greatest threat might be the one you least expect.
