# The Iron Wars (The Monarchies of God, #3)

**Author:** Paul Kearney  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

The third installment of Paul Kearney’s Monarchies of God series, *The Iron Wars*, plunges the reader straight into the jagged aftermath of a throne‑room betrayal. King Abeleyn of Hebrion lies broken in a coma, his once‑glittering court reduced to a powder keg where his fiancée and a former lover sharpen their blades for a succession struggle that feels less like politics and more like a blood‑soaked duel of egos. Into this volatile vacuum steps Corfe Cear‑Inaf, promoted to colonel but saddled with a ragtag band of ill‑equipped savages and a covert order that smells of sabotage. His mission is presented as a forgone failure—a deliberate exile engineered by a jealous monarch who wishes to see him crumble. Yet Kearney never lets the premise settle into simple tragedy; instead, he threads Corfe’s desperate march with a relentless, almost tactile sense of dread that makes every muddy step feel like a march toward an inevitable, yet still shocking, climax.

Parallel to Corfe’s forsaken campaign, the novel follows Richard Hawkwood and Lord Murad as they return from a newly discovered continent bearing tidings of horror that ripple outward like a dark tide. Their cargo hold hides something ancient and ravenous, a threat that promises to dwarf the petty squabbles of Hebrion’s nobility. Kearney deftly intertwines these two narrative strands, using the external menace as a mirror to the internal corruption festering within the kingdom. The result is a story where personal ambition and cosmic horror are not separate themes but two sides of the same blood‑stained coin.

What stands out most in Kearney’s prose is his unflinching willingness to paint violence, lust, and the hunger for power in vivid, almost tactile detail. He does not shy away from the gore of a battlefield or the slick, sweaty intimacy of a betrayal; instead, he renders each scene with a cinematic clarity that makes the reader feel the weight of a sword‑blow and the chill of a whispered promise. Yet this explicitness never feels gratuitous. It serves the larger purpose of exposing how easily civilization’s veneer cracks when primal drives are left unchecked. The author’s style is marked by short, punchy sentences that accelerate during action sequences, giving the combat a breath‑less urgency, while longer, more lyrical passages linger on the political machinations and the creeping sense of doom that settles over the continents like a fog.

The central theme of *The Iron Wars* emerges as the corrosive nature of power when it is divorced from responsibility. Abeleyn’s incapacitation creates a power vacuum that is filled not by wise counsel but by the raw, unfiltered ambition of those who see a crown as a prize to be seized rather than a duty to uphold. Corfe’s hopeless mission becomes a crucible that tests whether honor can survive when the system that ordained it is already rotting. Simultaneously, the eldritch horror lurking in the ship’s hold acts as an external manifestation of the internal rot—a reminder that the true monsters are often those we invite in through our own greed and fear. Kearney suggests that when leaders prioritize personal glory over the welfare of their people, they open the door to forces—both human and supernatural—that will devour everything they cherish.

The novel’s pacing is relentless; there are few moments of respite, and each chapter ends with a hook that compels the reader forward. This can be exhausting for those who prefer a more measured, contemplative fantasy, but it perfectly matches the book’s tone of impending catastrophe. The world‑building remains rich without becoming encyclopedic; Kearney drops hints of ancient gods, forgotten wars, and shifting allegiances through dialogue and occasional flashbacks, allowing the reader to piece together the larger tapestry without slowing the narrative’s drive.

In terms of character, Corfe stands out as a reluctant hero whose grit and stubbornness make him sympathetic despite his brutal methods. Hawkwood and Murad provide a counterpoint of world‑weary experience, their camaraderie offering brief glints of levity amidst the gloom. The female contenders for the throne—Abeleyn’s fiancee and former lover—are portrayed with enough complexity to avoid becoming mere pawns; their motivations are rooted in love, loss, and a fierce desire to shape their own destinies, even as they are swept up in the larger machinations of power.

*The Iron Wars* is a stark, uncompromising addition to the Monarchies of God saga. It blends gritty military fantasy with creeping cosmic horror, delivering a narrative that is as much about the internal wars waged within the human heart as it is about the clang of steel on foreign shores. Kearney’s style—direct, visceral, and unapologetically bold—makes the book a gripping read for fans who appreciate fantasy that does not flinch from the darkness lurking both within and without its characters.
