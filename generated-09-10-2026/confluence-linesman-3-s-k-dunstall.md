# Confluence (Linesman, #3)

**Author:** S.K. Dunstall  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

Confluence, the third entry in S.K. Dunstall’s Linesman trilogy, deepens the series’ core premise: humanity’s reliance on mysterious, quantifiable “lines” that enable faster‑than‑light travel, and the rare individuals who can hear and manipulate them. Ean Lambert remains the story’s beating heart—a self‑taught, slum‑born linesman whose unorthodox ability to perceive the lines as music sets him apart from the sterile, guild‑trained elite. When an alien vessel appears at the rim of explored space, its lethal defense field forces every power to confront a paradox: the very technology that binds the galaxy together may also be its undoing unless someone can speak its language.

Dunstall’s greatest strength lies in turning a hard‑science concept into a visceral, almost lyrical experience. The “song” of the lines is rendered with such sensory detail that readers can almost hear the hum beneath Ean’s fingertips, making the abstract mechanics of faster‑than‑light travel feel intimate and personal. This auditory motif dovetails neatly with the novel’s central theme: communication as both bridge and barrier. The alien ship does not simply pose a military threat; it challenges humanity’s assumption that dominance over the lines equates to mastery over the cosmos. Ean’s willingness to listen—rather than to command—forces the competing factions to reconsider whether true progress requires humility and empathy, not just technical prowess.

Stylistically, Dunstall balances brisk, plot‑driven chapters with moments of quiet introspection. Her prose is clean and unpretentious, allowing the world‑building to emerge through action and dialogue rather than exhaustive exposition. The political machinations of the galactic powers feel grounded, each faction’s motives clear enough to drive tension without overwhelming the narrative. Supporting characters—ranging from the wary cartel leaders to the skeptical linesman peers—serve as foils that highlight Ean’s isolation and, ultimately, his quiet leadership.

What sets Confluence apart from typical space‑opera fare is its insistence that the most powerful technology is meaningless without the capacity to understand the “other.” Dunstall refuses to glorify brute force; instead, she celebrates the quiet, persistent act of listening as a revolutionary stance. The novel’s climax, therefore, feels earned: it is not a spectacular explosion of weaponry but a resonant chord that redefines humanity’s relationship with the lines that carry them across the stars.

In sum, Confluence is a thought‑provoking blend of hard science and emotional depth, anchored by a compelling protagonist and a narrative that sings as loudly as the lines it explores. Fans of intelligent, character‑driven SF will find much to admire in Dunstall’s latest offering.
