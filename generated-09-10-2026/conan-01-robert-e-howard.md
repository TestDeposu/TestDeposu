# Conan 01

**Author:** Robert E. Howard  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

**A Sword, a Storm, and the Birth of a Legend: Robert E. Howard’s *Conan the Barbarian* (Volume 1)**

Robert E. Howard’s *Conan the Barbarian* (2016 edition) is not merely a collection of stories—it is the raw, unpolished birth of a myth. These 18 tales, first published in *Weird Tales* between 1933 and 1936, are the foundation upon which the entire Hyborian Age was built. Here, in their original pulp glory, is Conan not as he’s been sanitized for modern audiences, but as Howard envisioned him: a brutal, cunning, and endlessly adaptable survivor whose only law is his own survival. This volume, reimagined with updated illustrations and arranged in Rippke’s meticulous chronology, offers a visceral, unfiltered glimpse into the mind of the man who created the ultimate sword-and-sorcery hero.

At its core, *Conan the Barbarian* is a celebration of primal force—both in its protagonist and the world that shapes him. Howard’s Conan is no knight in shining armor; he is a Cimmerian, a man of the north, raised in the blood-soaked crucible of war. His early stories, like *The Frost Giant’s Daughter* and *Rogues in the House*, paint him as a young mercenary, a thief with a knife and a knack for outsmarting foes far larger than himself. These tales are lean, brutal, and steeped in the kind of action that feels like a living, breathing nightmare. Howard’s prose is sparse but devastating, his descriptions so vivid they leap off the page: the "black, jagged teeth" of the Frost Giant’s daughter, the "iron shadows" that stalk Conan through the moonlit streets of a foreign city. There’s no romanticizing here—just raw, unflinching violence, where survival is a daily gamble and mercy is a luxury few can afford.

What makes these stories endure is Howard’s ability to balance Conan’s ferocity with a deep, almost poetic understanding of the world around him. The Hyborian Age is a land of clashing civilizations—Aquilonian knights, Stygian sorcerers, Shemitic merchants, and the ever-present threat of the "Black King" and his cults. Howard doesn’t just drop Conan into this world; he immerses him in its contradictions. In *The Pool of the Black One*, Conan faces a cult of fanatics who worship a monstrous entity, their devotion twisted by fear and superstition. In *The Devil in Iron*, he battles a sorcerer who wields dark magic with chilling precision. These aren’t just action scenes; they’re studies in power, in the clash between brute strength and arcane cunning. Howard understands that Conan’s greatest weapon isn’t his sword—it’s his mind, his ability to read a room, a man, a situation, and turn it to his advantage.

Yet for all his cunning, Conan is never a hero in the traditional sense. He’s a survivor, a man who does what he must to stay alive, whether that means betraying allies, seducing princesses, or slaughtering entire armies. His morality is fluid, his loyalties temporary. This is a man who, in *Red Nails*, steals a princess from a rival king only to abandon her when the going gets tough. In *The Hour of the Dragon*, he becomes a king—but only because he’s forced to, and even then, his rule is as much about survival as it is about governance. Howard doesn’t glorify Conan; he presents him as he is—a product of his time, a man who thrives in chaos but is never above it.

The real genius of these stories lies in Howard’s world-building. The Hyborian Age is a living, breathing entity, a patchwork of cultures and conflicts that feel eerily familiar yet utterly alien. Howard’s essays, like *The Hyborian Age—Conan’s World*, provide a fascinating backdrop, explaining the politics, the geography, and the mythos that shape Conan’s adventures. It’s a world where magic is real but rare, where kings rise and fall in the blink of an eye, and where the line between civilization and barbarism is as thin as a drawn sword. This isn’t just a setting for Conan’s exploits; it’s a character in its own right, one that Howard populates with memorable villains, from the monstrous Black Colossus to the enigmatic sorcerer Thoth-Amon.

The illustrations in this edition are a welcome addition, capturing the gritty, almost prehistoric feel of Howard’s world. They’re not just decorative—they’re essential, bringing to life the towering giants, the shadowy cultists, and the exotic landscapes that Conan traverses. Together with the text, they create a sense of immediacy, as if you’re standing right there in the dust-choked streets of a Hyborian city, the scent of blood and incense thick in the air.

For modern readers, some of these stories may feel dated—Conan’s misogyny, his casual racism, and his unapologetic brutality are products of their time. But that’s part of their power. Howard didn’t write these tales with political correctness in mind; he wrote them with a single goal: to create the ultimate action hero, a man who embodies the untamed spirit of the wild. And in that, he succeeded.

*Conan the Barbarian* (Volume 1) is more than a collection of adventure stories—it’s a time capsule, a snapshot of pulp fiction at its most daring. It’s a book for readers who love their heroes unapologetically flawed, their worlds rich with danger, and their prose sharp enough to cut like a sword. If you’ve ever wanted to experience the raw, unfiltered birth of a legend, this is the place to start.
