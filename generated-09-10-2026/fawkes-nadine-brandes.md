# Fawkes

**Author:** Nadine Brandes  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

History is written by the victors, but Nadine Brandes rewrites it with pigment, plague, and peril. In her 2018 young adult historical fantasy novel, Fawkes, Brandes takes the infamous 1605 Gunpowder Plot—a moment etched in British history as a failed conspiracy to blow up Parliament and assassinate King James I—and brilliantly injects it with a lethal dose of magic. The result is a vibrant, high-stakes reimagining that asks a timeless question: what happens when the legacy you inherit is a fuse waiting to be lit?

At the narrative center is Thomas Fawkes, son of the notorious Guy Fawkes. While history remembers his father as a fanatic, Thomas faces a much more immediate and terrifying enemy than the crown: the Stone Plague. Creeping silently through his body, this affliction turns its victims to literal stone, threatening to rob Thomas of his future before it even begins. In a world where social standing and survival depend on the Color Test—a ritual granting individuals magical control over specific elements like wood, shadow, or blood—Thomas is desperate for a gray mask that might cure him. Instead, he is met with abandonment by his father, plunging him into a desperate quest that forces him into the epicenter of the treasonous plot to kill the king. 

Brandes crafts her world through a striking thematic lens centered on identity, loyalty, and the dangerous allure of absolute truth. The magic system of color masks serves as a brilliant metaphor for societal division and conformity. Just as the color-coded masks dictate a person’s function and place in seventeenth-century London, so too do inherited ideologies dictate Thomas’s path. The author explores how easily people are blinded by the colors they wear, blindly following the factional lines drawn by their parents, their rulers, or their peers. Thomas’s internal conflict brilliantly mirrors this external magic; he must peel away the layers of what he has been taught to believe in order to discover his own moral compass.

Compounding this thematic depth is the introduction of Emma Areben, a formidable foil to the rigid factionalism of Thomas's world. Controlling not just one color, but all of them, Emma represents limitless potential and a profound connection to creation rather than destruction. Through Emma, Brandes champions the theme of autonomy. She challenges the characters—and by extension, the readers—to question whether loyalty is owed blindly to family and tradition, or consciously to truth and righteousness. The contrast between Guy Fawkes’s explosive, destructive agenda and Emma’s holistic, unifying magic forms the ideological battleground where Thomas’s soul hangs in the balance.

Stylistically, Brandes writes with a rich, sensory prose that brings a plague-ravaged, seventeenth-century London vividly to life. Her background in historical fantasy shines through her meticulous pacing. The novel begins as a slow-burning character study of a desperate, dying boy seeking his father's approval, but it steadily accelerates into a breathless, pulse-pounding thriller as the date of the Fifth of November draws near. Brandes avoids the trap of a sanitized historical backdrop; the threat of the Stone Plague infuses every page with a chilling, body-horror urgency that grounds the whimsical elements of color magic in stark, fatal reality. 

Fawkes is ultimately a tale about the pigments we use to paint our own destinies. Brandes takes a dusty page from the history books and breathes vibrant, terrifying life into it, delivering a gripping narrative about the heavy cost of loyalty and the courage it takes to choose your own truth. It is an imaginative, emotionally resonant adventure that will linger in the reader's mind long after the final page is turned.
