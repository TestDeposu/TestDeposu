# Running from the Deity (Pip & Flinx, #11)

**Author:** Alan Dean Foster  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2005

When science fiction stalwart Alan Dean Foster returns to his most beloved pairing in Running from the Deity, the eleventh installment of the sprawling Pip and Flinx series, he manages to inject a fresh breath of cosmic dread into a universe that has long felt comfortably familiar. For decades, readers have watched Philip Xxur—known simply as Flinx—grow from an intuitive urchin with a deadly miniature flying snake into a reluctant savior. In this 2005 contribution, Foster pivots away from localized political intrigue and petty criminals, elevating the stakes to a truly staggering, apocalyptic scale without ever sacrificing the intimate character-driven charm that defines the series.

The central theme of Running from the Deity is the crushing, often absurd burden of involuntary divinity. Flinx has always possessed unique, if volatile, mental abilities, but here he finds himself literally cast in the role of a god. Threatened by an ancient, mindless primordial horror leaking out of the Great Emptiness across three hundred million light-years of space, the Commonwealth relies on Flinx to locate and activate an extinct race’s planet-sized sentient weapon. Yet, true to the trademark irony of Foster’s storytelling, before Flinx can save the galaxy from annihilation, he must first escape the perils of piety. 

When his spaceship forces him down on the restricted, pre-spaceflight world of Arrawd, Flinx’s desperate need for repairs is thoroughly derailed by the local populace. Instead of facing the customary bounty hunters and assassins who usually pepper his existence, Flinx is besieged by something far more terrifying to a guy just trying to do his job: hordes of well-meaning sentients bent on worshipping him. Foster brilliantly uses this detour to explore the absurdity of messiah complexes. Flinx does not want followers, temples, or hymns; he wants his ship fixed so he can convince a sentient planet to destroy a cosmic behemoth. The clash between high-stakes space opera and the awkward comedy of accidental religious reverence forms the beating heart of the narrative.

Stylistically, Foster remains a master of brisk, accessible, and evocative world-building. His prose is unpretentious, prioritizing momentum and wonder. He excels at painting vivid, tactile alien environments with just a few brushstrokes, making the lush vistas of Arrawd feel as real and breathing as the cold, terrifying abyss of the Great Emptiness. The dialogue sparkles with the characteristic dry wit shared between Flinx and his fiercely loyal, venomous pip-pet, Pip, anchoring the cosmic madness in a recognizable, familial bond. Even when dealing with multi-megaparsec horrors and extinct mega-weapons, Foster never loses sight of the human—and alien—heart at the center of his universe.

Running from the Deity is a thoroughly entertaining ride that rewards longtime fans while offering enough standalone wonder to captivate newcomers. It balances existential terror with gentle satire, proving that even when the universe is ending, there is always room for a little chaos, a lot of charm, and a hero who would much rather be anywhere else.
