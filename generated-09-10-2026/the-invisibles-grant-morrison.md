# The Invisibles

**Author:** Grant Morrison  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2001

Grant Morrison’s The Invisibles stands as one of the most audacious, mind-bending, and politically subversive works in the history of graphic literature. Operating at the intersection of pop culture, chaos magic, high philosophy, and counterculture rebellion, Morrison crafts a narrative that is less a traditional comic book and more an active psychological transmission. Volume two of this landmark series dives deeper into the frantic labyrinth of a hidden war where reality itself is the ultimate battleground, fought between the monolithic forces of totalitarian control and the chaotic, liberating anarchy of the human spirit.

At the beating heart of The Invisibles lies the eternal struggle between authority and anarchy. Morrison views reality not as a static environment, but as a construct—a prison of consensus perception engineered by malevolent archival forces known as the Conspiracy. Against this monolithic control stands the eponymous cell of freedom fighters: the Invisibles. They are a ragtag, beautifully misfit crew of psychic terrorists, time-travelers, and revolutionaries. In this volume, we witness the terrifying escalation of the war as the Conspiracy launches its most brutal, direct assault yet. The tension is palpable, weaponized by the constant, dizzying sensation that nothing is as it seems, and that trust is a luxury no revolutionary can afford.

What elevates this volume—and the series as a whole—is Morrison's relentless fascination with identity, transformation, and marginalized power. This is brilliantly embodied in the exploration of Lord Fanny, the transvestite Brazilian shaman whose harrowing, vivid origin story is finally laid bare in these pages. Fanny is a masterclass in character writing: fierce, vulnerable, profane, and deeply connected to the Earth's most ancient, primordial magic. Through Fanny and the newest recruit, the cynical scouser Dane McGowan, Morrison champions the idea that true power comes from embracing the margins, shattering binaries, and rewriting the self. The personal is truly political, and in Morrison's universe, liberating your own mind is the first and most dangerous step toward liberating the world.

Morrison’s writing style in The Invisibles is nothing short of hallucinatory. It is dense, heavily layered, fiercely intelligent, and dripping with occult symbolism. Dialogue snaps with punk-rock cynicism one moment and shifts into transcendental philosophy the next. Morrison famously intended the comic to be a magical talisman, a sigil designed to alter the reader’s consciousness, and that intent bleeds through every panel. The pacing is relentless, echoing the breathless panic of urban guerrilla warfare mixed with the boundless expanses of hyperspace. It demands an active, engaged reader willing to let go of linear comfort and surrender to the chaotic flow of ideas.

Ultimately, this volume of The Invisibles is a thrilling, provocative piece of speculative fiction that refuses to offer easy answers. It challenges us to question the structures of power we accept every day, from media consumption to governmental control. Even as the characters face annihilation and the labyrinth of deception tightens around them, the book pulses with a defiant, punk-rock joy. It is a vital dispatch from the edge of imagination, proving that art can still be a weapon, and that ideas are truly bulletproof.
