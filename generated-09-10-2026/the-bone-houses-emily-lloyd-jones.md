# The Bone Houses

**Author:** Emily Lloyd-Jones  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

The Bone Houses by Emily Lloyd‑Jones reads like a whispered legend that has been unearthed from the mist‑shrouded hills of medieval Wales and set to pulse with a restless, almost feral heartbeat. At its core, the novel grapples with the weight of inherited grief and the stubborn insistence of the past to refuse burial. Seventeen‑year‑old Aderyn—known to everyone as Ryn—tends her family’s graveyard in the isolated village of Colbren, a place where the dead have a disconcerting habit of rising again as “bone houses.” This supernatural blight is not merely a plot device; it is the physical manifestation of unresolved sorrow, a curse that festers whenever the living ignore the debts they owe to those who have gone before. As Ryn and the enigmatic mapmaker Ellis venture into the craggy mountains seeking the source of the malaise, the story transforms into a pilgrimage of self‑discovery, forcing both characters to confront the parts of themselves they have buried alongside the dead.

Lloyd‑Jones’s style is where the novel truly shines. She writes with a lyrical, almost incantatory cadence that feels simultaneously ancient and immediate. Descriptions of the Welsh landscape are rendered in rich, tactile detail—craggy ridges “like the spine of some slumbering beast,” fog that clings to the stones of the graveyard “as if the earth itself were holding its breath.” Yet this lyricism never overwhelms the narrative drive; instead, it sharpens the tension, making each step toward the mountain feel like a careful tread on thin ice. The author balances the creeping dread of classic horror with the wonder of a fairy tale, allowing moments of stark violence to sit beside passages of quiet, almost pastoral beauty. This duality mirrors the book’s thematic core: the thin line between life and death is not a wall but a membrane, porous enough for both terror and tenderness to seep through.

Characterization is another strength. Ryn is a protagonist whose ferocity is tempered by a deep, aching sense of responsibility. She is not a typical “chosen one” heroine; her power lies in her stubborn love for her siblings and her refusal to let the graveyard fall into ruin. Ellis, meanwhile, arrives with a mapmaker’s precision and a past shrouded in mystery, offering a foil to Ryn’s grounded pragmatism. Their partnership evolves from wary alliance to something resembling trust, and their dialogue crackles with a restrained intensity that feels true to the period without sounding stilted. Supporting characters—Ryn’s siblings, the wary villagers, the lingering specters of the fae—are sketched with enough specificity to populate the world without stealing focus from the central journey.

The novel’s pacing mirrors the ascent into the mountains: it begins with a slow, deliberate establishment of atmosphere and stakes, then quickens as the bone houses grow more aggressive and the duo’s quest gains urgency. Lloyd‑Jones knows when to linger on a haunting image—a bone‑clad hand scraping against a coffin lid, the echo of a lullaby sung over a grave—and when to thrust the reader forward into action. This rhythm keeps the tension taut while allowing space for the novel’s deeper meditations on memory, legacy, and the ways communities narrate (or silence) their own histories.

If there is a minor critique, it lies in the occasional reliance on familiar tropes of the “cursed land” and the “reluctant hero with a hidden past.” While Lloyd‑Jones infuses these elements with fresh Welsh folklore and a distinctive voice, seasoned readers of YA horror may anticipate certain beats before they arrive. Nevertheless, the emotional authenticity of Ryn’s journey and the atmospheric richness of the setting largely outweigh these predictabilities, making the familiar feel freshly haunting.

In sum, The Bone Houses is a hauntingly beautiful fusion of horror and fairy tale that uses its supernatural premise to explore very human themes: how we cope with loss, how we honor those who came before us, and how the stories we tell ourselves can either bind us to the past or set us free. Emily Lloyd‑Jones’s lyrical prose, keen sense of place, and emotionally resonant characters make this novel a standout entry in contemporary YA fantasy—one that lingers long after the final page is turned, much like the echo of a bone house’s restless sigh in the Welsh wind.
