# Prey (Infected, #1)

**Author:** Andrea Speed  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

Title: Walking the Line Between Beast and Badge: A Review of Andrea Speed’s Prey

In a literary landscape frequently saturated with sanitized, sparkling urban fantasy, Andrea Speed’s 2020 novel Prey carves out a brutal, fiercely unapologetic niche. As the opening installment of the Infected series, Prey thrusts readers headfirst into a world irrevocably altered by a pathogen that turns ordinary humans into "infecteds"—specifically, werecats. Speed does not use lycanthropy merely as a trendy supernatural metaphor; instead, she treats it as a complex societal fault line, a biological reality that triggers systemic oppression, religious fervor, and violent prejudice. 

At the pulsing, cynical heart of this gritty urban landscape is Roan McKichan. A born-infected and a disgraced ex-cop turned private investigator, Roan is a masterclass in hardboiled character design. He is tired, limping, sarcastic, and navigating a society that alternately fears, hates, or fetishizes his kind. When the murder of a former police officer pulls Roan into a convoluted web of investigations, Speed wastes no time establishing her primary thematic engine: the commodification and criminalization of otherness. 

The brilliance of Prey lies in how Speed braids multiple disparate plot threads into a singular, suffocating rope. Alongside the murdered ex-cop, Roan must track down a missing teenage boy deeply enmeshed in the dangerous subculture of "cat obsession." Compounding the chaos is Eli Winters, the charismatic leader of the Church of the Divine Transformation—a religious organization that both reveres and exploits infecteds—who hires Roan to hunt down a serial killer targeting their community. Paired with his tiger-strain partner, Paris Lehane, Roan’s investigations quickly morph into a descent through layers of societal rot, personal grudges, and visceral danger. 

Speed’s narrative style is unapologetically direct, gritty, and kinetic. She writes with the rhythm of classic noir fiction, infusing her prose with a sharp, cynical wit that acts as Roan’s psychological armor against a hostile world. The world-building is visceral and lived-in; Speed does not rely on heavy-handed exposition. Instead, the socioeconomic reality of being infected—the institutional discrimination, the black-market thrills, the physical toll of alternate strains—is woven seamlessly into the DNA of the narrative. The pacing is relentless, echoing the ticking-clock urgency of a traditional detective procedural, yet elevated by the high-stakes biology of predatory instincts and shifting power dynamics.

The central theme of Prey is the friction between human civilization and primal nature. Through Roan and Paris, Speed explores what it means to possess biological impulses that society deems monstrous while simultaneously trying to uphold a moral code. The "infecteds" are not simple victims or mindless beasts; they possess agency, flaws, and internal politics that mirror human prejudice on a magnified scale. The introduction of an unidentifiable, highly intelligent feline species further blurs the line between human and animal, raising unsettling questions about evolution, dominion, and empathy.

Ultimately, Prey is a triumph of speculative noir. Andrea Speed has crafted a compelling, sharply written thriller that subverts traditional monster tropes in favor of a profound examination of bigotry and survival. With its unforgettable protagonist, intricate plotting, and relentless atmospheric tension, Prey sinks its claws into the reader from the very first page and refuses to let go.
