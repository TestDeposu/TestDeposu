# Deceit (Beastly Tales, #2)

**Author:** M.J. Haag  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

**A Curse of Desire and Defiance: *Deceit* by M.J. Haag**

M.J. Haag’s *Deceit*—the second installment in *Beastly Tales*—is a dark, sensual, and psychologically layered retelling of *Beauty and the Beast* that strips away the fairy-tale veneer to expose the raw, intoxicating tension between obsession and redemption. Here, the curse isn’t just a spell; it’s a mirror, reflecting the beast’s fractured self and the woman who dares to see beyond the monster. Haag’s prose is sharp as a dagger, her dialogue crackling with wit and venom, while her exploration of power, control, and the cost of love makes this a story that lingers long after the last page.

At its heart, *Deceit* is a battle of wills—one where Benella, the former Beauty, is no longer the passive princess but a woman who has learned to navigate the beast’s labyrinthine estate with cunning and defiance. She’s no longer the girl who feared her captor; she’s the one who studies him, who notices the way his claws flex when he’s frustrated, the way his voice drops to a growl when he’s near her. But Benella isn’t just a voyeur. She’s a strategist, a survivor who understands that the beast’s rage is as much a weapon as his curse. And yet, for all her intelligence, she’s not immune to the pull of him—a man who is both her jailer and her salvation.

Haag’s strength lies in her unflinching portrayal of desire as both a curse and a catalyst. The beast isn’t a mindless brute; he’s a man tormented by the enchantress’s whispers, by his own self-loathing, and by the knowledge that he’s trapped in a body that repels the one woman who might save him. His frustration isn’t just about the physical restrictions of his curse—it’s about the psychological ones. He’s been told he’s unworthy of love, and Benella, with her sharp tongue and sharper mind, is the first to challenge that narrative. But the tension between them isn’t just about breaking the curse; it’s about whether either of them can break free from the roles they’ve been forced into.

The sexual situations in *Deceit* are not mere titillation—they’re a battleground. Haag writes desire with a precision that feels both visceral and psychological, making the act of love (or the threat of it) a tool of power. The beast’s restraint is as much a part of his curse as his fangs; Benella’s resistance is her armor. Their dynamic is a dance of dominance and submission, but never without mutual respect—or the threat of violence. Haag doesn’t shy away from the darker aspects of their relationship, where trust is a fragile thing and every touch could be a test.

What makes *Deceit* stand out in the crowded retelling genre is Haag’s refusal to romanticize the beast. He’s not a tragic hero in the traditional sense; he’s a man who has been broken by his own choices and the cruelty of fate. Benella, meanwhile, is no naive damsel. She’s a survivor, a woman who has learned to play the game of her captor’s rules while secretly plotting her own escape. Their relationship is a push-and-pull of mutual destruction and mutual growth, where every moment of intimacy is a step closer to either salvation or ruin.

The prose is lush but never overwrought, with a rhythm that mirrors the tension between the two protagonists. Haag’s descriptions are vivid, from the oppressive weight of the enchanted estate to the raw, almost feral energy of the beast’s desire. The dialogue is razor-sharp, filled with barbs and double entendres that keep the reader guessing about who holds the upper hand.

*Deceit* isn’t just another *Beauty and the Beast* retelling—it’s a story about the cost of love, the nature of power, and the ways in which we are both the architects and the victims of our own curses. Haag’s writing is bold, her characters are complex, and her exploration of desire and defiance makes this a tale that’s as compelling as it is controversial. It’s a book that will leave you breathless, questioning, and utterly hooked—because in the end, the real beast isn’t the man in the castle. It’s the curse of wanting what you can’t have, and the defiance it takes to break free.
