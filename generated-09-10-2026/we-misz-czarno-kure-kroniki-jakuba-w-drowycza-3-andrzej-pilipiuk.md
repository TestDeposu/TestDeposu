# Weźmisz czarno kure... (Kroniki Jakuba Wędrowycza, #3)

**Author:** Andrzej Pilipiuk  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

Weźmisz czarno kure… (The Black Chicken…) plunges readers back into the rollicking world of Jakub Wędrowycz, the lovable rogue‑hero of Andrzej Pilipiuk’s beloved Kroniki Jakuba Wędrowycza series. Set in a sleepy Polish hamlet where the line between the everyday and the uncanny is perpetually blurred, the novel opens with Jakub stumbling upon a jet‑black chicken whose ominous clucking seems to herald a cascade of supernatural mischief. From mischievous house spirits to a scheming local magistrate who insists on taxing the very air, Jakub’s attempts to keep his tavern tab paid and his heart unbroken spiral into a series of absurd, yet strangely poignant, encounters.

At its core, the book explores the tension between age‑old folk belief and the relentless march of modernity—a theme Pilipiuk treats with both affection and irreverent wit. Jakub, neither saint nor sinner, embodies the trickster archetype: he outwits demons not with sword or spell but with sharp tongue, a knack for improvisation, and a healthy dose of self‑deprecating humor. Through his misadventures, Pilipiuk asks whether rationalism can ever truly dispel the magic that lingers in the whisper of the wind, the rustle of rye, and the old wives’ tales that villagers still swear by. The answer, delivered with a grin and a mug of mead, is that folklore persists because it mirrors our deepest fears, desires, and the stubborn hope that even the most beleaguered soul can find a way to outwit fate.

Pilipiuk’s style is as distinctive as his protagonist. He writes in a breezy, colloquial Polish that bursts with idiomatic flavor, rapid-fire dialogue, and a rhythm that feels like a folk song sung in a tavern at midnight. Translated into English, the narrative retains its punchy cadence, peppered with vivid metaphors and sudden shifts from slapstick to melancholy. The author deftly layers satire atop the fantastical—mocking bureaucratic pomposity, romanticizing rural simplicity, and celebrating the absurdity of human nature—all while keeping the plot hurtling forward like a carriage careening down a cobblestone lane. Readers will find themselves laughing at Jakub’s latest blunder one moment and, the next, pausing to ponder the quiet wisdom hidden beneath the jest.

The novel’s strength lies in its ability to make the mythic feel immediate and the mundane feel magical. By anchoring grand, otherworldly conflicts in the intimate concerns of a single village—love, honor, the next meal—Pilipiuk creates a microcosm where the supernatural is not a distant spectacle but a part of daily life, as familiar as the smell of baking bread and as unpredictable as a sudden storm. Weźmisz czarno kure… is therefore more than a comic romp; it is a celebration of storytelling itself, reminding us that the oldest tales continue to thrive because they speak to the timeless human impulse to laugh, to wonder, and to survive—preferably with a good drink in hand and a rogue’s grin on our face.
