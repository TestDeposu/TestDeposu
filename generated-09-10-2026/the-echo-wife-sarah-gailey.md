# The Echo Wife

**Author:** Sarah Gailey  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Sarah Gailey’s 2021 novel, The Echo Wife, strips away the polite veneer of modern marriage and reconstructs it with the cold, precise instruments of science fiction. At its core, this novel is a razor-sharp exploration of identity, ownership, and the insidious ways we project our insecurities onto the people we love—and the versions of ourselves we leave behind. 

The premise is as deliciously macabre as it is conceptually brilliant: Evelyn Caldwell, a brilliant and fiercely uncompromising geneticist, discovers that her unfaithful husband, Nathan, has been sleeping with Martine—a genetically engineered clone of Evelyn herself. Martine was grown from Evelyn's own groundbreaking research, designed to be softer, more compliant, and altogether more palatable than the fiercely independent original. When Nathan winds up dead, Evelyn and her clone are forced into a reluctant, tense alliance to clean up the mess. 

Gailey uses this provocative sci-fi setup to dive deep into a profound thematic exploration of self-hatred and internalized misogyny. Martine is not merely a mistress; she is a physical manifestation of Nathan’s desire to domesticate Evelyn’s brilliance. Through the dynamic between the two women, Gailey asks a haunting question: How do you hate a part of yourself when it has been torn from your DNA and given a beating heart? The interplay between Evelyn and Martine forms the emotional anchor of the book. Evelyn, hardened by ambition and rejection, views her clone with a toxic mix of revulsion and protective instinct, while Martine struggles to find autonomy when her very genesis was programmed to serve another. 

Gailey’s writing style is punchy, clinical, and unapologetically dark. The prose moves with the speed and precision of a scalpel, perfectly reflecting Evelyn’s analytical mind. There is a delicious acidity to the narrative voice, blending the high-stakes tension of a psychological domestic thriller with the speculative dread of bioethics gone wrong. Gailey avoids getting bogged down in dense scientific exposition, choosing instead to focus on the psychological fallout of technological hubris. The domestic sphere becomes a laboratory where power dynamics are tested, mutated, and ultimately dismantled. 

While the plot delivers plenty of genre thrills, twists, and bloody cover-ups, it is the uncompromising character study that elevates The Echo Wife above standard pulp thriller fare. Gailey refuses to offer easy redemptions or comforting resolutions. Instead, she leaves the reader to grapple with messy, complicated women who refuse to be neatly categorized as victims or villains. 

Ultimately, The Echo Wife is a striking, fast-paced triumph that proves speculative fiction is at its best when it uses the impossible to expose uncomfortable human truths. Gailey has crafted a brilliant cautionary tale about the dangers of trying to clone the uncontainable.
