# Return to Mars (The Grand Tour, #7; Mars, #2)

**Author:** Ben Bova  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

Return to Mars picks up where Ben Bova’s earlier Martian saga left off, thrusting veteran astronaut Jamie Waterman back onto the rust‑colored sands of the Red Planet with a mission that is as much about economics as it is about exploration. Bova’s premise is simple yet potent: can the pursuit of pure science coexist with the lure of profit, and what happens when personal ambition collides with the collective responsibility to preserve an alien world? The novel’s central tension—Waterman’s steadfast commitment to scientific integrity versus Dex Trumball’s vision of Mars as a high‑end tourist playground—serves as a microcosm of the larger ethical debates that have haunted space‑faring narratives since the dawn of the Space Age.

From the opening pages, Bova’s prose displays the hallmarks that earned him a reputation as a hard‑science storyteller who never sacrifices narrative drive for technical fidelity. He explains orbital mechanics, life‑support systems, and the geology of Valles Marineris with a clarity that feels like a lecture from a passionate professor, yet he embeds these details seamlessly into the action so that the reader never feels bogged down by exposition. The result is a reading experience where the wonder of discovery is palpable: when Waterman’s team uncovers what appears to be a primitive cliff dwelling, the description is both scientifically grounded and emotionally resonant, evoking the same awe that first gripped humanity when we laid eyes on the Moon’s cratered surface.

Characterization, while not the novel’s strongest suit, serves its purpose within the thematic framework. Jamie Waterman emerges as the archetype of the principled explorer—his internal monologue reveals a man haunted by the memory of his first glimpse of possible alien architecture, now driven by a need to validate that vision without compromising Mars’ pristine environment. Dex Trumball, on the other hand, is less a villain than a embodiment of contemporary entrepreneurial zeal; his charm and confidence make his arguments for commercial development surprisingly persuasive, which heightens the moral ambiguity at the story’s core. Their clashes are rendered with crisp dialogue that crackles with both professional rivalry and personal jealousy, allowing Bova to explore how ego and ideology can distort even the noblest of scientific endeavors.

The plot moves forward through a series of setbacks—equipment sabotage, mistrust among crew members, and the ever‑present threat of Martian dust storms—that keep the pacing tight. Bova’s skill lies in using these obstacles not merely as plot devices but as opportunities to deepen the thematic inquiry. Each malfunction forces Waterman to choose between expediency and rigor, and each decision reverberates through the crew’s morale, illustrating how the ethics of space exploration are lived in real‑time, not just debated in conference rooms.

What makes Return to Mars particularly striking is Bova’s ability to extrapolate current trends into a plausible near‑future. The debate over privatizing celestial bodies mirrors today’s conversations about lunar mining, space tourism, and the regulatory frameworks governing extraterrestrial commerce. By anchoring his speculative fiction in genuine scientific concerns—such as planetary protection protocols and the potential for indigenous microbial life—Bova invites readers to consider whether humanity’s drive to profit from space might ultimately undermine the very knowledge we seek to gain.

Stylistically, the novel leans heavily on clear, declarative sentences and a third‑person limited perspective that stays close to Waterman’s thoughts and perceptions. This narrative choice intensifies the sense of isolation and responsibility that comes with commanding a mission millions of miles from Earth. Bova’s descriptions of the Martian landscape are spare yet vivid: the ochre horizons, the whisper‑thin atmosphere, the way light fractures through the thin air—all rendered with an economy of language that makes the setting feel both alien and intimately familiar.

In sum, Return to Mars is a compact yet thought‑provoking entry in Bova’s Grand Tour series that balances hard‑science rigor with a compelling ethical drama. It asks readers to weigh the promise of scientific enlightenment against the temptations of commercial exploitation, all while delivering a suspenseful, well‑paced adventure that feels both timely and timeless. For fans of scientifically grounded science fiction and anyone fascinated by the future of humanity’s foothold on other worlds, this novel offers a rewarding journey—one that lingers long after the final page is turned.
