# Herr aller Dinge

**Author:** Andreas Eschbach  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Andreas Eschbach has long been a master architect of speculative fiction, renowned for his ability to ground dizzying futuristic concepts in deeply human emotional landscapes. With his 2011 novel, Herr aller Dinge, translated into English as Lord of All Things, Eschbach tackles one of humanity’s oldest and most elusive dreams: the complete eradication of social inequality. 

At its core, the novel asks a deceptively simple, earth-shattering question: What if every human being on the planet were equally wealthy? 

The narrative unfolds through the lives of two childhood friends from vastly different worlds. Charlotte, the privileged daughter of a French diplomat in Tokyo, and Hiroshi, the son of a humble local washerwoman, cross paths as children. Despite their parents fierce disapproval and the seemingly impassable chasm between the rich and the poor, a profound bond forms. When adults insist that social divides are unbridgeable, young Hiroshi does not accept defeat. Instead, it plants the seed of a brilliantly simple, obsessive life plan. As their paths diverge, cross again, and separate once more, Hiroshi pursues a singular, monumental goal: to dismantle the global disparity between wealth and poverty. His ultimate motivation is as old as literature itself—he believes it is the only way to truly win Charlotte’s love.

What makes Herr aller Dinge a standout in contemporary science fiction is Eschbach’s trademark style. Rather than relying on fast-paced, laser-shooting space opera tropes, Eschbach opts for a methodical, deeply thoughtful pacing. He builds his world-building brick by meticulous brick, allowing the sociological and technological implications of Hiroshi’s plan to unfold with terrifying plausibility. The author does not just present a utopian dream; he interrogates the surreal consequences of a society radically re-engineered from the ground up. What happens to ambition, motivation, and human relationships when material scarcity is entirely banished? Eschbach explores these philosophical dilemmas with a cool, journalistic clarity that contrasts sharply with the sweeping, romantic desperation at the heart of Hiroshi's quest.

The novel thrives on the tension between the macro and the micro. On one hand, it is a grand economic thought experiment, exploring nanotech-driven abundance and the total restructuring of global capitalism. On the other hand, it remains an intimate, poignant coming-of-age story about unrequited longing, obsession, and the heavy price of playing god with civilization. Charlotte and Hiroshi serve as compelling proxies for humanity’s eternal struggle: the yearning to connect across boundaries versus the structures that eternally push us apart.

Herr aller Dinge is not merely a book you read; it is a conceptual maze that lingers in your mind long after the final page. Eschbach challenges his readers to look at the world outside the window, questioning the very foundations of the society we take for granted. It is a stunning, intellectually stimulating triumph of German sci-fi—proof that the most profound futuristic adventures are those that examine the timeless human heart.
