# Divine Misfortune

**Author:** A. Lee Martinez  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

In Divine Misfortune, acclaimed urban fantasy author A. Lee Martinez does what he does best: he takes a wildly absurd premise and grounds it in the painfully relatable mundanity of modern adult life. Published in 2010, this off-the-wall comedic novel tackles a deeply human theme—the desperation for upward mobility and financial security—by filtering it through a satirical lens of consumerism, divine bureaucracy, and supernatural roommates.

The narrative kicks off when a perpetually overlooked working couple, Teri and Phil, decide they have waited long enough for the corporate ladder to yield results. Frustrated by stalled careers and stagnant bank accounts, they do what any modern couple would do: they shop for a deity online. Martinez uses this brilliant hook to skewer the gig economy and the ridiculous lengths to which people will go for a slice of the American dream. Choosing a god turns out to be a bureaucratic nightmare of fine print, hefty spiritual sacrifices, and bizarre commandments. Blood, chastity, and grand rituals are par for the course until they stumble upon Luka, a couch-surfing, trash-talking raccoon god of prosperity whose only demand is a modest cut of their newfound luck and a place to sleep.

At its core, the central theme of Divine Misfortune is the commodification of faith and the transactional nature of modern worship. Martinez explores how easily sacred relationships can be reduced to corporate partnerships. Luka is not a distant, terrifying entity demanding absolute obedience; he is essentially a supernatural roommate who watches television, eats snacks, and expects his portfolio to grow. By demoting divinity to the level of a startup business partner, Martinez cleverly critiques how humanity trivializes higher powers for personal gain, reducing divine intervention to a mere shortcut for a corner office.

Martinez’s writing style is the driving force of the book. Known for his signature blend of snappy dialogue, fast pacing, and chaotic world-building, he avoids the heavy, brooding angst typically found in urban fantasy. Instead, he leans heavily into screwball comedy and situational irony. The introduction of secondary divine figures—such as a thoroughly heartbroken love goddess and an ancient, vengeful deity with a severe grudge—escalates the suburban chaos into a full-blown cosmic farce. The humor stems from the stark contrast between epic mythologies and the dull, carpeted reality of a middle-class living room. 

While the plot moves at a brisk, entertaining clip, Martinez ensures that the character dynamics remain grounded. Teri and Phil are not epic heroes; they are exhausted, everyday people trying to navigate a world that feels permanently rigged against them. Their marriage is tested not just by the sudden influx of divine intervention, but by the creeping realization that success comes with unforeseen spiritual and emotional tax brackets.

Divine Misfortune is a sharp, witty, and deeply entertaining critique of ambition wrapped in a supernatural sitcom. A. Lee Martinez proves that you do not need grand, sweeping epics to tell a compelling story about power and belief; sometimes, all it takes is a mischievous raccoon god crashing on your pull-out sofa. For readers seeking a clever escape from the grimdark conventions of contemporary fantasy, this book is an absolute must-read.
