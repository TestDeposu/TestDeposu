# Flight

**Author:** Sherman Alexie  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Flight by Sherman Alexie is a daring, compact odyssey that thrusts a troubled fifteen‑year‑old half‑Native, half‑Irish boy named Zits into the violent heart of America’s past. After escaping yet another foster home, Zits falls under the sway of Justice, a magnetic stranger who convinces him that a gun can answer the ache of abandonment. The pull of the trigger, however, launches Zits into a surreal time‑travel experience: he inhabits an FBI agent during the civil rights movement, a mute Lakota boy at Little Bighorn, a nineteenth‑century Indian tracker, and finally a modern‑day airline pilot. Each leap forces him to confront the same raw emotions—rage, fear, helplessness—from wildly different perspectives, blurring the lines between hero, villain, and victim.

Alexie’s signature style shines through the novel’s brisk, lyrical prose. He balances gritty realism with flashes of magical realism, allowing the supernatural jumps to feel less like gimmicks and more like extensions of Zits’s fractured psyche. The humor that characterizes his earlier work surfaces in sardonic observations and bitterly funny dialogue, offering brief respites amid the unrelenting tension. Yet beneath the wit lies a piercing empathy; Alexie does not shy away from the brutality of history nor the quiet desperation of a foster‑care kid searching for belonging. Instead, he uses the temporal shifts as a mirror, showing how personal trauma echoes across collective violence and how understanding can germinate only when we inhabit another’s skin.

The novel’s central theme is the quest for identity amid inherited pain. Zits’s mixed heritage leaves him perpetually “half‑in, half‑out” of both Native and white worlds, a state that fuels his alienation and his impulse to lash out. By forcing him to live multiple lives, Alexie suggests that empathy is not a passive feeling but an active, sometimes violent, reclamation of self. The climax—Zits returning to his own body with a nascent comprehension of what it means to be simultaneously oppressor and oppressed—offers a hopeful, if tentative, resolution: healing begins when we acknowledge the full spectrum of our humanity.

Flight is a compact yet expansive read, ideal for readers who appreciate Alexie’s unflinching look at contemporary Native life, enjoy a dash of speculative twist, and seek stories that challenge the simplistic binaries of good and evil. Its brevity belies the depth of its interrogation of history, trauma, and the redemptive power of seeing the world through another’s eyes.
