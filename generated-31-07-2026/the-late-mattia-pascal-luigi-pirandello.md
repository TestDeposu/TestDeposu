# The Late Mattia Pascal

**Author:** Luigi Pirandello  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

What is identity, if not a costume we wear until it suffocates us? Luigi Pirandello’s 1904 masterpiece, *The Late Mattia Pascal*—expertly rendered in this spirited edition—grapples with this disquieting question through a narrative that is as philosophically profound as it is riotously entertaining. Long before existentialism became a literary fashion, Pirandello was charting the labyrinth of the self, asking a fundamental question: if you are given the chance to shed your miserable life and start anew, who will you become?

The novel introduces us to Mattia Pascal, a man crushed by the twin failures of a disastrous marriage and a ruined estate. When a fateful trip to the casino at Monte Carlo yields a massive fortune, fate intervenes even more dramatically back home. Upon reading in the newspaper that his own corpse has been fished out of a local millrace—a terribly disfigured body mistakenly identified as him—Mattia seizes the ultimate loophole. He does not correct the record. Instead, he steps out of the grave as a free man, adopting the alias Adriano Meis, ready to write a blank check for his destiny.

Yet, Pirandello is not interested in writing a simple escapist fantasy. The core theme of *The Late Mattia Pascal* is the impossibility of true reinvention. Mattia soon discovers that to live in modern society, one needs more than just money; one needs civil registry, a history, and paper proof of existence. By severing his legal ties to the world, Adriano Meis becomes a ghost walking among the living. He cannot rent a proper apartment, he cannot marry the woman he loves, and he cannot even seek justice when he is robbed, because a non-person has no recourse before the law. Pirandello brilliantly illustrates that total freedom is, in reality, total exile.

What elevates this existential tragedy into a timeless classic is Pirandello’s inimitable style. The author possesses a rare genius for wedding profound philosophical inquiry with biting, self-aware wit. Mattia narrates his own absurd odyssey with a blend of melancholic irony and comic verve. He is a man watching his own life as if it were a play, acutely aware of the masks we all wear. This theatricality permeates the prose, turning every interaction into a dance of illusions. Pirandello mocks our rigid social conventions, exposing the hypocrisy and fragility of the roles we assign ourselves and others.

As Mattia eventually realizes the cruel joke of his pseudo-suicide—returning home only to find his wife remarried and his place in the world irrevocably filled—the novel shifts from farce to a poignant meditation on mortality and belonging. He must accept the fate of being a living ghost, visiting his own grave and coming to terms with the man he once was.

*The Late Mattia Pascal* is a dazzling, fast-paced psychological adventure that reads as fresh today as it did over a century ago. Pirandello holds up a cracked mirror to human nature, forcing us to laugh at our own desperate attempts to define who we are. It is a brilliant, striking reminder that while we may long to escape our burdens, we are ultimately tethered to the reality of our own existence.
