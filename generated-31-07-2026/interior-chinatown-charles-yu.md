# Interior Chinatown

**Author:** Charles Yu  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

To be a person of color in America is often to feel like an extra in someone else’s movie. Charles Yu takes this poignant sociological reality and turns it into a brilliant, high-concept theatrical nightmare in his National Book Award-winning 2020 novel, Interior Chinatown. Written entirely in the format of a screenplay, the book is a devastatingly funny and deeply melancholic send-up of Hollywood stereotyping, media representation, and the immigrant experience.

The story follows Willis Wu, a man whose life is literally bound by the script of Black and White, a long-running police procedural filmed endlessly at the Golden Palace restaurant in the heart of Chinatown. Willis doesn't harbor delusions of grandeur; he knows his place. Today, he might be Background Oriental Making a Weird Face. Tomorrow, if he plays his cards right, he could graduate to Delivery Guy or even Disgraced Son. But his ultimate aspiration—the absolute ceiling for anyone who looks like him in this universe—is to become Kung Fu Guy. 

Yu’s stylistic choice to frame the narrative as a shooting script is nothing short of genius. By utilizing stage directions, dialogue cues, and camera angles, he visually and structurally reinforces his central theme: the relentless pigeonholing of Asian Americans into flattened, homogenized tropes. The characters do not just inhabit a world shaped by media; their very identities are dictated by it. The Golden Palace is both a literal television set and a metaphor for the invisible walls of the model minority myth, a gilded cage where the menu never changes and the roles are rigidly assigned by decades of racist television history.

Yet, beneath the dazzling inventiveness and the biting, postmodern satire lies a profound and aching vulnerability. When Willis accidentally stumbles off his mark and into a speaking role, the narrative cracks open. What begins as a surreal comedy about television production slowly evolves into an intimate family tragedy. Yu pivots the lens toward the immigrant journey—the quiet sacrifices, the generational trauma, and the unspoken grief of parents who traded their true identities for the promise of assimilation, only to find themselves relegated to the background of a society that refuses to truly see them. 

Through the legal drama unfolding in the background of the television set, the novel expands its scope to interrogate the historical weaponization of the judicial and social systems against Asian communities. Yu juxtaposes the absurdity of network television with the stark, unscripted pain of real life, forcing the reader to confront the scripts we are all handed at birth—and the heavy cost of trying to rewrite them.

Interior Chinatown is a triumph of form and feeling. It is a book that makes you laugh out loud with its razor-sharp wit, only to quietly break your heart a few pages later. Charles Yu has crafted a daring, wildly original masterpiece that speaks volumes about who gets to be the hero of their own story, and who is forever trapped in the wings. Essential reading for anyone who has ever felt like a prop in their own life.
