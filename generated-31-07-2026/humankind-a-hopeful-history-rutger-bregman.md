# Humankind: A Hopeful History

**Author:** Rutger Bregman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

For centuries, Western thought has been shackled by a bleak, repetitive chorus. From Machiavelli and Thomas Hobbes to Sigmund Freud and modern evolutionary theorists, civilization has operated on a foundational, unquestioned premise: scratch the surface of any human being, and a selfish, savage beast emerges. It is a philosophy that bleeds into our daily news feeds, underpins our legal systems, and dictates our economic models. We are, we are told, fundamentally bad.

In his international bestseller *Humankind: A Hopeful History*, Dutch historian and author Rutger Bregman looks this centuries-old cynicism squarely in the eye and offers a radical, exhilarating counter-narrative. Bregman posits that humanity is not hardwired for selfishness and competition, but rather for kindness, trust, and profound cooperation. Far from being a naive utopian daydream, this perspective, as Bregman skillfully demonstrates, is rooted in hard science, evolutionary biology, and a meticulous re-examination of history.

What sets *Humankind* apart from conventional academic texts is Bregman’s infectious, journalistic verve. He employs his signature wit, refreshing frankness, and gripping storytelling to dismantle some of the darkest myths of modern psychology and sociology. 

Consider the famous Stanford Prison Experiment, a cornerstone of modern education used to prove how easily humans slip into tyranny when given power. Bregman digs into the archives to reveal the hidden flaws, nudges, and manipulations behind the experiment, exposing it as more of a theatrical production than objective science. Similarly, he contrasts the fictional savagery of William Golding’s *Lord of the Flies* with a genuine, little-known historical account of six boys who survived a shipwreck on a remote Tongan island for over a year. Their real-life story was not one of murder and tribal warfare, but of cooperation, music, tending a garden, and maintaining a strict democratic rota. 

Bregman leads readers on a sweeping tour across the past 200,000 years of *Homo sapiens*, reframing how we view our own evolutionary success. He argues that our evolutionary edge was never "survival of the fittest" in the brutal, competitive sense, but rather "survival of the friendliest." Those of us who were capable of empathy, communication, and community-building out-survived the rest. 

Even when confronting humanity’s darkest hours—such as the London Blitz during World War II—Bregman finds hidden resilience. Instead of descending into panic and chaos under the bombs, Londoners forged deeper solidarity, proving that crises tend to bring out our innate altruism rather than destroy it. He moves seamlessly from historical events to contemporary applications, touching on everything from how we design our cities to how we manage our workplaces and prisons.

The implications of Bregman’s thesis are nothing short of revolutionary. He argues that the stories we tell about ourselves become self-fulfilling prophecies. When our institutions, politicians, and economic systems assume the worst of people, they inevitably bring out the worst in us. Conversely, if we dare to build a society rooted in the realistic belief in human generosity and collaboration, we lay the bedrock for genuine, systemic change.

Hailed as the *Sapiens* of 2020, *Humankind* is a much-needed antidote to cultural despair. It is a striking, deeply persuasive book that challenges us to unlearn our cynicism. Bregman does not offer a blind, Pollyannaish optimism; instead, he presents a rigorous, hopeful realism. By the time you turn the final page, you will not only view human history through a fresh, illuminating lens, but you will also look at your fellow human beings with renewed respect and radical hope.
