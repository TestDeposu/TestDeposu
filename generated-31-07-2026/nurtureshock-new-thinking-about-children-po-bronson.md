# NurtureShock: New Thinking About Children

**Author:** Po Bronson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

For decades, modern parenting has operated on a comfortable autopilot of well-intentioned assumptions. We praise our children's intelligence to boost their self-esteem, enforce strict anti-lying rules through moral lectures, and assume that diverse schools naturally breed integrated friendships. But what if almost everything we think we know about raising children is not just wrong, but actively backfiring? 

This is the provocative premise at the heart of NurtureShock: New Thinking About Children, the 2009 landmark collaboration by award-winning science journalists Po Bronson and Ashley Merryman. Eschewing the touchy-feely advice of traditional parenting manuals, Bronson and Merryman plunge headfirst into the cold, hard data of developmental psychology, neuroscience, and sociological research. The result is a gripping, paradigm-shifting critique that strips away parental sentimentality and replaces it with empirical truth.

The central theme of NurtureShock is simple yet revolutionary: good intentions do not equate to good parenting strategies. Modern parents are more involved, caring, and attentive than any previous generation, yet society continues to grapple with rising aggression, academic anxiety, and social alienation among youth. The authors argue that we have failed to look closely at the science of childhood development, relying instead on folklore and cultural myths. 

Throughout the book, Bronson and Merryman dismantle cherished dogmas one by one with surgical precision. Take, for instance, the ubiquitous practice of praising a child for being smart. Conventional wisdom dictates that telling a child they are a natural genius builds confidence. However, as the authors reveal through brilliant storytelling and analysis, this practice actually creates a fragile mindset. Children praised for innate intelligence become terrified of failure, shying away from challenges because a single mistake threatens their identity as the smart kid. Instead, the science shows we must praise the process—effort, strategy, and perseverance—fostering a growth mindset that embraces struggle.

Similarly, the book tackles thorny societal paradoxes that make readers squirm with recognition. Why do 98 percent of children agree that lying is morally wrong, yet 98 percent of them routinely lie to their parents? Bronson and Merryman investigate the mechanics of deception and discover that harsh crackdowns on lying do not deter children; rather, they simply teach children to become better, more covert liars. In another chapter, they explore why interracial friendships actually decrease in many integrated schools—a counterintuitive finding that challenges the naive assumption that proximity alone cures racial bias. 

What elevates NurtureShock from a dry academic literature review into a riveting page-turner is the authors' distinct literary style. Bronson and Merryman write with the pacing and narrative tension of investigative journalists. They do not present science as a collection of dusty abstracts; they frame each chapter as a mystery. We meet the researchers, walk through the labs, and witness the surprising behavioral experiments firsthand. The prose is razor-sharp, accessible, and utterly devoid of condescension. They respect the reader's intelligence, inviting us to look in the mirror and confront our own flawed parenting instincts.

Admittedly, NurtureShock can feel unsettling. It refuses to offer a comforting roadmap or a set of easy, step-by-step rules to guarantee a Harvard acceptance letter or a harmonious bedtime routine. Instead, it offers something infinitely more valuable: clarity. By exposing the disconnect between our methods and children's actual neurological and psychological wiring, Bronson and Merryman empower us to parent smarter, not harder.

In a crowded marketplace of parenting fads, NurtureShock remains an essential, enduring masterpiece. It is a wake-up call that demands to be read not just by parents, but by educators, policymakers, and anyone invested in the future of human development. Read it to unlearn the myths, challenge your assumptions, and finally understand what is actually happening inside the minds of the next generation.
