# The Great Believers

**Author:** Rebecca Makkai  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

"The Great Believers" by Rebecca Makkai is a heart-wrenching and masterfully crafted novel that delves into the complexities of friendship, love, and redemption in the face of tragedy and loss. Set against the backdrop of the devastating AIDS epidemic in 1980s Chicago, Makkai weaves a narrative that is both historically informed and deeply personal, exploring the ways in which this catastrophic event shaped the lives of those who lived through it.

The novel follows two interconnected storylines: the first, which takes place in the 1980s, follows Yale Tishman, a charismatic and ambitious development director, as he navigates the highs and lows of his career, all while struggling to come to terms with the growing AIDS crisis around him. One by one, his friends are dying, and Yale finds himself increasingly isolated, until he is left with only Fiona, Nico's little sister, as a reminder of the lives he lost.

Thirty years later, Fiona, now a grown woman, is in Paris, embarking on a quest to track down her estranged daughter, who has disappeared into a cult. As she searches for answers, Makkai masterfully interweaves Fiona's story with Yale's, demonstrating the long-lasting impact of the AIDS epidemic on both individuals and communities. Through these two narrative threads, Makkai sheds light on the darker aspects of human nature, revealing the ways in which fear, prejudice, and stigma can lead to devastating consequences.

Makkai's writing is a testament to her skill as a storyteller. With precision and compassion, she crafts a narrative that is both a tribute to the lives lost during the AIDS epidemic and a testament to the power of human resilience. Her prose is lyrical, evocative, and at times, heart-wrenchingly beautiful, conjuring the vibrant spirit of 1980s Chicago and the city's thriving arts scene. Yet, it is in the quiet moments, when the narrative slows and the reader is left to reflect on the devastating consequences of the epidemic, that Makkai's true genius is revealed.

One of the most striking aspects of "The Great Believers" is its nuanced exploration of the complicated relationships between friends and lovers. Makkai's portrayal of Yale's relationships with his partner, Charlie, and later, Nico, is tender and nuanced, revealing the deep emotional connections that bind us together. And yet, it is in the face of tragedy that these relationships are tested, and the fragility of human connection is laid bare.

Throughout the novel, Makkai's use of language is a masterclass in subtlety. She eschews melodrama and sentimentalism, opting instead for a measured, introspective tone that captures the quiet despair of those living through the AIDS epidemic. And yet, it is in this quietness that the true horror of the epidemic is conveyed: the slow, incremental loss of life; the erosion of hope; and the devastating impact on loved ones left behind.

Ultimately, "The Great Believers" is a novel about the power of human connection in the face of adversity. Despite the chaos and destruction of the AIDS epidemic, Makkai's characters find ways to hold on to hope, to love, and to each other. As Yale so eloquently puts it, "There's a beauty in the way we live and love, a beauty that's worth fighting for."

In "The Great Believers", Rebecca Makkai has given us a novel that is at once a tribute to those lost during the AIDS epidemic and a testament to the enduring power of human connection. This is a book that will leave you breathless, moved, and haunted by its characters and their stories.
