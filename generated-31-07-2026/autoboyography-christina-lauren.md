# Autoboyography

**Author:** Christina Lauren  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

Autoboyography by Christina Lauren is a tender, sharply observed coming‑of‑age story that folds the quiet urgency of first love into the rigid expectations of faith and family. Tanner Scott, a bisexual teen who has retreated into the closet after moving from California to Utah, signs up for the elite Seminar writing course on a dare—only to find that the four‑month deadline becomes a crucible for self‑discovery. His mentor, Sebastian Brother, a Mormon prodigy whose own Seminar novel sold the previous year, is everything Tanner thought he wanted to avoid: disciplined, devout, and utterly magnetic. What begins as a reluctant flirtation over shared drafts quickly deepens into a love that forces both boys to confront the parts of themselves they’ve been taught to hide.

The novel’s greatest strength lies in its balance of humor and heartache. Christina Lauren—known for their breezy, dialogue‑driven romance—brings the same snappy wit to Autoboyography, letting Tanner’s sarcastic internal monologue offset the heavier moments of religious guilt and familial pressure. Yet beneath the jokes is a sincere exploration of how storytelling itself becomes a refuge: the act of writing a book mirrors the characters’ attempts to author their own identities. The Seminar’s looming deadline isn’t just a plot device; it mirrors the ticking clock of adolescence, where every decision feels both monumental and fleeting.

Stylistically, the authors employ a fluid, present‑tense narrative that pulls readers directly into Tanner’s sensory world— the smell of ink on paper, the chill of a Utah winter hallway, the flutter of a first kiss behind the library stacks. Their prose is lean but lyrical, allowing quiet scenes (a shared glance over a manuscript, a hesitant hand brushing against a notebook) to resonate as loudly as any grand confession. The alternating focus on Tanner’s external bravado and Sebastian’s internal devotion creates a layered portrait of two young men negotiating love amid divergent worlds, without reducing either to a stereotype.

What sets Autoboyography apart from its YA peers is its refusal to offer easy answers. Tanner’s bisexuality is presented not as a phase to be “fixed” but as an integral part of his self‑understanding, while Sebastian’s Mormon upbringing is portrayed with respect and nuance—his faith is neither vilified nor romanticized, but examined as a living, shaping force. The novel thus becomes a meditation on how belief, desire, and creativity intersect, suggesting that authenticity can be forged even when the surrounding culture demands conformity.

In a genre often saturated with tropes, Autoboyography feels fresh because it trusts its characters to be messy, contradictory, and ultimately hopeful. Christina Lauren’s signature blend of humor, honesty, and emotional precision makes this novel not just a love story, but a quiet manifesto for anyone who has ever felt caught between who they are and who they’re expected to be.
