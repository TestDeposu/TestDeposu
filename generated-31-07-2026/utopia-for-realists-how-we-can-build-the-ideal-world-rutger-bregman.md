# Utopia for Realists: How We Can Build the Ideal World

**Author:** Rutger Bregman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

Title: The Audacity of Hope: Why Rutger Bregman’s Utopian Vision is the Reality We Desperately Need

What if everything you thought you knew about the limits of society was a collective illusion? What if the 40-hour workweek, extreme wealth inequality, and locked borders are not immutable laws of nature, but merely outdated design choices? In Utopia for Realists: How We Can Build the Ideal World, Dutch historian Rutger Bregman invites readers on a breathtaking intellectual journey that shatters the cynical boundaries of modern political discourse and dares us to dream bigger. 

Bregman, celebrated by critics as a more politically radical Malcolm Gladwell, has crafted a manifesto that arrives not a moment too soon. In an era defined by political polarization, economic anxiety, and pervasive pessimism, this book acts as an antidote to despair. Bregman’s core thesis is deceptively simple yet earth-shattering: what we now dismiss as "utopian" is often just history waiting to happen. Every major civilizational leap—from the abolition of slavery to universal suffrage and the establishment of democracy—was once branded as an impossible fantasy by the realists of its day. Bregman challenges us to reclaim our radical imagination and realize that being unreasonable is the only way to build a better world.

At the heart of the book are three bold policy proposals that initially sound too good to be true: a universal basic income (UBI), a 15-hour workweek, and a world of open borders. Skeptics will scoff, pointing to economic models or political feasibility. But Bregman does not deal in idle armchair philosophy; he deals in historical evidence and empirical data. 

Through compelling storytelling and meticulous research, he transports the reader back to Dauphin, a small Canadian town in the 1970s that quietly and successfully eradicated poverty using a guaranteed annual income. He resurrects the forgotten history of Richard Nixon’s administration, which came tantalizingly close to passing a basic income bill for millions of Americans. By grounding these radical ideas in historical reality, Bregman transforms them from abstract dreams into actionable blueprints. 

Stylistically, Bregman is a master of narrative non-fiction. He writes with a rare blend of scholarly rigor and journalistic flair. His prose is punchy, accessible, and relentlessly optimistic, stripped of dry economic jargon yet rich with intellectual depth. He has a unique knack for taking complex sociological data and weaving it into a fast-paced, page-turning narrative. He doesn't lecture the reader from a pedestal; instead, he sits beside us, looks at the absurdities of modern consumerist life—where we work jobs we dislike to buy things we do not need—and gently asks, "Isn't there a better way?"

Critics of utopian thinking often argue that such ideas ignore human nature or economic constraints. Bregman anticipates these objections and dismantles them with sharp wit. He argues that our current economic system is failing not because we lack resources—we are wealthier, healthier, and better educated than any generation in human history—but because we lack imagination. We have become prisoners of pragmatism, settling for tinkering at the edges of a broken system rather than reimagining the foundation.

Utopia for Realists is not just a book; it is a cultural provocation. It forces us to confront our deepest biases about poverty, labor, and human potential. It reminds us that progress is not a gift from above, but the result of daring to believe in a world that does not yet exist. 

If you are exhausted by the daily cycle of political cynicism, if you suspect that human beings are capable of achieving far more than endless rat races and climate anxiety, this book is your mandatory reading. Bregman proves that the only thing standing between us and the ideal world is our own lack of audacity.
