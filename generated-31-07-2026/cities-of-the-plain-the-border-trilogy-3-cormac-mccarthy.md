# Cities of the Plain (The Border Trilogy, #3)

**Author:** Cormac McCarthy  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1998

In "Cities of the Plain," Cormac McCarthy concludes The Border Trilogy with a narrative that is both a poignant elegy for a bygone era and a powerful exploration of the human condition. Set in 1952, the story follows John Grady Cole and Billy Parham, two cowboys bound together by their experiences and their connection to the land, as they navigate the changing landscape of the American West.

McCarthy's prose is, as always, breathtaking in its beauty and precision. His writing is a masterclass in subtlety, conveying the complexity of human emotions and the harsh realities of life through sparse, economical language. The characters of John Grady and Billy are drawn with remarkable depth and nuance, their struggles and triumphs rendered with a quiet intensity that is both deeply moving and profoundly insightful.

At its core, "Cities of the Plain" is a novel about the fragility of human existence and the impermanence of all things. McCarthy's characters are confronted with the inevitability of loss and the inexorable passage of time, and their responses to these challenges are both heartbreaking and deeply human. The novel is also a powerful exploration of the American West, a land of stunning beauty and unforgiving harshness, where the boundaries between reality and myth are constantly blurred.

One of the most striking aspects of McCarthy's writing is his ability to evoke the sense of a particular time and place. The world of "Cities of the Plain" is a richly textured one, full of vivid details and telling observations that bring the characters and their surroundings to life. From the dusty, sun-baked landscapes of New Mexico to the vibrant, chaotic streets of Ciudad Juárez, McCarthy's descriptions are at once precise and evocative, conjuring up a world that is both familiar and strange.

The novel's central plot, which revolves around John Grady's doomed love affair with a Mexican prostitute, is both a powerful exploration of the human heart and a searing critique of the societal norms that govern our lives. McCarthy's portrayal of the characters' emotions is unsparing and unflinching, yet it is also infused with a deep sense of compassion and understanding. The result is a narrative that is both deeply unsettling and profoundly moving.

Throughout "Cities of the Plain," McCarthy's style is characterized by its simplicity, directness, and lack of sentimentality. His writing is stripped bare of ornamentation, yet it is also possessed of a quiet, unassuming beauty that is both deeply affecting and profoundly memorable. The novel's themes of loss, love, and the search for meaning in a seemingly meaningless world are timeless and universal, and McCarthy's treatment of them is both deeply personal and profoundly resonant.

In short, "Cities of the Plain" is a masterpiece of contemporary American literature, a novel that is both a powerful exploration of the human condition and a deeply moving elegy for a lost world. It is a testament to McCarthy's skill and craftsmanship as a writer that he is able to evoke such a strong sense of time and place, and to create characters that are at once so deeply flawed and so profoundly human.
