# Pack Up the Moon

**Author:** Kristan Higgins  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

**A Love That Outlasts the Stars: Kristan Higgins’ *Pack Up the Moon* and the Quiet Courage of Grief**

Grief is a thief—it steals your breath, your rhythm, even the way you walk through the world. But what if, in its wake, love doesn’t just linger? What if it *rewrites* the rules? Kristan Higgins’ *Pack Up the Moon* is a novel that dares to ask these questions, weaving a story so tender and honest about loss that it feels like a whispered secret between two souls. It’s a book for the grieving, the lonely, and the hopeful—the kind that doesn’t just tell you how to move forward, but *shows* you the messy, beautiful detours along the way.

At its heart, *Pack Up the Moon* is a love letter to Lauren, a woman whose absence leaves her husband, Joshua Park, adrift in a world that suddenly feels too loud, too bright, too *alive* without her. Josh isn’t the kind of man who fits neatly into the world—his quiet intensity, his scientific precision, his social awkwardness—makes him an outsider even in his own grief. But Lauren, ever the pragmatist, leaves him with a final gift: twelve letters, each a step in her plan to help him navigate the first year without her. These aren’t clichéd platitudes; they’re raw, specific, and deeply personal, guiding Josh through the chaos of widowhood with the same humor and heart that defined their marriage.

Higgins’ genius lies in her ability to make grief *funny*. There’s a scene where Josh, in his first post-grief grocery run, accidentally buys a dozen roses—only to realize he has no idea how to arrange them. The absurdity is bittersweet, a reminder that life, even in its darkest moments, refuses to be solemn all the time. But the humor never overshadows the pain; it’s woven in like sunlight through storm clouds, a necessary contrast that keeps the story from feeling like a maudlin elegy. Josh’s journey isn’t linear. There’s anger, denial, moments of numbness, and then—slowly—glimpses of joy that feel like stolen victories. Higgins doesn’t rush him. She lets him stumble, fail, and find his footing in his own time.

The letters Lauren leaves behind are the emotional core of the novel, but they’re also a masterclass in storytelling. Each one is a snapshot of their relationship—her dry wit, her unshakable love for him, her quiet strength. They reveal Lauren as a woman who didn’t just love Josh; she *knew* him, in all his quirks and vulnerabilities. And through her words, Josh begins to see himself again, not as a man broken by loss, but as someone capable of healing, of laughter, of *living*. The letters are a bridge between past and present, a way for Lauren to stay with him even when she’s gone.

What makes *Pack Up the Moon* so compelling is its refusal to romanticize grief. There’s no neat resolution, no sudden epiphany that erases the pain. Instead, Higgins presents grief as a landscape—sometimes barren, sometimes lush with unexpected flowers. Josh’s journey isn’t about "getting over" Lauren; it’s about learning to carry her with him, to let her love shape his future without erasing her past. It’s a theme that resonates deeply in an era where mental health and emotional honesty are finally being talked about, but rarely with such warmth and nuance.

Higgins’ writing is deceptively simple. Her prose is clean, uncluttered, but every word carries weight. She has a knack for capturing small, telling details—the way Josh’s hands tremble when he touches something new, the way his old routines feel like foreign territory. Her characters are deeply human, flawed in the best way, and Josh’s arc is one of quiet courage. He’s not a hero in the traditional sense; he’s a man learning to be present in his own life, to accept help, to let himself be vulnerable. That’s the kind of storytelling that lingers long after the last page.

*Pack Up the Moon* is also a love letter to the idea that love doesn’t just end with death—it *transforms*. Lauren’s love for Josh doesn’t disappear; it evolves, becomes part of the fabric of his new life. And in that, Higgins offers a profound truth: grief isn’t the opposite of joy; it’s just joy in a different form. The book is perfect for book clubs because it sparks so many conversations—about loss, about friendship, about the ways we support each other when life feels overwhelming. It’s uplifting without being saccharine, funny without being flippant, and deeply moving without ever feeling sentimental.

In a world that often demands we move on quickly, *Pack Up the Moon* is a reminder that healing isn’t a race. It’s a dance, a stumble, a moment of quiet triumph. And in the end, that’s what makes it so powerful. It’s a book for anyone who’s ever loved deeply and had to learn how to live without it—and for those who love them, too.
