# The Devil and the Dark Water

**Author:** Stuart Turton  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

The Devil and the Dark Water throws its readers onto a creaking 17th‑century vessel where rationality battles the palpable whisper of the demonic, and Stuart Turton proves once again that he can engineer a puzzle as tight as a ship’s hull while letting the atmosphere seep into every sentence. The novel’s core theme is the tension between empirical investigation and the lure of the inexplicable—a duel that plays out not only in the murder‑mystery plot but also in the characters’ inner lives. Samuel Pipps, famed detective, is imprisoned and thus stripped of his usual agency; his bodyguard Arent Hayes and the sharp‑witted noblewoman Sara Wessel must become the eyes and mind of the investigation. Their quest forces them to confront superstition, guilt, and the ways in which past sins echo across time, making the voyage as much a psychological reckoning as a search for a killer.

Turton’s style is a deliberate homage to the golden age of locked‑room fiction, yet it feels unmistakably contemporary. He layers dense historical detail—ship rigging, Dutch mercantile politics, the specter of plague—without letting the exposition bog down the narrative. Instead, the setting becomes a character in its own right: the creaking timbers, the salt‑laden wind, the eerie symbols on the sail all contribute to a claustrophobic mood that heightens every clue and red herring. The prose swings between crisp, almost reportorial narration during Pipps’s flashbacks and a more lyrical, tense voice when the supernatural threats surface, mirroring the book’s central conflict between reason and myth.

What makes the novel especially compelling is how Turton uses the “impossible miracles” promised by the unseen voice as structural beats. Each miracle—an impossible pursuit, an impossible theft, an impossible murder—acts as a locked‑room set piece that demands both logical deduction and an openness to the uncanny. The reader is invited to piece together clues alongside Arent and Sara, experiencing the thrill of the chase while constantly questioning whether the explanations lie in human malice or something beyond comprehension. This duality sustains suspense until the final reveal, which manages to satisfy both the lover of classic whodunits and the fan of occult horror.

The character dynamics further enrich the theme. Arent’s steadfast loyalty contrasts with Sara’s concealed motives and her own haunted past, creating a partnership that evolves from reluctant alliance to genuine trust. Their interactions are laced with wit and occasional friction, providing moments of levity that prevent the story from becoming overly grim. Pipps, though physically absent for much of the narrative, looms large through his reputation and the moral questions his alleged crime raises—questions about justice, reputation, and the cost of brilliance.

Stuart Turton’s ambition to blend Shirley Jackson’s atmospheric dread with Sherlock Holmes‑ian deduction pays off. He avoids cheap jump scares; instead, the horror emerges from the unknown, the unexplained symbols, and the creeping sense that the ship itself might be complicit. The historical grounding adds weight to the supernatural suggestions, making the eventual resolution feel earned rather than contrived.

If there is a minor critique, it lies in the occasional density of the historical minutiae, which, while impressive, can momentarily slow the pace for readers less invested in 17th‑century maritime life. Yet this very detail is also the novel’s strength, immersing the audience fully in a world where the line between myth and reality is as thin as the ship’s planks.

In sum, *The Devil and the Dark Water* is a masterclass in constructing a tightly plotted mystery that is as intellectually satisfying as it is emotionally resonant. Turton’s style—meticulous, evocative, and deftly balanced between logic and legend—makes the novel a standout entry in the modern locked‑room tradition and a haunting reminder that the greatest monsters are often the stories we tell ourselves to explain the unknown.
