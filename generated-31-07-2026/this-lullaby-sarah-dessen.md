# This Lullaby

**Author:** Sarah Dessen  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2002

Sarah Dessen has long held a revered status as a master architect of young adult contemporary fiction, and her 2002 novel, This Lullaby, stands as a shimmering testament to her enduring appeal. Long before the current renaissance of the YA genre, Dessen was already carving out a distinct literary space by treating teenage emotional landscapes with gravity, nuance, and acute psychological insight. This novel captures the fragile, messy transition from youthful self-protection to the terrifying vulnerability of genuine intimacy, offering a compelling critique of emotional detachment disguised as independence.

At the heart of This Lullaby is Remy Starr, a fiercely pragmatic protagonist who has inherited her mother’s serial-dating cynicism. Remy’s philosophy on romance is governed by a strict, unyielding rulebook designed to guarantee zero collateral damage: never stay too long, never invest too deeply, and absolutely never, under any circumstances, date a musician. To Remy, boys are disposable soundtracks, temporary interludes before the inevitable fade-out. She prides herself on being the heartbreaker rather than the heartbroken, a defensive mechanism forged from witnessing her mother’s string of failed marriages. 

Then comes Dexter. Chaotic, unpolished, persistent, and, worst of all, the lead singer of a struggling band, Dexter is everything Remy’s rulebook warns against. Dessen’s brilliance lies not in the inevitability of their collision, but in the friction it creates. Dexter is not a brooding, tragic savior meant to rescue Remy from her icy exterior; rather, he is a persistent force of nature who simply refuses to be categorized or discarded according to Remy's schedule. 

The central theme of the novel examines the illusion of control. Dessen explores how the walls we build to keep pain out often end up locking us inside our own isolation. Remy believes her rules equate to strength, but Dessen steadily peels back the layers to reveal that true strength is not about avoiding hurt, but about having the courage to risk it. Loving someone, as the novel’s critical acclaim aptly notes, requires a leap of faith with no guarantee of a soft landing. 

Dessen’s signature writing style anchors this emotional journey. Her prose is deceptively breezy, highly conversational, and effortlessly engaging, yet it hums with an undercurrent of sharp emotional intelligence. She excels at pacing, allowing the thawing of Remy’s guarded heart to feel earned rather than rushed. The dialogue snaps with realism, and the supporting cast—from Remy’s deeply flawed, romantic mother to her fiercely loyal group of girlfriends—adds rich texture to the narrative world, grounding the central romance in a realistic web of female friendship and familial complexity.

What makes This Lullaby endure more than two decades after its publication is its refusal to offer cheap resolutions. Dessen understands that growing up is not about finding a magic fix in another person, but about learning how to dismantle your own armor. Remy and Dexter’s romance jumps off the page because it is built on banter, mutual challenge, and gradual understanding rather than instantaneous, effortless perfection. 

Ultimately, This Lullaby is a winning, resonant story about the music we make of our lives when we finally decide to stop living by someone else’s sheet music. It is a poignant reminder that while love songs might be cliché, the terrifying, beautiful risk of actually feeling them is entirely worthwhile.
