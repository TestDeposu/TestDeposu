# Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters

**Author:** Richard P. Rumelt  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

Good Strategy Bad Strategy cuts through the fog that often surrounds corporate planning with a razor‑sharp focus on what actually makes a strategy work. Richard P. Rumelt, a veteran of both academia and consulting, argues that most so‑called strategies are little more than wish lists—vague aspirations, buzzword‑laden missions, and a scattershot of initiatives that lack coherence. The antidote, he proposes, is to identify the “crux”: the single, tractable challenge whose resolution unlocks the greatest forward momentum. By honing in on this addressable problem and marshalling resources around a clear, coherent action plan, leaders can turn ambiguity into advantage.

Rumelt’s narrative style is one of the book’s strongest assets. He eschews dry theory in favor of vivid, story‑driven illustrations—from Elon Musk’s relentless focus on reusable rockets at SpaceX to the U.S. military’s painful reckoning with Vietnam‑era tactics. Each case study feels less like a textbook example and more like a battlefield report, allowing readers to see the mechanics of good strategy in action. The author’s prose is direct, sometimes almost conversational, yet packed with insight; he repeatedly returns to a climbing metaphor that frames strategy as finding the right handhold on a sheer cliff rather than merely shouting for the summit. This metaphor anchors the abstract concept in a tangible, visceral experience, making the lessons stick.

The core thesis—that effective strategy is less about setting lofty goals and more about diagnosing and overcoming a pivotal obstacle—resonates across sectors. Rumelt shows how organizations flounder when they spread effort thinly across numerous “strategic initiatives” without first diagnosing where their real leverage lies. By contrast, when a leader isolates the crux—whether it’s a supply‑chain bottleneck, a technology gap, or a cultural rigidity—and concentrates coherent policy, resources, and execution on that point, progress accelerates. The book’s practical toolkit, including the notion of a “Strategy Foundry™,” offers a repeatable process for senior teams to surface, test, and refine these cruxes, turning strategy from an occasional off‑site exercise into an ongoing discipline.

What separates Good Strategy Bad Strategy from countless other management tomes is its refusal to settle for platitudes. Rumelt demands rigor: a good strategy must contain a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and a set of coherent actions. Anything missing from this triad is labeled “bad strategy,” and he pulls no punches in critiquing ubiquitous fluff—vision statements that say nothing, SWOT analyses that masquerade as insight, and the endless chase of “best practices” without context. His candid assessments can feel uncomfortable, especially for leaders accustomed to the comfort of vague optimism, but that discomfort is precisely where growth begins.

The book’s brevity belies its depth. Each chapter builds logically on the last, moving from the pitfalls of bad strategy to the anatomy of good strategy, then to the challenges of sustaining strategic advantage in dynamic environments. Rumelt’s ability to blend scholarly research with real‑world warfare, entrepreneurship, and corporate turnarounds makes the text both authoritative and accessible. Readers will finish with a clear mental model: strategy is not a motivational poster; it is a focused, problem‑solving activity that requires honesty, discipline, and the courage to act on what truly matters.

In an age where “strategic thinking” is often reduced to buzzword bingo, Good Strategy Bad Strategy offers a much‑needed corrective. It equips leaders with a lens to spot the genuine lever for change and the resolve to pull it. For anyone who has ever sat through a strategy workshop that felt more like a pep rally than a plan, this book is a wake‑up call—and a practical guide—back to the essence of effective leadership.
