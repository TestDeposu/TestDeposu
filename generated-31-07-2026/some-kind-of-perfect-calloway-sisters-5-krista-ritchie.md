# Some Kind of Perfect (Calloway Sisters, #5)

**Author:** Krista Ritchie  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Some Kind of Perfect, the fifth installment in Krista Ritchie’s Calloway Sisters saga, thrusts readers into the glittering, pressure‑cooker world of a reality‑TV show that promises fame, fortune, and the chance to save a faltering family fashion empire. At its core, the novel is less about the glitz of cameras and more about the tug‑of‑war between autonomy and expectation—a theme Ritchie explores with her trademark blend of steamy romance, sharp wit, and surprisingly sincere social commentary.

Rose Calloway, a Princeton graduate and budding designer, arrives at the show convinced she has her life mapped out: a successful career, a supportive (if eccentric) family, and a budding romance with the fiercely ambitious Connor Cobalt. Yet the moment she steps onto the set, the carefully constructed façade begins to crack. The show’s producer, a Hollywood veteran with a reputation for manipulating contestants for ratings, sees Rose’s virginity not as a personal boundary but as a plot device to drive drama. Connor, equally driven and protective, must navigate the treacherous waters of preserving Rose’s agency while simultaneously fighting to keep the show from imploding—a dual mission that forces both characters to confront what they truly value: love, integrity, or the allure of public validation.

Ritchie’s handling of the reality‑TV premise is one of the novel’s strongest assets. Rather than using the setting as a mere backdrop for cheap melodrama, she interrogates the ethics of televised voyeurism, the commodification of intimacy, and the ways in which performers are coerced into performing versions of themselves that satisfy audience appetites. The show’s endless challenges—fashion tasks, timed drills, public confessions—serve as metaphors for the societal pressures young women face to constantly prove their worth, both professionally and sexually. Rose’s internal struggle to say “no” to the producer’s demands without sacrificing her career mirrors a broader cultural conversation about consent and bodily autonomy, giving the romance a depth that elevates it beyond the usual trope of the protective hero rescuing a helpless heroine.

The chemistry between Rose and Connor crackles with the kind of electric tension that Ritchie’s fans have come to expect. Their dialogue is rapid‑fire, laced with sarcasm and genuine affection, allowing readers to feel the push‑pull of their competing desires: Connor’s urge to shield Rose from exploitation versus Rose’s fierce determination to prove she can succeed on her own terms. Ritchie excels at giving each character a distinct voice; Rose’s narrative is thoughtful and measured, while Connor’s chapters brim with confident bravado that gradually reveals a vulnerable core. This dual‑POV structure not only heightens the romantic stakes but also offers a nuanced look at masculinity—Connor’s protectiveness is never portrayed as possessive control; instead, it evolves into a partnership rooted in mutual respect.

The supporting cast—Rose’s sex‑addicted sister, the flamboyant model, the arrogant smart‑aleck, the explosive alcoholic, and the charming yet problematic “cretin”—adds layers of chaos and humor that keep the pacing brisk. Each secondary character embodies a different facet of the show’s manipulative machine, from the sister’s struggle with compulsive sexuality (handled with surprising sensitivity) to the alcoholic’s self‑destructive binges that mirror the industry’s own toxic cycles. Ritchie uses these personalities to underscore the central theme: when individuals are reduced to spectacle, their complexities are flattened, and the line between authentic self‑expression and performance blurs.

Stylistically, Ritchie’s prose is accessible yet vivid. She favours short, punchy sentences that mirror the rapid cuts of a reality‑TV episode, creating a reading experience that feels almost cinematic. Descriptions of the fashion studio, the lavish set, and the bustling Los Angeles backdrop are rich without becoming indulgent, allowing the emotional beats to shine through. Humor is woven throughout, often surfacing in the banter between Rose and Connor or in the absurdity of the show’s challenges, providing levity that balances the heavier moments of introspection and conflict.

That said, the novel is not without its shortcomings. The reliance on certain romance tropes—the jealous ex, the miscommunication that threatens to break the couple, the eventual grand gesture—can feel predictable at times. Some secondary arcs, particularly the sister’s addiction, are introduced with promise but resolve somewhat swiftly, leaving readers wanting a deeper exploration of the psychological nuances. Additionally, the producer’s motivation, while effective as an antagonistic force, occasionally veers into caricature, which slightly undermines the otherwise thoughtful critique of media exploitation.

Nevertheless, Some Kind of Perfect succeeds as a compelling blend of romance, social commentary, and entertainment. Ritchie manages to deliver a steamy, heart‑warming love story while inviting readers to question the costs of fame and the importance of retaining one’s voice in a world that constantly seeks to edit it. The novel’s brisk pacing, engaging characters, and timely themes make it a worthy addition to the Calloway Sisters canon and a satisfying read for anyone who enjoys contemporary romance with a dash of gritty realism.
