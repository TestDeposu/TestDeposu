# Evil Star (Power of Five, #2)

**Author:** Anthony Horowitz  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

When Anthony Horowitz unleashed *Evil Star* upon the young adult literary landscape in 2012, he achieved what few authors manage in the realm of supernatural thrillers: he successfully raised the stakes without stumbling into the dreaded sophomore slump. As the second installment in the critically acclaimed *Power of Five* (or *Gatekeepers*) series, this novel takes the terrifying cosmic mythology established in *Raven's Gate* and violently propels it onto a global stage. Horowitz proves once again that he is a master of suspense, blending classic horror tropes with modern teenage alienation to create a breathless, white-knuckle reading experience.

At the heart of *Evil Star* lies a deeply resonant thematic exploration of isolation, destiny, and the precarious fragility of the modern world. Matthew Freeman thought his nightmare was over after narrowly averting disaster at Raven's Gate, but Horowitz quickly strips away that illusion of safety. The central theme of the novel pivots on connection versus estrangement. Matt is just a fourteen-year-old boy from a troubled background, yet he is inextricably bound to four other children scattered across the globe—children he has never met and whose languages he does not speak. This geographical and cultural disconnection emphasizes the monumental weight resting on their adolescent shoulders. They are outsiders in their own societies, uniquely burdened with the knowledge that ancient, malevolent entities known as the Old Ones are orchestrating a return to power. Horowitz uses this premise to explore how extraordinary courage often blooms in the most marginalized and overlooked places.

This theme of the global outsider is brilliantly embodied by the introduction of the second Gatekeeper: Pedro, a destitute street kid navigating the unforgiving, sun-baked sprawl of Peru. Through Pedro, Horowitz expands the canvas of the series, shifting away from the claustrophobic, rainy English countryside of the first book and plunging the reader into a vibrant, sun-drenched, yet deeply menacing South American landscape. The juxtaposition of Matt's British reality with Pedro's brutal street survival grounds the supernatural elements in raw, gritty human emotion. They are not classic, cape-wearing superheroes; they are terrified children forced to navigate corruption, corporate evil, and ancient sorcery with very little adult help.

Stylistically, Anthony Horowitz writes with the precision of a veteran thriller novelist who deeply respects his audience's intelligence. His prose is lean, cinematic, and relentlessly paced. Chapters end on agonizing cliffhangers that make putting the book down an exercise in pure frustration. Horowitz has an uncanny ability to craft visceral, atmospheric settings—whether it is the suffocating heat of the Peruvian desert, the sterile corporate menace of the ominous National Organization for the Advancement of Humanity, or the creeping dread of a supernatural ambush. There is a palpable sense of physical danger in his writing; characters bleed, panic, and make mistakes, ensuring that the stakes always feel terrifyingly real. 

Furthermore, Horowitz channels the spirit of classic horror—reminiscent of Stephen King or James Herbert—and repackages it for a younger audience without ever sanitizing the scares. The evil in *Evil Star* is not misunderstood; it is ancient, cruel, and profoundly alien. This unwavering commitment to genuine peril is what elevates the novel above generic fantasy. 

*Evil Star* is a triumphant exercise in momentum and atmosphere. It broadens the scope of the *Power of Five* universe while deepening our emotional investment in its reluctant heroes. Horowitz delivers a masterclass in tension, cementing his status as a titan of contemporary speculative fiction. For anyone willing to look into the abyss alongside Matt and Pedro, this book offers a dark, thrilling, and utterly unforgettable ride.
