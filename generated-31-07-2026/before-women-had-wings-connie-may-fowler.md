# Before Women Had Wings

**Author:** Connie May Fowler  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2005

In The Problem with Murmur Lee, Connie May Fowler returns to the raw, lyrical territory that defined her earlier work, yet she crafts something structurally more daring. While the publisher’s synopsis frames the narrative around a sudden drowning, this is far from a standard procedural. Instead, Fowler offers a prismatic meditation on the geography of grief and the way the dead continue to edit the stories of the living long after they are gone.

The premise is deceptively simple: Murmur Lee Harp, the gravitational center of a disparate group of misfits, vanishes into the Iris Haven river, leaving behind a vacuum that her friends must navigate. There is Charlee Mudd, the self-exiled wanderer; Edith Piaf, a trans woman navigating the complexities of identity and courage; and Dr. Zachary Klein, a man whose anchor has been violently severed. But Fowler’s masterstroke is granting a voice to the deceased. By allowing Murmur Lee to narrate from the periphery of the afterlife, Fowler shifts the book from a mourning ritual into a surreal, haunting excavation of family history.

Fowler’s style remains her greatest asset. She writes with a Southern Gothic intensity that feels humid and lived-in, grounding the ethereal mystery of Murmur’s death in the grit of human frailty. She does not shy away from the "failings" mentioned in the synopsis; she leans into them. Her characters are messy, often contradictory, and deeply human. Whether she is describing the quiet, suffocating loneliness of a widower or the reclaimed confidence of a woman who transitioned in her sixties, Fowler writes with a startling empathy that avoids sentimentality.

The central theme is the untidy nature of intimacy. We often think we know those we love, but Fowler posits that our friends are essentially strangers who have allowed us to glimpse the edges of their secrets. As the survivors pick through the remnants of Murmur’s life, they aren’t just looking for the truth of her death; they are looking for absolution for their own pasts. The mystery of the river serves as a catalyst for the characters to finally stop running from their own ghosts.

This is not a book for those seeking a fast-paced thriller. It is a slow, rhythmic unraveling of lives tangled together by fate and choice. The narrative structure, weaving the voices of the living with the retrospective observations of the dead, creates a mosaic of memory. It challenges the reader to consider what we leave behind and, perhaps more importantly, what we take with us when someone exits our lives.

The Problem with Murmur Lee is a rewarding, if challenging, work that solidifies Connie May Fowler’s place as a poignant chronicler of the human condition. It is a story about the stubborn, inconvenient, and beautiful ties that refuse to be broken, even by the finality of the grave.
