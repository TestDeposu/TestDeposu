# Rock Paper Scissors

**Author:** Alice Feeney  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Rock Paper Scissors pulls the reader into a marriage that feels both familiar and eerily off‑kilter from the first page. Alice Feeney wastes no time establishing the central tension: Adam Wright, a successful screenwriter living with prosopagnosia, cannot recognize the faces of the people he loves most—including his wife, Amelia. This neurological quirk isn’t merely a gimmick; it becomes the lens through which Feeney explores trust, perception, and the ways we construct narratives about those closest to us. The couple’s ritual of exchanging traditional anniversary gifts—paper, cotton, pottery, tin—each accompanied by a sealed letter Amelia never lets Adam read, creates a ticking clock of secrecy that feels both intimate and ominous.

Feeney’s style is razor‑sharp and relentlessly paced. She alternates between Adam’s fragmented, present‑tense perspective and Amelia’s more measured, retrospective letters, allowing the reader to piece together the truth alongside the protagonists. The prose is economical yet evocative, each sentence nudging the reader forward while planting subtle doubts. Feeney’s reputation as the “queen of the twist” is well earned here; the novel’s mid‑point revelation reconfigures everything we thought we knew about the Wrights, and the final act delivers a gut‑punch that feels inevitable only in retrospect.

Beyond the mechanics of the plot, the book’s core theme is the fragility of identity within a long‑term partnership. By stripping Adam of facial recognition, Feeney forces us to ask: how well do we really know the people we share our lives with when we rely on visual cues, habits, and shared stories? The Scottish getaway, ostensibly a chance for renewal, becomes a stage where lies, misunderstandings, and hidden motives collide. The setting—misty lochs, ancient stone cottages—mirrors the murky motivations of the characters, adding an atmospheric layer that heightens the suspense without overshadowing the psychological drama.

Rock Paper Scissors succeeds because it marries a high‑concept premise with deeply human emotions. Feeney’s deft handling of unreliable perception, coupled with her talent for delivering shocks that feel both surprising and inevitable, makes this a compulsively readable domestic thriller. Fans of her earlier works will recognize her signature blend of domestic unease and masterful twists, while newcomers will find a gripping entry point into her catalogue.
