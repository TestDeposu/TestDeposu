# Swimming in the Dark

**Author:** Tomasz Jedrowski  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

To read Tomasz Jedrowski’s debut novel, *Swimming in the Dark*, is to immerse oneself in water that is at once breathtakingly clear and perilously deep. Set against the crumbling backdrop of early 1980s Communist Poland, Jedrowski crafts a stunning literary mosaic that examines the high cost of desire in a society determined to police the human heart. Often and rightfully compared to André Aciman’s *Call Me by Your Name*, this novel carves out its own distinct, bruised territory, trading sun-drenched Italian leisure for the gray, suffocating concrete of a regime on the brink of collapse.

At the narrative's emotional core is the intoxicating and fragile romance between Ludwik, a sensitive university student, and Janusz, a charismatic and enigmatic peer. They meet at a mandatory summer agricultural camp, a state-sanctioned endeavor meant to instill ideological conformity, but instead find themselves drawn into a secret world of their own making. Their stolen moments by a remote river, shared over illicit readings of James Baldwin’s *Giovanni’s Room*, capture the absolute vertigo of first love. Jedrowski writes about this awakening with aching lyricism and raw sensuality. He portrays desire not merely as a physical longing, but as a revolutionary act—an assertion of individual humanity in a system designed to grind individuality down into uniform ash.

However, the pastoral idyll cannot survive the harsh realities of Warsaw. As the couple returns to the capital, the fractures in their relationship begin to mirror the fractures in the nation itself. Jedrowski brilliantly anchors the personal to the political, showing how the oppressive machinery of the state infiltrates the most intimate spaces. Janusz, pragmatic and ambitious, chooses to navigate the corrupt political ranks, securing a coveted government position by trading compliance for comfort. Ludwik, conversely, is consumed by a moral fury, drawn to underground student protests and unable to stomach the hypocrisy, empty shelves, and moral rot of the regime. 

The central tragedy of the book is not simply that they are two men loving each other in a deeply homophobic society, but that they ultimately want fundamentally different things from the world. One seeks to survive within the walls of the machine; the other seeks to tear the walls down, even if it crushes him in the process. This divergence transforms a tender coming-of-age romance into a heartrending political tragedy about the compromises we make to stay alive, and the pieces of our souls we leave behind in the process.

Jedrowski’s prose style is one of the novel's greatest triumphs. It is spare yet evocative, shimmering with poetic intensity. He captures the melancholy of a gray era with striking sensory details—the taste of cheap vodka, the heavy air of suspicion in university lecture halls, the contrast between the vibrant green of the countryside and the oppressive monochrome of the city. He understands that oppression is not always loud; often, it is a quiet erosion, a slow suffocation that teaches people to police their own thoughts and whisper their deepest truths.

*Swimming in the Dark* is more than a historical romance or a political thriller; it is a profound meditation on freedom, memory, and the sacrifices demanded by integrity. Jedrowski has delivered an indelible debut that lingers long after the final page is turned, reminding us that while love cannot always save us from history, the courage to feel it remains our most defiant act.
