# Moriarty (Horowitz's Holmes, #2)

**Author:** Anthony Horowitz  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

Anthony Horowitz’s *Moriarty*—the second installment in his *Sherlock Holmes* series—is a masterclass in reinvention, a bold and brilliantly executed pastiche that doesn’t just pay homage to Conan Doyle’s legacy but reimagines it with the razor-sharp wit and psychological depth of a modern literary craftsman. Where many Holmes adaptations falter by either clinging too closely to the original or veering into campy parody, Horowitz strikes the perfect balance: a story that feels both eerily familiar and utterly fresh, a detective tale that lingers like a well-turned phrase in the mind.

At its core, *Moriarty* is a meditation on legacy, power, and the inescapable shadow of genius. The novel opens in the wake of Sherlock Holmes and Professor Moriarty’s infamous duel at the Reichenbach Falls—a moment that, in Doyle’s canon, was meant to be the definitive end of the criminal mastermind. But Horowitz, with the sanction of the Conan Doyle estate, dares to ask: *What if it wasn’t?* The vacuum left by Moriarty’s death is immediate and toxic, a void that threatens to unravel the very fabric of London’s underworld. Enter Frederick Chase, a seasoned Pinkerton detective sent from America to hunt down the new face of organized crime—a figure even more insidious than Moriarty himself. Chase’s mission, alongside Scotland Yard’s Inspector Athelney Jones (a faithful but flawed disciple of Holmes), becomes a desperate chase through the labyrinthine streets of Victorian London, where every alleyway and gentleman’s club hides a new threat.

Horowitz’s genius lies in his ability to weave this narrative with the same meticulous attention to detail that defined Doyle’s original stories. The prose is crisp, the dialogue crackles with intelligence, and the world-building is so immersive that one can almost smell the damp fog of the Thames or hear the distant chime of Big Ben. Yet, where Doyle’s Holmes was often a cold, almost clinical observer of human nature, Horowitz’s version—while retaining his brilliance—feels more human, more flawed, and thus more compelling. The novel’s exploration of Moriarty’s legacy isn’t just about crime; it’s about the fear of what comes next, the terror of a world without a clear villain, and the way power, once lost, can be seized by those who understand the game better than anyone.

The style is a delightful fusion of classic detective fiction and modern psychological suspense. Horowitz’s Holmes is still the deductive genius, but he’s also a man haunted by his past, his methods, and the knowledge that his greatest battles are never truly over. The supporting cast—from the gruff but loyal Jones to the enigmatic Chase—adds layers of texture, making the story feel less like a rehash and more like an evolution. The pacing is relentless, with twists that feel earned rather than contrived, and a sense of dread that builds with each chapter.

What makes *Moriarty* truly special, however, is its willingness to challenge the reader’s expectations. Horowitz doesn’t just tell a story; he invites us to reconsider the myths we’ve been taught. Is Moriarty really dead? Could his influence linger in ways even Holmes never imagined? The novel’s final act is a tour de force, a climax that subverts the very idea of closure, leaving the reader with the same unsettling sense of possibility that defined the original Holmes tales.

For fans of classic detective fiction, *Moriarty* is a love letter to the genre, but it’s also a testament to Horowitz’s skill as a storyteller who can make the familiar feel new again. It’s a book that rewards close reading, that lingers in the mind long after the last page, and that proves once more why Anthony Horowitz is one of the most gifted writers working in the Sherlock Holmes tradition today.
