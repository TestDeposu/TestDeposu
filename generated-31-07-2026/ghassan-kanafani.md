# عائد إلى حيفا

**Author:** Ghassan Kanafani  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

Ghassan Kanafani’s novella, *Returning to Haifa*, stands as a monumental pillar of Palestinian literature, cutting deep into the psychological and emotional anatomy of displacement, identity, and the tragic legacy of 1948. Without relying on sweeping historical lectures, Kanafani achieves something far more powerful: he personalizes the catastrophe. He shrinks the vast, complex geopolitical trauma of the Nakba into the living room of a single shattered home, transforming a political tragedy into a devastating human dilemma. 

The narrative unfolds in the immediate aftermath of the 1967 Six-Day War, a moment when the borders temporarily dissolved and Safiyya and Said, a Palestinian couple, are finally able to return to Haifa—the city they were forced to flee twenty years earlier during the creation of Israel. Their journey is not merely physical; it is a desperate pilgrimage back to the fragments of their past, specifically to retrieve their infant son, Khaldun, whom they had to leave behind in the frantic panic of their expulsion. 

Yet, Kanafani denies his characters—and his readers—any easy emotional catharsis. Upon returning to their old home, Safiyya and Said discover that the house is now occupied by an aging Polish-Jewish Holocaust survivor and her adopted son. That son is none other than Khaldun, renamed Dov, now raised as an Israeli soldier. 

This breathtaking confrontation forms the emotional and philosophical core of the book. Kanafani uses this agonizing triangle not to construct a simple melodrama, but to interrogate the very definitions of belonging, parenthood, and nationalism. Through Safiyya and Said, Kanafani examines the paralyzing grief of the exile and the dangerous nostalgia that threatens to freeze time. Through Dov, the author confronts the painful complexity of identity, addressing how histories are overwritten, how trauma is inherited, and how a people can be dispossessed not just of their land, but of their lineage.

Kanafani’s literary style is remarkable for its stark minimalism, razor-sharp precision, and relentless psychological depth. He writes with a journalist’s eye for stark reality combined with a dramatist's ear for profound dialogue. There is no superfluous ornamentation in his prose; every sentence is weighted with urgency and ideological clarity. He avoids sentimentality, forcing his characters—and us—to confront uncomfortable truths. The conversations between Said and Dov are masterpieces of ideological tension, where the definitions of "home," "humanity," and "loyalty" are completely dismantled and rebuilt. 

What makes *Returning to Haifa* enduringly striking is Kanafani’s refusal to offer neat resolutions. The book challenges static notions of patriotism, arguing that a true return is not merely a matter of stepping back onto old geography, but an active, painful awakening and a forward-looking commitment to liberation. It is a piercing, unforgettable masterpiece that continues to resonate because it speaks not only to the history of Palestine, but to the universal, aching human condition of what it means to lose everything, and how one finds the courage to rebuild from the ashes.
