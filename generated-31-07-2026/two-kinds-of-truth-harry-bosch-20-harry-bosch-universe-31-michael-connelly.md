# Two Kinds of Truth (Harry Bosch, #20; Harry Bosch Universe, #31)

**Author:** Michael Connelly  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

**Michael Connelly’s *Two Kinds of Truth* is a masterclass in the art of the detective novel—a gripping, morally complex thriller that proves why Harry Bosch remains one of literature’s most compelling protagonists.**

At the heart of this twenty-first installment in the Bosch series lies a question as old as the genre itself: *What is truth, and who gets to decide?* Connelly, ever the wordsmith, weaves this theme into the fabric of the story with a deft hand. Bosch, exiled from the LAPD after a controversial case, is forced to confront not just the criminals who prey on the vulnerable, but the very system that once protected him. The novel’s dual plot—one chasing a serial killer in the shadowy underworld of prescription drug trafficking, the other unraveling a decades-old wrongful conviction—serves as a mirror to Bosch’s own fractured reputation. The tension between these two narratives isn’t just narrative device; it’s a meditation on justice, perception, and the cost of truth.

Connelly’s prose is as sharp and precise as ever. His descriptions of the pill mill’s oppressive atmosphere—where desperation and greed collide—are visceral, painting a world where morality is a luxury few can afford. The dialogue crackles with authenticity, whether it’s the razor-sharp exchanges between Bosch and his reluctant allies or the chilling calm of the killer’s voice. Yet, what truly elevates the book is Connelly’s ability to make Bosch feel both human and heroic. The detective’s exhaustion, his stubbornness, and his quiet rage are all rendered with empathy, making his journey feel deeply personal. When Bosch must go undercover in a world that would happily bury him, the stakes aren’t just professional—they’re existential.

The novel’s pacing is relentless, but never at the expense of depth. Connelly balances the high-stakes action of the drugstore murders with the slower, more methodical unraveling of Bosch’s past, creating a rhythm that mirrors the detective’s own struggle. The reveal of the long-imprisoned killer’s claims isn’t just a plot twist; it’s a gut punch, forcing Bosch—and the reader—to question everything they thought they knew. By the time the truth emerges, it’s not just a resolution, but a reckoning.

What makes *Two Kinds of Truth* stand out isn’t just its gripping mystery, but its philosophical undercurrent. Connelly doesn’t shy away from the ambiguity of justice. There are no neat victories here; only the grim acceptance that some truths are liberating, while others leave scars. Bosch’s final confrontation with the killer isn’t just about stopping a murderer—it’s about reclaiming his own narrative. In that sense, the book is as much about redemption as it is about solving crimes.

For fans of Connelly’s work, this is a return to form—a thriller that’s as intellectually engaging as it is emotionally charged. For newcomers, it’s a perfect introduction to a series where the real mystery isn’t just who did it, but *why it matters*. *Two Kinds of Truth* is proof that Michael Connelly doesn’t just write cops; he crafts characters who feel like real people in a world that’s as brutal as it is beautiful.
