# The Art of Being Normal

**Author:** Lisa Williamson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

The Art of Being Normal by Lisa Williamson arrives as a quiet yet potent reminder that adolescence is less about fitting into a prescribed mold and more about daring to re‑shape that mold for oneself. Set against the ordinary corridors of Eden Park School, the novel follows two British teens—David Piper, who longs to live as a girl, and Leo Denton, a trans boy trying to stay invisible after his past is exposed—whose unlikely friendship becomes the scaffolding for a story that is as much about self‑definition as it is about acceptance.

At its heart, the book wrestles with the question of what “normal” really means when the world insists on labeling you before you’ve had a chance to name yourself. Williamson lets this theme unfold through the parallel inner lives of David and Leo, allowing readers to feel the weight of each character’s secret, the tremor of fear when a hallway whisper threatens to become a roar, and the quiet courage that surfaces when they choose to trust one another. The narrative avoids didacticism; instead, it shows normalcy as a fluid, personal construct—something that shifts with each honest conversation, each brave step toward authenticity, and each act of solidarity between friends.

Williamson’s style is notably accessible without sacrificing depth. She employs a dual‑first‑person perspective that alternates between David’s and Leo’s viewpoints, giving the reader intimate access to their distinct anxieties and hopes. This technique does more than vary the voice; it creates a rhythmic empathy that mirrors the push‑and‑pull of teenage friendship. The prose is crisp and conversational, peppered with British colloquialisms that ground the story in a recognizable setting while still feeling universal. Dialogue snaps with the awkward sincerity of adolescents navigating identity, and descriptive passages—whether detailing the sting of a locker‑room joke or the soft relief of a shared smile—are rendered with a tactile sensitivity that makes the emotional landscape palpable.

What distinguishes Williamson’s handling of transgender experiences is her refusal to reduce her characters to their gender dysphoria alone. David’s desire to be a girl is intertwined with his love of art, his fear of disappointing his parents, and his yearning for simple teenage milestones like a first dance. Leo’s struggle is equally layered: he grapples with the aftermath of being outed, the pressure to conform to masculine expectations, and the lingering guilt of protecting a secret that once kept him safe. By presenting these facets, the novel resists the trap of a “issue book” and instead offers a fully realized portrait of young people who happen to be trans—a nuance that reinforces the book’s central claim that normal is not a monolith but a mosaic of individual truths.

The novel’s pacing is steady, building tension through small, everyday confrontations rather than grandiose melodrama. This approach makes the eventual moments of revelation—David’s tentative coming out to his family, Leo’s decision to speak his truth in a school assembly—feel earned and resonant. Williamson balances these climaxes with quieter scenes of solidarity: shared lunches, late‑night texts, and the simple act of listening. These interludes reinforce the idea that support often arrives not in grand gestures but in the steady presence of a friend who sees you, wholly and without judgment.

If there is a minor critique, it lies in the occasional reliance on familiar YA tropes—the bemused best friend, the antagonistic bully, the oblivious parent—that can feel slightly predictable. Yet Williamson infuses these archetypes with enough specificity and heart that they never become clichés; instead, they serve as recognizable touchstones that allow the more innovative elements—the authentic trans narratives and the exploration of friendship as a lifeline—to shine brighter.

In sum, The Art of Being Normal is a welcome addition to contemporary LGBTQ literature. Williamson’s clear, empathetic prose and her commitment to portraying transgender teens as multidimensional individuals make the novel both an engaging read and an important mirror for readers seeking validation, understanding, or simply a well‑told story about the courage it takes to define one’s own normal.
