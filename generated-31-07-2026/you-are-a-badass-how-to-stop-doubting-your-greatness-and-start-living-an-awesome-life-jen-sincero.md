# You Are a Badass: How to Stop Doubting Your Greatness and Start Living an Awesome Life

**Author:** Jen Sincero  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

Jen Sincero’s *You Are a Badass: How to Stop Doubting Your Greatness and Start Living an Awesome Life* bursts onto the self‑help scene with the swagger of a stand‑up comic and the earnestness of a life‑coach who’s actually lived the advice she dispenses. At its core, the book is a manifesto for radical self‑belief: Sincero argues that the primary barrier between most people and the lives they crave isn’t external circumstance but an internal narrative of doubt, fear, and unworthiness. She invites readers to rewrite that story, to recognize the innate “badass” within, and to translate that recognition into concrete action—whether that means launching a business, attracting love, or simply feeling more joyful day to day.

Sincero’s main theme unfolds in three interlocking moves. First, she dismantles the myth that success is reserved for the lucky or the specially gifted, insisting instead that abundance is a mindset that can be cultivated. Second, she offers practical tools—affirmations, visualization exercises, and “money mantras”—designed to rewire subconscious belief systems. Third, she stresses the necessity of embodied courage: taking risks, speaking up, and embracing discomfort as the price of growth. Throughout, the author peppers her guidance with personal anecdotes—from her broke‑as‑a‑joke days as a freelance writer to her eventual six‑figure coaching business—showing that the transformation she preaches is not theoretical but lived.

What makes Sincero’s approach striking is her unapologetically candid, often hilarious voice. She writes as if she’s chatting over coffee with a best friend who isn’t afraid to drop a swear word or a sarcastic jab when the moment calls for it. This conversational tone disarms the reader’s defenses; the book never feels like a lecture from a distant guru but like a pep talk from someone who’s been in the trenches. Her chapters are short, punchy, and peppered with bold headings (“Stop Trying to Be Perfect,” “Your Money Story,” “Love Yourself Like Your Life Depends on It”), each ending with a quick “Action Step” that pushes the reader from insight to implementation. The humor—self‑deprecating, irreverent, and occasionally cheeky—serves a dual purpose: it makes the material memorable, and it subtly undermines the seriousness that can make self‑help feel intimidating.

Stylistically, Sincero leans heavily on storytelling and metaphor. She likens the inner critic to a “loud, annoying roommate” that must be evicted, and she visualizes wealth as a “flowing river” that one can learn to divert into one’s own bank account. These images are not merely decorative; they function as cognitive shortcuts that help readers grasp abstract concepts like belief shifting or energetic alignment. The author also employs repetition—certain phrases like “you are a badass” appear like a mantra, reinforcing the central claim through sheer frequency. This technique, while effective for motivation, can at times feel formulaic, especially for readers who prefer a more nuanced, evidence‑based discussion.

Critics of the book point out that its strength—its accessibility and humor—can also be a limitation. Sincero’s reliance on personal testimony and anecdotal evidence means that the book lacks the rigorous scientific backing found in more academic self‑help works. Concepts such as the Law of Attraction are presented with enthusiasm but little critical examination of the psychological research that both supports and contests them. Additionally, the book’s emphasis on individual agency sometimes glosses over systemic barriers—poverty, discrimination, mental health challenges—that can constrain one’s ability to simply “think positive” and achieve success. Readers seeking a deep dive into cognitive behavioral therapy, neuropsychology, or sociological analysis may find the treatment superficial.

Nevertheless, the book’s impact lies in its ability to ignite a spark. For many, the first step toward change is believing that change is possible, and Sincero excels at delivering that belief in a package that feels like a celebration rather than a chore. Her blend of humor, blunt honesty, and actionable advice creates a motivational cocktail that is especially potent for those who feel stuck in self‑sabotaging patterns or who have grown weary of overly solemn self‑help tomes. She invites readers to laugh at their own doubts, to treat fear as a signpost rather than a stop sign, and to claim their greatness with the same confidence they’d reserve for a winning sports team.

In sum, *You Are a Badass* is less a scholarly treatise and more a rallying cry—a high‑energy, joke‑filled invitation to stop apologizing for wanting more and start living as if you already deserve it. Sincero’s voice is the book’s greatest asset: it’s relatable, irreverent, and relentlessly encouraging. If you’re looking for a guide that will make you smirk, nod in recognition, and then actually do something different with your day, this one delivers. Just be prepared to meet the occasional oversimplification with a healthy dose of critical thinking—and to walk away feeling, quite literally, like a badass.
