# Under the Banner of Heaven: A Story of Violent Faith

**Author:** Jon Krakauer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2003

Faith is often romanticized as a gentle anchor in a chaotic world, a quiet source of solace and moral clarity. But what happens when absolute conviction intersects with absolute isolation? In Under the Banner of Heaven: A Story of Violent Faith, acclaimed investigative journalist Jon Krakauer rips away the sanitized veneer of religious history to examine the terrifying precipice where extreme devotion bleeds into bloodshed. Published in 2003, this gripping and deeply unsettling work establishes Krakauer not merely as an adventurer of the physical world, as seen in Into the Wild and Into Thin Air, but as a fearless cartographer of the human psyche and the dark corners of American religious fundamentalism.

At the narrative’s harrowing core is the brutal 1984 double murder of Brenda Lafferty and her infant daughter by her brothers-in-law, Ron and Dan Lafferty. The killers claimed they were acting under direct orders from God—a revelation known in fundamentalist Mormon lore as the "removal" revelation. Rather than treating this atrocity as an isolated incident involving two unhinged madmen, Krakauer uses the crime as a master key to unlock a much larger, subterranean world. He exposes the violent underbelly of America's fastest-growing indigenous religion, focusing on breakaway sects that continue to practice polygamy and view earthly laws as entirely subordinate to divine command.

Krakauer’s primary theme is the inherent peril of unquestioning obedience and literalist scriptural interpretation. He argues persuasively that religious extremism does not necessarily arise from a complete corruption of faith, but often from its most logical, unyielding extremity. By juxtaposing the modern-day Lafferty murders with the bloody, often overlooked origins of the mainstream Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints—including events like the Mountain Meadows Massacre—Krakauer draws a provocative, unbroken line between historical frontier violence and contemporary fundamentalist zealotry. He posits that when people are taught to prize divine revelation over communal empathy and civil law, violence ceases to be a crime and becomes, in the minds of the perpetrators, the ultimate sacred duty.

Stylistically, Krakauer demonstrates the rigorous instincts of an investigative reporter married to the narrative pacing of a master true-crime storyteller. The book moves fluidly between meticulous historical chronicle, sociological study, and pulse-pounding journalistic inquiry. Krakauer does not merely report the facts; he interrogates them, guiding the reader through dense theological thickets without ever losing momentum. His prose is stark, unflinching, and relentlessly analytical. He balances empathy for the victims with an unflattering, objective critique of institutional complicity, refusing to look away from the uncomfortable truths that mainstream religious organizations often prefer to bury.

Inevitably, the book stirred considerable controversy upon publication, drawing sharp criticism from the LDS Church for its conflation of mainstream Mormonism with the practices of violent, rogue fundamentalists. Yet Krakauer is careful to distinguish between the two, even as he illuminates how the religion’s foundational history—rich with visions, persecution, and prophetic mandates—created a fertile ideological ecosystem for extremists to exploit.

Under the Banner of Heaven is not an attack on faith itself, but a vital, chilling caution against certainty. It asks us to confront how easily sacred narratives can be weaponized by the desperate and the grandiose. Gripping, deeply researched, and profoundly provocative, Krakauer’s masterwork remains an essential American tragedy—a stark reminder that the banners of heaven have, on tragic occasions, been carried into the darkest depths of human depravity.
