# On the Come Up

**Author:** Angie Thomas  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

Angie Thomas proved with her sensational debut, The Hate U Give, that she possesses a rare and vital literary voice—one capable of capturing the raw pulse of contemporary youth with unflinching honesty. In her triumphant 2019 follow-up, On the Come Up, Thomas returns to the fictional neighborhood of Garden Heights to deliver a powerful, rhythm-driven novel that serves as both a vibrant love letter to hip-hop and a searing critique of systemic injustice. 

At the heart of the story is sixteen-year-old Bri, a fiercely talented aspiring rapper carrying the heavy, inherited legacy of her late underground legend father. When poverty strikes her household following her mother’s job loss and an eviction notice looms large, Bri channels her rage, hunger, and grief into her music. Her raw, unfiltered track goes viral, but instead of celebrating her lyrical genius, the media and her school administration swiftly weaponize the song against her. Labeled a menace and a hoodlum before she even turns seventeen, Bri finds herself trapped in a suffocating box of racist stereotypes. The central, gripping question of the novel asks: how far do you go to save your family when the world has already decided you are a villain?

Thomas’s stylistic brilliance lies in her ability to marry deep social commentary with irrepressible youthful energy. The author writes with a distinct, rhythmic cadence that mimics the very art form she celebrates. The prose crackles with authenticity, humor, and heart, bringing Garden Heights and its residents to vibrant, breathing life. Bri is not a sanitized protagonist; she is messy, defensive, fiercely loyal, and entirely human. Her internal conflicts regarding authenticity versus commercial appeal, and anger versus marketability, anchor the narrative in a deeply realistic struggle. Through Bri’s journey into battle rap, Thomas explores hip-hop not merely as a genre of music, but as an essential lifeline, an outlet for the marginalized, and a modern-day pulpit for truth-telling.

Furthermore, On the Come Up tackles the terrifying fragility of First Amendment rights for young Black artists. Thomas brilliantly illustrates how white-dominated media structures police Black expression, praising sanitized versions of struggle while criminalizing the genuine article. When society demands that Bri either bow down to their preconceived notions or become the aggressive caricature they expect, the novel transcends a typical coming-of-age story to become an urgent examination of autonomy and identity. 

Striking, insightful, and profoundly resonant, On the Come Up is a masterclass in YA contemporary fiction. Thomas does not offer easy answers or fairytale endings, but rather a fierce testament to resilience. It is an unforgettable reminder that for young Black artists, finding your voice is only half the battle—making the world listen without prejudice is the rest.
