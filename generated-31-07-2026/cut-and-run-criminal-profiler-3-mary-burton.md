# Cut and Run (Criminal Profiler, #3)

**Author:** Mary Burton  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

**Cut and Run: A Gritty, Unflinching Ride Through the Dixie Mafia’s Underworld**

Mary Burton’s *Cut and Run* isn’t just another crime novel—it’s a raw, unvarnished descent into the morally bankrupt heart of the American South, where the line between lawman and criminal blurs with the precision of a well-placed bullet. Set against the neon-lit backroads of Mississippi and the shadowy corridors of the so-called "Dixie Mafia," this third installment in the *Criminal Profiler* series is a masterclass in atmospheric tension, where every word carries the weight of a man who’s seen too much and still isn’t sure what to believe.

At its core, *Cut and Run* is a story about trust—how it’s earned, how it’s betrayed, and how it can unravel a man’s soul. The protagonist, Manfred "Hog" Webern, is a retired Dallas County deputy sheriff, a man who’s spent his life chasing criminals but now finds himself playing the role of one. His mission: infiltrate a traveling gang of Southern outlaws, led by the infamous Jasper Sparks, to stop their planned heist—a carnival robbery so audacious it would make Al Capone blush. But as Webern worms his way into their inner circle, he realizes the Dixie Mafia isn’t just a loose collection of thugs; it’s a family, bound by loyalty, blood, and a code as rigid as the bayou itself.

Burton’s writing is a knife’s edge of precision, slicing through the veneer of Southern charm to expose the rot beneath. The dialogue crackles with the cadence of the Deep South—slang-heavy, layered with menace, and dripping with the kind of dark humor that only criminals and cops understand. Lines like *"Ain’t no honor in a job like this, Hog. Just like there ain’t no honor in a noose"* land with the force of a well-aimed shot. The prose is lean, efficient, and unapologetically brutal, mirroring the world it describes. There’s no room for sentimentality here; Burton doesn’t flinch from the violence, the betrayal, or the moral ambiguity that defines her characters.

What makes *Cut and Run* truly compelling is its refusal to romanticize crime. The Dixie Mafia isn’t a band of Robin Hoods; they’re opportunists, sadists, and men who’ve long since given up on redemption. Characters like Slops Moline—a Charleston killer with a penchant for brutality—or Raymond "Hardhead" Weller, a contract assassin with a history as long as his list of victims, are drawn with a chilling realism. Even the women in the story, like the enigmatic Nell Bigelow, aren’t just eye candy or damsels; they’re players in their own right, navigating a world where power is measured in bullets and silence.

Themes of loyalty and betrayal weave through the narrative like a noose tightening around a neck. Webern’s undercover mission forces him to confront his own complicity in the gang’s crimes, blurring the line between hunter and prey. The carnival heist, while the central plot, is less about the money and more about the descent into madness that comes with such a reckless endeavor. Burton doesn’t just tell a story; she immerses the reader in the claustrophobic paranoia of a crew where every glance could be a threat, every silence a lie.

The Southern setting isn’t just a backdrop; it’s a character in its own right. The heat, the humidity, the stench of decay—all of it presses in on the story, making the tension palpable. Burton’s understanding of the region’s culture, its history of violence, and its twisted sense of honor gives the book a authenticity that feels earned. It’s a world where a man’s word is his bond, but his word can be bought, twisted, or broken with equal ease.

By the time the final curtain falls, *Cut and Run* leaves the reader breathless—not just from the action, but from the moral complexity of its characters. Burton doesn’t offer easy answers or neat resolutions. Instead, she leaves you with the lingering question: In a world where everyone’s a criminal in some way, who’s really the villain?

For fans of gritty, character-driven crime fiction, *Cut and Run* is a must-read. It’s a book that doesn’t just entertain; it haunts. And in the end, that’s the mark of great storytelling.
