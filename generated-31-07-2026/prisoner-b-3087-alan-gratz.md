# Prisoner B-3087

**Author:** Alan Gratz  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Surviving one Nazi concentration camp is a statistical impossibility. Surviving ten is a miracle. In Prisoner B-3087, acclaimed author Alan Gratz takes readers on a relentless, harrowing, and ultimately triumphant journey through the darkest chapter of human history. Based on the extraordinary true-life account of Jack Gruener, this 2013 middle-grade historical fiction novel transcends its target demographic to deliver a visceral punch that resonates with readers of all ages. Gratz does not merely recount history; he drops the reader directly into the freezing, starved, and terrified shoes of a young Polish boy whose world is systematically dismantled.

At the heart of the novel is the theme of identity versus dehumanization. When ten-year-old Yanek Gruener is rounded up in the Kraków ghetto, the Nazis do everything in their power to strip away his humanity. He is no longer a son, a nephew, or a student with dreams; he is reduced to a blue-inked serial number on his forearm: Prisoner B-3087. The narrative brilliantly explores how easily the oppressors try to erase a person, yet focuses even more intensely on Yanek’s internal battle to resist that erasure. Throughout his harrowing odyssey—from the Plaszów labor camp to the nightmare of Auschwitz, and onward through the frozen death marches—Yanek’s greatest struggle is not just keeping his body alive, but preserving his soul. He fights daily to remember who he was before the war, holding onto the flickering ember of his humanity in a world that operates on absolute cruelty. 

Gratz’s authorial style is uniquely suited for tackling such weighty subject matter for a younger audience. His prose is fast-paced, breathless, and unflinching. Knowing that the book is aimed at a younger demographic, Gratz wisely avoids the most graphic atrocities of the Holocaust, yet he never sanitizes the horror. Instead, he maintains a relentless momentum that mirrors the terrifying unpredictability of Yanek's existence. Short chapters propel the reader forward with a breathless urgency, mimicking the constant fight for survival where every second could be the last. One moment Yanek is facing the arbitrary brutality of Amon Göth, and the next he is hiding beneath floorboards, listening to the boots of soldiers searching for prey. 

Despite the overwhelming darkness, Gratz infuses the narrative with a striking, fragile thread of hope. Amid the depravity of the camps, Yanek encounters unexpected acts of kindness—a crust of bread shared in secret, a warning whispered in the dark, a fellow prisoner offering a moment of solace. These glimpses of light do not diminish the horror of the Holocaust; rather, they emphasize the resilience of the human spirit. Gratz shows that even when everything is stolen, the choice to hold onto hope and dignity remains within the individual. 

Prisoner B-3087 is more than just a historical novel; it is an urgent testament to resilience and an essential warning from the past. Gratz has crafted a masterpiece of accessible historical fiction that honors the memory of those who perished while celebrating the unimaginable courage of those who endured. It is a striking, unforgettable read that demands to be felt, remembered, and shared.
