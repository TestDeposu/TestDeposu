# The Diamond of Darkhold (Book of Ember, #4)

**Author:** Jeanne DuPrau  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2008

Returning to the Dark: A Review of The Diamond of Darkhold

Few modern children’s series have captured the fragile intersection of post-apocalyptic survival and hopeful human resilience quite like Jeanne DuPrau’s Book of Ember. With the fourth installment, The Diamond of Darkhold, DuPrau brings her celebrated tetralogy to a resonant, emotionally satisfying crossroads. For fans who first followed Lina Mayfleet and Doon Harrow out of the dying subterranean darkness of Ember and into the stark, unpredictable sunlight of Sparks, this final return to the ruins is a masterclass in narrative symmetry. 

The central theme of The Diamond of Darkhold anchors itself in the weight of legacy and the perils of human ingenuity. Having survived the harsh realities of their first aboveground winter, Lina and Doon are forced to look backward in order to move forward. When Doon uncovers a tattered book hinting at a legendary, long-lost device left behind by the mysterious Builders of Ember, the temptation to salvage the past proves irresistible. Yet, DuPrau’s narrative is never merely a simple scavenger hunt. It explores a deeper philosophical question: What do we do with the dangerous remnants of our ancestors' mistakes? As the protagonists journey back into the pitch-black shell of their former home, the book contrasts the blinding glare of untapped power with the steady, flickering warmth of community collaboration. The "diamond" of the title is not just a technological marvel, but a metaphor for the enduring clarity of human hope found in the most pitch-black circumstances.

DuPrau’s signature literary style remains a cornerstone of the series' enduring appeal. Her prose is deceptively simple, highly accessible to younger readers, yet remarkably rich in sensory detail. In The Diamond of Darkhold, she brilliantly weaponizes atmosphere. The transition from the sprawling, snowy vulnerability of Sparks back to the claustrophobic, silent ruins of Ember is rendered with chilling precision. Without relying on gratuitous darkness, DuPrau evokes a deeply atmospheric tension. Every echoing footstep in the abandoned tunnels, every scrap of decaying paper, and every shadow cast by a flickering match feels pregnant with history. Her pacing is brisk and cinematic, effortlessly balancing high-stakes survival elements with quiet, character-driven moments of introspection and growing maturity between Lina and Doon.

Ultimately, The Diamond of Darkhold serves as a fitting capstone to a modern classic. It honors the loyal readership that has propelled the Ember series onto nearly thirty state award lists and past the three-and-a-half-million-copies-sold milestone. DuPrau avoids easy answers, presenting a world scarred by ecological collapse and technological hubris, yet stubbornly redeemed by curiosity, friendship, and the refusal to let the dark win. For anyone who fell in love with that first electric spark of light in the original novel, this final journey into the ruins is an absolute must-read.
