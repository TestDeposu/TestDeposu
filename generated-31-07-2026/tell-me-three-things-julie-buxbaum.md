# Tell Me Three Things

**Author:** Julie Buxbaum  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

Grief is a notoriously difficult landscape to navigate, particularly for a teenager. In her engaging and emotionally resonant young adult debut, Tell Me Three Things (2016), author Julie Buxbaum explores the winding, often painful roads of adolescent displacement, loss, and unexpected connection. Blending contemporary wit with genuine vulnerability, Buxbaum crafts a narrative that feels both comfortingly familiar and refreshingly authentic, anchoring her story in the universal longing to be truly understood.

The novel introduces us to Jessie, whose world has been upended twice over. First comes the devastating loss of her mother, and shortly after, a jarring cross-country move from Chicago to Los Angeles. Her father’s sudden marriage to a woman he met online forces Jessie into the role of reluctant stepdaughter to a pretentious teenage boy, all while attempting to survive the cutthroat social ecosystem of the ultra-intimidating Wood Valley High School. Jessie feels entirely wrong in her new environment—isolated, grieving, and profoundly out of place. 

Enter Somebody/Nobody, or SN for short. In a premise that pays clever homage to classic epistolary traditions while updating them for the digital age, this anonymous schoolmate reaches out via email to offer Jessie an insider's guide to surviving the social jungle. What begins as a cautious exchange of digital advice quickly blossoms into a profound emotional lifeline. Buxbaum deftly captures the intoxicating rush of forging a deep connection unburdened by physical appearances or social hierarchies. As Jessie and SN share secrets, anxieties, and banter, the email inbox transforms into a sanctuary of trust. Naturally, the mystery deepens: Who is SN? And can a relationship built entirely behind a screen survive the harsh light of reality?

Buxbaum’s authorial style is one of the book's greatest strengths. Writing with an acute ear for contemporary adolescent dialogue, she avoids the trap of sounding like an adult trying desperately to mimic youth culture. Instead, Jessie's internal monologue is sharp, self-deprecating, and laced with a dry humor that acts as a vital defense mechanism against her grief. The prose moves at a brisk, addictive pace, driven by the irresistible mystery of SN’s identity and the undeniable chemistry of the correspondence. 

Crucially, Buxbaum does not treat Jessie’s grief as a mere plot device to be resolved by a romantic subplot. The shadow of her mother's death permeates the narrative, grounding the story's lighter romantic elements in genuine emotional stakes. The exploration of blended families, the awkwardness of new environments, and the courage it takes to trust someone again are rendered with nuance and empathy. 

Tell Me Three Things succeeds because it honors the complexity of teenage emotion without ever taking itself too seriously. It is a tender, funny, and deeply comforting read that speaks directly to anyone who has ever felt like an outsider looking in. Buxbaum reminds us that sometimes, the most profound connections happen when we least expect them, and that healing often begins simply by letting someone in.
