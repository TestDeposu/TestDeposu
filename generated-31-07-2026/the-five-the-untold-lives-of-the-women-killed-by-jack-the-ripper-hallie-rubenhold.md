# The Five: The Untold Lives of the Women Killed by Jack the Ripper

**Author:** Hallie Rubenhold  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

"The Five: The Untold Lives of the Women Killed by Jack the Ripper" by Hallie Rubenhold is a masterful work of narrative nonfiction that shatters the long-held myths surrounding the infamous "Ripper" murders of 1888. By shifting the focus away from the killer and onto the lives of the five victims – Polly, Annie, Elisabeth, Catherine, and Mary Jane – Rubenhold has crafted a book that is both a tribute to these women and a scathing indictment of the societal ills that led to their tragic deaths.

Rubenhold's writing style is engaging and compassionate, making it easy to become invested in the lives of these women. Through meticulous research, she has managed to reconstruct their biographies, revealing complex and multifaceted individuals who defy the simplistic "prostitute" label that has been affixed to them by history. We learn about their childhoods, their careers, their loves, and their losses, and in doing so, we come to understand the difficult choices they faced and the limited options available to them in the poverty-stricken world of Victorian London.

One of the most striking aspects of "The Five" is its portrayal of the grinding poverty and desperation that characterized life for many women in 19th-century London. Rubenhold's vivid descriptions of the workhouses, lodging houses, and hidden corners of the city bring to life a world that is both familiar and yet, utterly foreign. We see the daily struggles of women who were forced to navigate a society that was stacked against them, where a single misstep could lead to disaster.

Rubenhold's investigation also challenges the long-held assumption that all of the victims worked as prostitutes, a myth that has been perpetuated by the press and popular culture. By examining the evidence and challenging the prevailing narrative, Rubenhold gives these women back their dignity, revealing them to be more than just victims of a serial killer. They were mothers, daughters, sisters, and friends, with hopes, dreams, and aspirations that were brutally cut short.

What sets "The Five" apart from other true crime books is its refusal to sensationalize the murders or to focus on the killer. Instead, Rubenhold keeps the spotlight firmly on the women, using their stories to shed light on the broader social and economic issues of the time. The result is a book that is both a gripping narrative and a powerful work of social history.

In short, "The Five" is a masterpiece of narrative nonfiction that will appeal to fans of true crime, history, and social justice. It is a testament to the enduring power of women's stories and a reminder that, even in the darkest of times, there is always hope and always dignity to be found.
