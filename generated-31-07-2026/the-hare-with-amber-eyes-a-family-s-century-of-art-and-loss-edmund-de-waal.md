# The Hare With Amber Eyes: A Family's Century of Art and Loss

**Author:** Edmund de Waal  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

History is too often written in the grand strokes of wars, treaties, and the rise and fall of empires, leaving the intimate casualties of time buried in the rubble. In The Hare with Amber Eyes, Edmund de Waal achieves the miraculous: he writes a monumental history of a century of European loss and cultural displacement using objects that fit inside the palm of a hand. 

At the center of this breathtaking memoir are two hundred and sixty-four netsuke—tiny, exquisite Japanese wood and ivory carvings depicting animals, ghosts, and figures. When de Waal, a renowned ceramicist, inherits the collection from his great-uncle Iggie in Tokyo, he does not merely catalog them; he follows their ghosts backward in time. What begins as a curiosity transforms into an obsessive, deeply moving archaeological dig through his own ancestry, tracing the journey of the netsuke from a burgeoning empire in Odessa, through the glittering, art-soaked salons of fin de siècle Paris, to the dark, suffocating reality of occupied Vienna before their eventual return to Japan.

The central theme of the book is the fragility and resilience of culture in the face of catastrophe. Through the lens of the Ephrussi family—a dynasty of immensely wealthy and cultured Jewish grain magnates turned bankers—de Waal explores how art acts as both an anchor and a witness. The netsuke are passed from hand to hand, pocket to pocket, surviving the horrors of the Holocaust that nearly obliterated the family and their vast collections. While palaces are looted, fortunes are erased, and identities are systematically stripped away by the Nazi regime, these diminutive wooden and ivory creatures endure, whispering the stories of the people who once loved them. 

De Waal’s prose style is that of a master craftsman, echoing his vocation as a potter. His writing is tactile, precise, and rhythmic, shaped by an acute sensitivity to material culture. He does not just tell the reader about a room; he describes the weight of the air, the texture of the wood, the specific shade of light hitting a porcelain vase. This focus on the sensory makes the tragedy of the Ephrussi family all the more visceral. When the Nazis ransack the Vienna palace, stripping the family of everything, the violence feels doubly potent because de Waal has made us intimately familiar with the beauty that was there.

The book moves with a melancholic grace, balancing the glittering intellectual heights of Parisian high society—where Proust is a guest and the family patronizes the avant-garde—with the stark, chilling descent into the twentieth century’s darkest hours. De Waal is an honest narrator; he does not romanticize his ancestors, nor does he judge them. He observes them with the cool, analytical eye of an artist and the aching tenderness of a descendant trying to piece together a shattered mirror.

Ultimately, The Hare with Amber Eyes is a triumph of memory over oblivion. It reminds us that history is not just a sequence of dates, but a collection of physical things touched by human hands, carrying the residue of joy, terror, and survival across generations. Edmund de Waal has crafted a modern classic—a quiet, devastating, and ultimately luminous masterpiece of family history and material culture that lingers in the mind long after the final page is turned.
