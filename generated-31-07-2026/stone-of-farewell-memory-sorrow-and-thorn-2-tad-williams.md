# Stone of Farewell (Memory, Sorrow, and Thorn, #2)

**Author:** Tad Williams  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1990

Tad Williams did not merely write a sequel when he penned Stone of Farewell, the magnificent second installment in his Memory, Sorrow, and Thorn trilogy; he cemented a paradigm shift in the epic fantasy genre. Published in 1990, this monumental work took the foundational tropes established by Tolkien and breathed a melancholic, complex realism into them, laying the structural bedrock for modern grimdark and high fantasy alike. It is a book that demands patience, rewards endurance, and ultimately breaks the heart only to put it back together with threads of pure wonder.

At the pulsing center of Stone of Farewell lies the profound and haunting theme of memory—how the weight of the past dictates the agony of the present, and how forgotten histories inevitably rise to claim their due. Williams weaves a narrative where geography and history are inextricably linked to sorrow. The realm of Osten Ard is not merely a backdrop for swordplay; it is a living, breathing entity bruised by ancient conflicts, currently choking under the unnatural winter brought by the undead Storm King, Ineluki. The land itself remembers the blood spilled upon it, manifesting this historical trauma in blighted forests, freezing winds, and a pervasive sense of dread that seeps into the bones of every character.

Stylistically, Williams is a master of the slow burn, utilizing a sprawling, mosaic narrative structure that mimics the cadence of historical fiction rather than fast-paced pulp adventure. He refuses to rush. Instead, he lingers in the quiet, desolate spaces between battles, exploring the psychology of displacement and survival. The tattered remnants of the human army are not invincible heroes; they are freezing, starving, and terrified fugitives dragging themselves toward the mythical Stone of Farewell. This deliberate pacing creates a powerful atmosphere of claustrophobia and desperation, contrasting sharply with the breathtaking, sweeping vistas of ancient magic and otherworldly splendor found in the hidden heartland of the Sithi. 

The character development, particularly that of the erstwhile scullery maid turned reluctant hero, Simon, anchors the cosmic stakes in deeply human emotional truth. Simon’s journey away from wide-eyed innocence into the harsh, gray compromises of maturity mirrors the tonal shift of the entire saga. Williams writes with an uncommon empathy for his characters, infusing even minor players with distinct voices, doubts, and motivations. The prose is lush, lyrical, and unapologetically descriptive, painting Osten Ard with a brush dipped in both breathtaking beauty and bleak despair.

While some modern readers accustomed to breakneck pacing might find the middle sections demanding, those who surrender to Williams’s rhythm will discover a masterclass in world-building and tonal cohesion. Stone of Farewell is a bridge between despair and hope, a harrowing middle chapter that elevates the entire trilogy into the upper echelon of speculative fiction. It reminds us that before a dawn can break, the night must reach its absolute coldest. Essential reading for anyone who wishes to understand the DNA of contemporary epic fantasy.
