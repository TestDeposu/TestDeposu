# He, She and It

**Author:** Marge Piercy  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1991

Marge Piercy’s 1991 speculative masterpiece, *He, She and It*, stands as a towering achievement in feminist science fiction, seamlessly bridging the gap between ancient rabbinic lore and a chilling, corporatized twenty-first-century dystopia. Winner of the Arthur C. Clarke Award, this rich and complex novel challenges readers to examine the very definitions of humanity, freedom, and creation through a narrative that is as intellectually stimulating as it is emotionally devastating. 

Set in a mid-twenty-first-century world ravaged by ecological collapse and carved up by ruthless multinational conglomerates, the novel follows Shira Shipman. After losing her corporate marriage and, more agonizingly, her young son to the predatory custody laws of her zone, Shira retreats to Tikva, a fortified Jewish free town. It is here, under the watchful eye of her brilliant grandmother Malkah, that Shira becomes entangled in a dangerous clandestine project: the integration and socialization of Yod, a sophisticated cyborg engineered for defense. Yod is a marvel of artificial intelligence—capable of profound emotion, sophisticated intellect, and lethal violence. Yet, as Shira and Yod grow closer, the boundaries between human and machine begin to blur, forcing both of them to confront what it truly means to have a soul.

Piercy’s narrative brilliance shines brightest in her structural choice to interweave Shira’s contemporary struggle with the legendary sixteenth-century tale of the Golem of Prague, created by Rabbi Judah Loew. Malkah tells this historical story to Yod as a way of teaching him about human nature, but the parallel serves a much larger thematic purpose for the reader. By juxtaposing the sixteenth-century mystic’s creation of a clay protector with the high-tech creation of a cyborg soldier, Piercy explores the timeless anxieties surrounding technology, hubris, and the ethics of creation. Who holds the responsibility for a created being? Does a constructed entity possess inalienable rights? These questions resonate with equal force in the muddy streets of medieval Prague and the sterile, neon-lit corridors of Piercy’s future world.

At its core, the central theme of *He, She and It* is the commodification of life and the relentless quest for autonomy in a world dominated by patriarchal capitalism. Piercy envisions a future where nature has been largely destroyed, information is currency, and human bodies are treated as corporate property. Within this bleak landscape, Tikva represents a beacon of resistance, preserving community, history, and spiritual depth. Shira’s personal arc—from a traumatized corporate exile to a woman reclaiming her agency—mirrors the broader societal struggle for liberation. Through Shira, Malkah, and the cyborg Yod, Piercy dismantles traditional binaries of strength and weakness, creator and created, human and non-human. 

Piercy’s literary style is marked by a rare combination of lyrical warmth and unflinching sociological insight. Unlike much of the hard science fiction of its era, which often prioritized cold technological extrapolation over human interiority, *He, She and It* is deeply rooted in the sensory and emotional realities of its characters. Her prose is dense, immersive, and unabashedly political. She builds her future world with meticulous detail, exploring everything from virtual reality networks and cybernetic enhancements to Jewish ritual, culinary traditions, and maternal love. The dialogue is sharp, and the philosophical debates embedded within the narrative never slow the momentum of a plot driven by high-stakes corporate espionage and existential peril.

Ultimately, *He, She and It* is a triumph of the imagination that feels increasingly prophetic with each passing decade. In an era dominated by artificial intelligence debates, algorithmic surveillance, and corporate overreach, Piercy’s 1991 warning resonates with urgent clarity. It is a bold, compassionate, and unforgettable adventure that questions the cost of survival and celebrates the enduring power of courage and connection. For readers seeking science fiction that engages both the mind and the heart, Piercy’s visionary novel remains an absolute essential.
