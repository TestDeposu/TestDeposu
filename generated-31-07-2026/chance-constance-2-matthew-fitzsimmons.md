# Chance (Constance, #2)

**Author:** Matthew FitzSimmons  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

Chance (Constance, #2) thrusts readers into a near‑future where quantum computing and medical breakthroughs have turned human cloning from speculative fiction into a luxury commodity for the elite—and a moral battleground for activists who call it an affront to nature. Matthew FitzSimmons builds on the tense atmosphere of his debut, The Rope, delivering a sequel that is as much a philosophical inquiry as it is a pulse‑pounding thriller.

The novel’s core theme revolves around identity and autonomy. Constance D’Arcy awakens eighteen months after a routine consciousness upload, only to discover that her original self is presumed dead and that her most recent memories have vanished. This abrupt rupture forces her—and the reader—to confront unsettling questions: What makes a person “themselves” when their mind can be copied, stored, and re‑installed? Is a clone merely a vessel, or does it inherit the soul of its source? FitzSimmons handles these dilemmas with a deft hand, letting the philosophical undercurrents surface through Constance’s frantic search for truth rather than through didactic exposition.

Stylistically, FitzSimmons excels at tight, cinematic prose. Chapters are short, often ending on a cliff‑hanger that propels the narrative forward with relentless momentum. His dialogue crackles with urgency, and the detective who becomes Constance’s uneasy ally is rendered with just enough grit to feel authentic without overshadowing the protagonist’s inner turmoil. The author’s world‑building is economical yet vivid: sterile clinics, neon‑lit black markets, and the quiet dread of a society that has commodified immortality are sketched in a few deft strokes, allowing the plot to take center stage.

What sets Chance apart from typical sci‑fi thrillers is its emotional core. Constance’s fear is not just of being hunted; it is of losing herself in a process designed to cheat death. FitzSimmons mirrors this internal conflict with external danger—shadowy corporations, militant anti‑clone factions, and a mysterious assassin who seems always one step ahead. The tension between these forces creates a relentless cat‑and‑mouse game that keeps the pages turning.

While the novel leans heavily on genre conventions—secret labs, covert operatives, and a relentless chase—its strength lies in the way it uses those tropes to explore deeper ethical questions. Readers who enjoyed the moral complexity of Kazuo Ishiguro’s Never Let Me Go or the high‑octane suspense of Blake Crouch’s Dark Matter will find much to admire here. FitzSimmons proves that a thriller can be both intellectually stimulating and viscerally exciting.

In sum, Chance is a compelling continuation of Constance D’Arcy’s saga, marrying FitzSimmons’s sharp, propulsive storytelling with a timely meditation on what it means to be human in an age where death can be postponed—if you can afford the price.
