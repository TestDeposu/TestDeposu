# Prometheus Bound

**Author:** Aeschylus  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1990

**Title: A Revelatory "Prometheus Bound": Aeschylus' Timeless Tragedy in a Modern Light**

Aeschylus' "Prometheus Bound" has long been a staple of classical literature, its themes of defiance, suffering, and divine retribution resonating through the ages. However, for those familiar with more traditional translations, James Scully and C. John Herington's 1990 version offers a refreshing and revelatory experience. This dynamic duo, comprising a prize-winning poet and a renowned Aeschylean scholar, has crafted a translation that not only captures the play's raw power and drama but also underscores its enduring relevance to our modern times.

At the heart of "Prometheus Bound" lies the titanic figure of Prometheus, bound to a rock as punishment for defying the gods by giving fire to humanity. Scully and Herington's translation masterfully captures the character's defiant spirit, his unyielding resolve a beacon of human resilience in the face of divine tyranny. Prometheus' famous words, "I will not tell" when pressed to reveal the secret of Zeus' impending downfall, are imbued with a fierce, uncompromising dignity that is both inspiring and chilling.

The translators' poetic prowess is evident in their ability to render Aeschylus' original Greek into English that is both accessible and evocative. They eschew the stilted, formal language often associated with classical translations, opting instead for a more conversational, yet no less powerful, tone. This choice serves to emphasize the play's universality, making its ancient themes feel immediate and urgent.

Moreover, Scully and Herington's decision to include the extant fragments of the companion plays, "Prometheus Unbound" and "Prometheus Pyrphoros," adds significant depth to the reading experience. These fragments provide valuable context and continuity, allowing readers to fully appreciate the arc of Prometheus' story and the intricate web of divine intrigue that surrounds him.

The play's exploration of power dynamics, both divine and mortal, is another aspect that lends itself to modern interpretation. The portrayal of Zeus as a capricious, tyrannical ruler invites comparisons to contemporary political figures, while Prometheus' status as a persecuted hero fighting for the rights of humanity echoes the struggles of marginalized communities worldwide.

In conclusion, Scully and Herington's "Prometheus Bound" is not just a translation; it is a reimagining, a reawakening of Aeschylus' timeless tragedy. Their work serves as a testament to the enduring power of classical literature, demonstrating how ancient stories can speak directly to our modern sensibilities. This is a Prometheus unbound, indeed, freed from the shackles of outdated translations and given new life for a new generation of readers.
