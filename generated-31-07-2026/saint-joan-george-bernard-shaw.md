# Saint Joan

**Author:** George Bernard Shaw  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

George Bernard Shaw’s Saint Joan stands as a towering achievement in modern drama, dismantling centuries of romanticized myth to offer a gripping, intellectually ferocious examination of history, faith, and institutional power. Written shortly after Joan of Arc’s canonization in 1920, the play refuses to treat its subject as a mere plaster saint or a tragic victim. Instead, Shaw resurrects the Maid of Orleans as a formidable, fiercely practical visionary whose biggest crime was not heresy, but independence of thought. 

At the heart of Shaw’s masterpiece lies a radical thematic reinterpretation of the tragedy. Most storytellers look for villains to drive their plots, but Shaw boldly dispenses with malice. As he famously noted in his preface, there are no villains in this piece. The tragedy of Saint Joan does not stem from cartoonish evil, but from the terrifying clash of well-meaning institutions. The churchmen who condemn her and the feudal lords who betray her are not acting out of cruelty; they are acting out of duty, preservation, and righteousness. To the medieval Catholic Church, Joan’s direct line to God bypassed centuries of necessary hierarchy, threatening chaos and anarchy. To the feudal state, her nationalism threatened the established feudal order. Shaw brilliantly frames her trial not as a kangaroo court of corrupt men, but as a tragic inevitability—a collision between the extraordinary individual and the rigid systems designed to protect the status quo. 

Shaw’s stylistic brilliance is on full display here, elevating what could have been a dry historical chronicle into a crackling, modern debate. Eschewing archaic language, Shaw has his 15th-century characters speak with the crisp, witty, and unapologetically contemporary intelligence of modern intellectuals. The dialogue is razor-sharp, packed with paradoxes and ironies that keep the audience constantly off-balance. Joan herself is rendered not as a trembling mystic, but as a refreshingly earthy, stubborn, and profoundly pragmatic farm girl who happens to hear voices. Through her, Shaw explores the loneliness of true genius and prophetic vision. 

The play’s structural pacing is relentless, culminating in the famous epilogue—a daring metatheatrical stroke where the ghosts of the past gather in 1920 to contemplate Joan’s belated sainthood. Here, Shaw delivers his final, biting critique: society is always eager to crown its rebels once they are safely dead and neutralized, yet it would crucify them all over again if they dared to return today. 

Saint Joan is far more than a period piece about a medieval heroine. It is a timeless, unsettling interrogation of how society treats those who see the world differently. Shaw challenges us to look past our own righteousness and ask a difficult question: When the truly inspired arrive, do we recognize them, or do we burn them at the stake for disturbing our peace? This dazzling, provocative work remains essential reading for anyone who values theater that challenges the mind and stirs the conscience.
