# Sweet Thing (Sweet Thing, #1)

**Author:** Renee Carlino  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Renee Carlino has long held a distinct place in contemporary romance, celebrated for her ability to anchor sweeping emotional journeys in the messy, hyper-relatable realities of young adulthood. In her 2013 breakout novel, Sweet Thing, Carlino leans entirely into this signature style, delivering a tender, bittersweet narrative about the paralysis of post-college life, grief, and the courage it takes to finally choose oneself. 

At its core, Sweet Thing is an exploration of the tension between expectation and authenticity. The protagonist, Mia Kelly, is the quintessential over-achiever on paper—an Ivy League graduate with a business degree and classical piano training. Yet, like many in their early twenties, she is fundamentally stuck. Carlino captures this paralysis with striking precision. Mia’s hesitation is not born of laziness, but of a profound fear of miscalculating her life’s trajectory. She is torn between the sensible blueprint laid out by society and the unpredictable, visceral pull of her true love: music. 

This internal paralysis is brilliantly externalized through the sudden death of Mia’s father. Grief acts as both the narrative catalyst and the emotional crucible for Mia’s transformation. When she relocates from Ann Arbor to New York City to take over her late father’s bohemian neighborhood café, Carlino paints a vibrant, sensory-rich backdrop. The café—a haven for undiscovered musicians and artists—serves as a physical manifestation of the father’s spirit and a sanctuary where Mia can begin to untangle her own desires. 

Enter Will, a gorgeous, charming guitarist who effortlessly walks into Mia’s life, first as a chance encounter, then as a friend, and eventually as a roommate. The friends-to-lovers and forced-proximity tropes are handled with a refreshing lightness by Carlino. Rather than relying on melodramatic misunderstandings, the tension in their relationship stems from Mia’s desperate need to suppress her passions—both for music and for Will. Carlino’s prose shines in these quiet, domestic moments between the two characters. The shared glances across a cluttered apartment, the strumming of a guitar in the background, and the unspoken chemistry build an intimacy that feels earned and deeply romantic. 

What elevates Sweet Thing above standard genre fare is Carlino’s refusal to treat self-discovery as a neat, linear process. Mia’s journey is not just about getting the guy; it is about reclaiming her voice. Her father’s legacy acts as a gentle, lingering nudge, opening her heart not only to romantic love, but to the terrifying, liberating prospect of pursuing her own artistic passions. Carlino writes with an empathetic pen, understanding that sometimes we have to lose the path we thought we wanted to find the one we were always meant to walk.

Empathetic, lyrical, and achingly nostalgic, Sweet Thing is an irresistible read for anyone who has ever stood at a crossroads, paralyzed by the weight of potential. Carlino crafts a love letter to music, New York City, and the messy beauty of becoming who you are, making this debut a timeless staple of contemporary romance.
