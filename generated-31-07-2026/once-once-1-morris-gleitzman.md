# Once (Once, #1)

**Author:** Morris Gleitzman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

To write about the Holocaust through the eyes of a child is a delicate and harrowing literary endeavor, yet in his 2010 masterpiece Once, Australian author Morris Gleitzman achieves the impossible. He crafts a narrative that is simultaneously heartbreaking and life-affirming. Set in 1942 Poland, the novel introduces us to Felix, a young Jewish boy hidden in a Catholic orphanage by parents who are desperate to protect him. Felix, however, is blissfully ignorant of the geopolitical nightmare consuming his world. To him, his prolonged stay is merely a temporary inconvenience while his bookseller parents handle business affairs. 

Gleitzman’s brilliant stylistic choice to filter unimaginable atrocities through the innocent lens of storytelling forms the emotional core of the book. Felix lives in a world of narratives. When he finds a whole carrot in his soup, it is not a mere vegetable, but a coded sign from his parents that they are coming to reclaim him. When he witnesses Nazi soldiers burning books in the town square, his naive imagination rationalizes the horror, convincing him they are simply foreign librarians helping to clear out the orphanage's outdated inventory. 

This technique of childlike innocence meeting brutal reality is both striking and devastating. As Felix eventually flees the orphanage to warn his parents of perceived dangers, he embarks on a perilous odyssey across Nazi-occupied Poland. Step by step, the protective armor of his imagination begins to crack. Gleitzman masterfully orchestrates this loss of innocence, allowing the reader to experience the creeping realization of horror right alongside the protagonist. Yet, what makes Once a triumph of modern children’s literature is that it refuses to wallow purely in despair. 

Amidst the grim surroundings, Gleitzman infuses the narrative with an indomitable spirit of love, fierce friendship, and even unexpected humor. Felix’s encounters on the road—particularly his bond with a young orphan girl named Zelda whom he rescues—highlight the resilience of the human spirit. Even as the shadows lengthen and the stark realities of the Holocaust become impossible to ignore, Felix never truly loses his hope. 

Gleitzman’s prose is deceptively simple, echoing the voice of a child, yet it carries the profound weight of historical trauma. He respects his young readership by refusing to sanitize the atrocities, yet he shields them with the warmth of empathy and human connection. Once is a poignant, unforgettable reminder of the power of hope in the darkest of times, and a testament to the fact that stories can be both a refuge and a mirror to the truth. It is a compact, punchy, and emotionally resonant novel that leaves an indelible mark on every reader who turns its final page.
