# Funny in Farsi: A Memoir of Growing Up Iranian in America

**Author:** Firoozeh Dumas  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2003

Bridging Cultures with Laughter: A Review of Firoozeh Dumas’s *Funny in Farsi*

In a literary landscape often saturated with immigrant memoirs weighed down by trauma, political despair, and cultural alienation, Firoozeh Dumas’s 2003 bestseller, *Funny in Farsi: A Memoir of Growing Up Iranian in America*, arrives like a breath of fresh, sun-drenched Southern California air. Chronicling her family’s relocation from Iran in 1972 when she was just seven years old, Dumas crafts a collection of autobiographical vignettes that is remarkably heartwarming, sharply observant, and profoundly funny. Without shying away from the genuine complexities of displacement, Dumas achieves a rare literary feat: she uses humor not to deflect from hardship, but to illuminate the resilience, warmth, and shared humanity that tie her family to their adopted homeland.

At the very heart of *Funny in Farsi* is the theme of identity and the delicate, often hilarious dance of assimilation. Dumas explores what it truly means to become an American without erasing where you came from. When the family first arrives, guided largely by the rose-colored memories of her engineer father’s graduate school days, they are met with a baffling array of American customs. From the linguistic minefield of menus featuring "hush puppies" to the culinary mystery of a Thanksgiving turkey that tastes like cardboard, the family’s misadventures highlight the absurdity of culture shock. Yet, rather than feeling alienated, Dumas and her family lean into the absurdity with an exuberant, affectionate embrace of America. 

Dumas’s narrative style is defined by a lack of sentimentality and an abundance of wry wit. She paints her family members not as generalized tropes, but as vibrant, eccentric individuals. There is her father, a sweet, quixotic dreamer who tries his luck on *Bowling for Dollars* and in Las Vegas; her elegant mother, who gracefully refuses to master the English language; and an uncle who battles the caloric onslaught of American fast food with a battalion of bizarre weight-loss gadgets. Even when geopolitics intrude—most notably during the Iranian revolution, which cost her father his job and suddenly turned their heritage into a public liability—Dumas maintains a tone that is remarkably buoyant. 

What makes *Funny in Farsi* enduringly striking is its profound sophistication masked as lighthearted comedy. Dumas does not lecture her readers on tolerance or multiculturalism; instead, she invites us into her living room, encouraging us to laugh alongside her family at the universal growing pains of fitting in. By bridging the gap between two vastly different cultures with clever self-deprecation and boundless affection, Dumas reminds us that the immigrant story is, at its core, simply an American story. Heartfelt, deeply engaging, and universally resonant, *Funny in Farsi* is a sparkling triumph of the memoir genre that leaves readers not with an accent, but with a renewed appreciation for the power of family love and cultural discovery.
