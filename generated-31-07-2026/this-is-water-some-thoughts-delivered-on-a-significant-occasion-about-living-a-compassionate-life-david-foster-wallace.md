# This Is Water: Some Thoughts, Delivered on a Significant Occasion, about Living a Compassionate Life

**Author:** David Foster Wallace  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

David Foster Wallace’s This Is Water is a masterclass in philosophical brevity, a rare jewel extracted from the commencement address he delivered at Kenyon College in 2005. Expanded into book form posthumously in 2009, this slim volume packs an existential wallop that outlasts many tomes ten times its size. Wallace, celebrated for his sprawling, maximalist fiction and labyrinthine intellect, here strips away the literary pyrotechnics to offer something infinitely more valuable: a plain-spoken, piercing manual for the art of being human.

The central theme of the book is deceptively simple: the challenge of real-time awareness in the crushing banality of daily adult life. Wallace opens with a timeless parable about two young fish swimming along who meet an older fish coming the other way, who nods at them and says, Morning, boys. How’s the water? The two young fish swim on for a bit, and eventually one of them looks over at the other and asks, What the hell is water? 

From this homespun beginning, Wallace launches into a profound critique of the default human setting—our inherent, unyielding egocentrism. We naturally navigate the world convinced that we are the center of the universe, that our immediate needs, frustrations, and discomforts are the primary metric of reality. Stuck in slow grocery store lines, crawling through morning traffic, or suffering through fluorescent-lit cubicle farms, our default state is one of resentment and quiet desperation. We view every inconvenience as a personal insult engineered by the cosmos to torment us.

Wallace’s antidote to this spiritual claustrophobia is radical empathy. He argues that true education is not about filling the head with facts, but rather about learning how to exercise some control over how and what you think. It is about simple awareness—awareness of what is so real and essential, so hidden in plain sight all around us, all the time, that we have to keep reminding ourselves over and over: This is water. This is water. 

By consciously choosing to override our default settings, Wallace suggests we can reframe the miserable, mundane irritations of existence. The screaming toddler in line, the exhausted mother, the rude driver—these people are not obstacles in our personal movie; they are struggling human beings fighting their own silent, desperate battles. To choose to think this way is not merely touchy-feely altruism; it is a matter of psychological survival, a way to keep from dying inside before your heart actually stops.

What makes This Is Water so striking is Wallace’s distinct literary style. Even in a speech format, his voice is unmistakably his own—a brilliant collision of casual, self-deprecating American vernacular and razor-sharp, exacting philosophy. He possesses a rare talent for examining the mundane without ever being banal. There is no academic pretense here, no ivory-tower abstraction. Instead, Wallace speaks directly to the reader with a desperate, urgent sincerity. You can feel the weight of his own intense consciousness behind every sentence, a haunting reminder of the acute sensitivity that both fueled his genius and tormented his spirit.

In a culture obsessed with self-help gimmicks and superficial productivity hacks, This Is Water offers something infinitely deeper: a philosophical lifeline. It does not promise happiness; it promises freedom. It dares us to wake up from our self-absorbed trances and notice the vibrant, shared reality humming all around us. Short enough to be read in a single sitting, yet profound enough to be revisited annually, Wallace’s final address remains an essential, life-altering text for anyone trying to navigate the choppy waters of modern existence.
