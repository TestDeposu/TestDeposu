# The Sentence is Death (Hawthorne & Horowitz, #2)

**Author:** Anthony Horowitz  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

Anthony Horowitz has carved out a brilliantly idiosyncratic niche in modern crime fiction, and with The Sentence is Death, the second installment in his Hawthorne and Horowitz series, he proves that his clever meta-fictional experiment is no mere one-hit wonder. Published in 2019, this novel once again blurs the boundaries between author and subject, reality and fiction, delivering a traditional British whodunnit wrapped in a sharply modern, self-referential package. 

The central mystery kicks off with the brutal murder of Richard Pryce, a high-profile celebrity-divorce lawyer who is bludgeoned to death in his bachelor pad using an exceptionally expensive, unopened bottle of 1982 Chateau Lafite wine—a strange weapon choice, especially since Pryce didn't even drink. Adding to the perplexing crime scene are the victim's final, cryptic phone-called words ("You shouldn’t be here. It’s too late . . .") and a mysterious three-digit number painted on the wall by the killer. Baffled by the puzzle, Scotland Yard brings in the abrasive, brilliant private investigator Daniel Hawthorne. And tagging along, much to his own chagrin, is Anthony Horowitz himself, acting as Hawthorne’s reluctant chronicler and Watson-esque sidekick.

The dominant theme of The Sentence is Death—much like its predecessor, The Word Is Murder—is the complex, often manipulative relationship between the creator and the creation, and the very nature of storytelling itself. Horowitz uses meta-fiction not just as a postmodern gimmick, but as a structural engine. By casting himself as a character in his own book, a stressed-out author struggling with deadlines, writer's block, and the abrasive personality of his detective, Horowitz breaks the fourth wall with delightful audacity. He constantly reminds the reader that they are consuming a constructed narrative, subtly poking fun at the tropes of the genre while simultaneously executing them with surgical precision. 

The thematic core also explores the idea of judgment and hidden sins. Richard Pryce made a living out of dismantling marriages and exposing the ugliest sides of wealthy, famous people, accumulating a massive roster of bitter enemies in the process. As Hawthorne and Anthony dig deeper into Pryce’s life, the narrative examines how professional detachment can breed personal danger, and how the secrets people bury inevitably come back to haunt them—often literally. Hawthorne himself is a study in hidden depths; he is a man made entirely of locked doors and evasions. While the murder of the lawyer drives the external plot, the internal tension of the novel relies on Anthony’s quest to uncover Hawthorne’s personal history, creating a dual mystery that elevates the stakes.

Horowitz’s writing style is a masterclass in accessible sophistication. He channels the spirit of the Golden Age of Detective Fiction—think Agatha Christie or Arthur Conan Doyle—infused with a contemporary, fast-paced cinematic energy. His prose is clean, witty, and relentlessly engaging. The dialogue snaps with dry British humor, particularly in the barbed exchanges between the exasperated author and the stubbornly cryptic Hawthorne. Horowitz is a fair-play mystery writer in the truest sense; he scatters clues across the pages with absolute transparency, challenging the reader to solve the puzzle alongside the investigators, even while distracting them with clever misdirection.

What makes the book truly striking is its structural confidence. It is notoriously difficult to write a book about writing a book while maintaining a high-stakes murder mystery, yet Horowitz pulls it off with effortless charm. The pacing is pitch-perfect, moving swiftly from the opulent, cynical world of celebrity divorce lawyers to the drab, rain-slicked streets where investigations actually happen. 

Ultimately, The Sentence is Death is a triumphant genre-bender. It satisfies the cravings of traditional mystery lovers with its intricate puzzle and classic clues, while offering a clever, winking critique of the thriller genre for more literary-minded readers. Horowitz has crafted a thoroughly entertaining page-turner that leaves you laughing, guessing, and eagerly anticipating the next chapter in their uneasy partnership.
