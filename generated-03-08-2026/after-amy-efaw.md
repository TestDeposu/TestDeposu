# After

**Author:** Amy Efaw  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Title: The Anatomy of Denial: A Review of Amy Efaw’s After

Few young adult novels dare to venture into the harrowing psychological territory that Amy Efaw maps out in her 2009 release, After. At first glance, the premise presents a chilling mystery: Devon Davenport, a straight-A student, star athlete, and quintessential overachiever, is suddenly locked inside a juvenile detention facility facing a potential life sentence. The unthinkable crime? Abandoning her newborn baby in a trash can. But Efaw is not interested in crafting a straightforward courtroom thriller or a sensationalized true-crime adaptation. Instead, she anchors her narrative in a far more unsettling exploration of human psychology, asking a deeply uncomfortable question: How far can the human mind fracture to protect itself from a reality it refuses to accept?

The central theme of After is the terrifying power of psychological denial and dissociation. Devon’s descent into the unimaginable is not portrayed as a calculated act of malice, but as the result of a mind shattering under the immense pressure of perfectionism, fear, and adolescent isolation. As a "good girl" who has internalized every expectation placed upon her by parents, peers, and herself, Devon cannot fathom experiencing an unplanned pregnancy. In Efaw’s harrowing portrayal, denial is not merely a choice; it becomes a literal, physiological state of being. Devon’s brain edits out her growing abdomen, the physical symptoms, and the impending birth, wrapping her in a cocoon of delusion so airtight that she genuinely believes nothing is happening. 

Efaw’s writing style matches the fractured state of her protagonist’s psyche. The narrative weaves masterfully between Devon’s current, sterile reality in detention—where she faces hostile guards, skeptical social workers, and the looming threat of the legal system—and fragmented flashbacks of the months leading up to the tragedy. The prose is emotionally raw, unsparing, and deliberately claustrophobic. Efaw refuses to offer easy answers or cheap sentimentality. She does not excuse Devon’s actions, nor does she allow the reader to easily dismiss her as a monster. Instead, she forces us into the suffocating confines of Devon’s internal world, creating a deeply empathetic yet profoundly disturbing reading experience. 

What makes After so striking is its refusal to let the reader remain a passive observer. By exploring the grey area between conscious intent and psychological dissociation, Efaw challenges our societal definitions of guilt, conscience, and culpability. The book serves as a stark critique of the environments we build for our youth—environments where failure is catastrophic, shame is paralyzingly heavy, and young people feel so utterly alone that they might hide a pregnancy rather than face the disappointment of those they love. 

Ultimately, After is a triumph of empathetic storytelling. It is not an easy book to digest, nor is it meant to be. It is a haunting, emotionally demanding page-turner that lingers long after the final page is turned, challenging our beliefs about the fragility of the human mind and the devastating cost of silence.
