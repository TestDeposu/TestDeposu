# The Declaration of Independence / The Constitution of the United States

**Author:** Founding Fathers  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

This pocket‑sized volume gathers the two cornerstone texts of American democracy—the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution of the United States—alongside the Bill of Rights and every subsequent amendment, all prefaced by a brisk, thought‑provoking introduction from celebrated civil libertarian Alan Dershowitz. The main theme is unmistakable: the enduring principles of liberty, self‑governance, and the rule of law that were forged in the revolutionary era and continue to shape the nation’s political discourse. By presenting these documents in their original language, the edition invites readers to confront the raw ideas that motivated the Founders, while Dershowitz’s essay bridges the eighteenth‑century text with contemporary debates over impeachment, free speech, and judicial review.

The style of the core texts is, of course, formal and juridical—concise clauses, precise terminology, and a deliberate rhythm that reflects the eighteenth‑century Enlightenment optimism balanced with pragmatic compromise. Dershowitz’s introduction adopts a markedly different tone: conversational, accessible, and unabashedly partisan in its defense of civil liberties. He writes with the vigor of a courtroom advocate, employing vivid anecdotes and pointed rhetorical questions that make the historical material feel urgent rather than antiquarian. This juxtaposition creates a productive tension: the solemn, timeless voice of the Constitution meets the modern, argumentative voice of a scholar‑activist, encouraging readers to see the documents not as relics but as living tools for civic engagement.

The edition’s greatest strength lies in its utility as a quick reference. Stripped of extensive footnotes, historiographic essays, or critical apparatus, it delivers the texts in a clean, portable format ideal for students, teachers, or any citizen seeking a reliable copy for classroom debate, courtroom citation, or personal study. The lack of scholarly commentary, however, is also its chief limitation. Readers looking for contextual analysis—such as the compromises over slavery, the evolution of judicial interpretation, or the global influence of these doctrines—will need to supplement this volume with additional sources. Moreover, Dershowitz’s introduction, while engaging, leans heavily toward a particular libertarian perspective; those seeking a more neutral historiographic overview may find it somewhat polemical.

Overall, this edition succeeds in its stated mission: to make the foundational writings of the United States readily available and to remind readers of their ongoing relevance. By pairing the original texts with a dynamic, modern commentary, it offers both a reverent nod to the past and a provocative invitation to participate in the present‑day conversation about what those words mean today.
