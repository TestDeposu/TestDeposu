# RoomHate

**Author:** Penelope Ward  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

Penelope Ward has long mastered the art of contemporary romance, but in RoomHate, published in 2017, she elevates the classic enemies-to-lovers trope by anchoring it in a landscape of profound shared history. The narrative centers on Amelia and Justin, childhood best friends and adolescent first loves who spent their formative summers tucked away in the idyllic sanctuary of Amelia’s grandmother’s house. When these absentee parents leave the sprawling property to the now-adult duo, the stage is set for a nostalgic reunion that quickly spirals into emotional chaos. Ward captures a universal human truth in the premise: you can never truly go home again, especially not with the person who holds the blueprint to your past. 

At its core, the main theme of RoomHate is the razor-thin line dividing deep-seated affection and festering resentment. Amelia, now a grounded schoolteacher seeking solace in the walls of her childhood memories, expects a peaceful summer getaway. Instead, her plans are spectacularly derailed when Justin arrives unannounced—not alone, but accompanied by his glamorous girlfriend, Jade, and his ever-present guitar. What follows is a masterclass in domestic tension. Ward brilliantly exploits the forced proximity of the shared inheritance, turning the beloved vacation home into a psychological battleground of lingering misunderstandings, unresolved grief, and suffocating jealousy. The house itself becomes a silent character, echoing with the ghosts of who they used to be while they tear each other apart in the present.

Ward’s writing style is effortlessly magnetic, characterized by snappy dialogue, emotional pacing that keeps the pages turning, and a dual-perspective narrative that allows readers to feel every sting of misunderstanding from both sides. She avoids overly flowery prose in favor of direct, visceral emotional beats. When Justin and Amelia spar, the banter is laced with a potent cocktail of hostility and undeniable chemistry. Ward has a distinct knack for making the reader feel the heat of the New England summer air mixed with the suffocating weight of unexpressed feelings. Every guitar strum, every lingering glance at Jade's presence, and every slammed door heightens the palpable friction between the protagonists. 

What makes RoomHate a standout in Ward’s bibliography is how she handles the evolution of emotional intimacy. The transition from childhood nostalgia to adult heartache is handled with a delicate yet gripping touch. The inclusion of Jade acts as a catalyst, forcing Amelia to confront the reality that the boy she loved grew up, moved on, and perhaps left her behind in the process. Yet, the beauty of the story lies in the peeling back of these defensive layers. As the walls of hatred and sarcasm crumble, Ward exposes the raw, beating heart of a first love that never truly died. 

Ultimately, RoomHate is a delightfully consuming read that delivers all the emotional catharsis romance fans crave, wrapped in a setting of sun-drenched memories and stormy nights. Penelope Ward proves once again that she knows how to make us ache for characters before giving us the satisfaction of watching them heal together. It is a poignant, witty, and fiercely romantic exploration of second chances that lingers in the mind long after the final page is turned.
