# Death Note, Vol. 5: Whiteout (Death Note, #5)

**Author:** Tsugumi Ohba  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

In Death Note, Volume 5: Whiteout, creators Tsugumi Ohba and Takeshi Obata elevate their legendary psychological cat-and-mouse game into a chilling commentary on modern capitalism and the corruptibility of absolute power. This installment pivots away from the traditional moral crusade of ridding the world of evil, steering the narrative into darker, more cynical waters where the ultimate weapon of divine judgment is reduced to a hostile takeover tool.

The central theme of this volume revolves around the commodification of morality and the terrifying adaptability of systemic corruption. When Light Yagami surrenders his Death Note—and with it, every memory of his dark crusade—he undergoes a profound psychological reset. Freed from the burden of being Kira, Light’s righteous indignation vanishes, replaced by the genuine, untainted innocence of a brilliant young man fighting false accusations. Yet, Ohba brilliantly explores the horrifying rigidity of institutional justice through L’s unwavering suspicion. L recognizes that incarceration is the only variable that halted the Kira murders, proving that absolute proof matters far less than statistical reality. 

However, the masterstroke of Whiteout lies in the emergence of a successor. As a new wave of Kira-style executions sweeps Japan, the ideological purity of the original killings evaporates. The new owner of the Death Note has no grand vision of a utopia, no messiah complex, and no desire to cleanse humanity. Instead, they use supernatural assassination for insider trading, corporate sabotage, and pure financial gain. By introducing a Kira who kills for capital, Ohba brilliantly satirizes the ruthless nature of big business. In this volume, the boundary between corporate greed and supernatural mass murder blurs entirely, suggesting that given the power to play god, humanity will inevitably use it to balance a ledger.

Ohba’s writing style remains a masterclass in psychological chess and claustrophobic tension. The narrative pacing in Whiteout is relentless, driven almost entirely by piercing internal monologues, rapid-fire deductions, and high-stakes psychological warfare conducted from behind glass confinement walls. Even with Light stripped of his memories and reduced to a spectator in his own fate, the intellectual sparring never loses its edge. Ohba constructs a labyrinth of logic where every single word, alibi, and coincidence is weighed on an invisible scale. The brilliance of the plotting forces the reader to constantly recalibrate their allegiance, wondering if a guiltless Light is more dangerous than a memory-wiped one.

Visually, Obata’s stark, moody artwork complements Ohba’s cerebral scripts to perfection. The contrast between the sterile confinement cell and the cutthroat, fluorescent-lit boardrooms of corporate Japan mirrors the thematic shift of the volume. The panels are dense with shifting expressions—shadowed eyes, beads of cold sweat, and manic grins—that capture the immense psychological pressure bearing down on every character. 

Death Note, Vol. 5: Whiteout is a gripping, sharply executed turning point in the series. It proves that the true horror of the Death Note was never just the notebook itself, but the corrupting human nature that surrounds it. Ohba challenges us to consider a terrifying reality: if justice can be bought and sold, the ultimate executioner will eventually punch a clock on Wall Street.
