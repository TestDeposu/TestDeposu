# Necropolis (Power of Five, #4)

**Author:** Anthony Horowitz  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

Anthony Horowitz is a master of propulsion, and in *Necropolis*, the fourth installment of his formidable *Power of Five* (Gatekeepers) series, he delivers a masterclass in escalating stakes and apocalyptic dread. Published in 2012, this novel finds Horowitz operating at the absolute peak of his dark fantasy powers, steering a narrative that has steadily mutated from a chilling British supernatural mystery into a sprawling, breathless global war between humanity and ancient, malevolent forces.

At the core of *Necropolis* lies the defining theme of the series: the crushing weight of destiny versus the fragile resilience of youth. Horowitz has always excelled at isolating his teenage protagonists, stripping away adult support systems and forcing them to bear the agonizing responsibility of saving a world that remains blissfully ignorant of its impending doom. In this volume, that theme is amplified by the claustrophobia of the setting. The pivot to Hong Kong—specifically the labyrinthine, shadowy underworld beneath the gleaming glass and steel of the modern metropolis—acts as a brilliant physical manifestation of the book's thematic undercurrents. The shining facade of civilization is merely a thin crust over an abyss of corruption and ancient evil, mirroring the internal struggle of the Gatekeepers themselves, who must delve into the darkest corners of their own fear and power to survive.

Horowitz’s authorial style remains a primary engine of the book's success. His prose is lean, cinematic, and relentlessly paced. There is no narrative fat here; chapters end on razor-sharp hooks that make putting the book down an exercise in frustration. He writes with a kinetic urgency that captures the breathless panic of being hunted across continents by an all-powerful corporate conglomerate like Nightrise. Yet, amid the relentless action and body-horror elements that push the boundaries of young adult fiction, Horowitz maintains a sharp focus on character dynamics. The inclusion of the final Gatekeeper, Scarlet, adds a volatile new dynamic to the group. Her integration into the established trio of Matt, Jamie, and Scott is handled with friction and emotional authenticity, reminding the reader that these apocalyptic warriors are still, fundamentally, frightened children.

What makes *Necropolis* particularly striking is Horowitz’s willingness to embrace genuine darkness. He does not offer easy consolations or cheap victories. The villains are formidable, corporate, and terrifyingly modern in their bureaucratic efficiency, wedding ancient cosmic horror to contemporary dystopian surveillance. This blending of the timeless supernatural with modern technophobia gives the novel its razor-sharp bite. 

While readers familiar with Horowitz’s previous works will recognize his trademark plotting precision, *Necropolis* feels heavier, darker, and more desperate than what came before. It is a middle-of-the-series triumph that refuses to tread water, instead plunging its heroes—and its audience—headfirst into the furnace. For fans of high-stakes supernatural thrillers, Horowitz remains unmatched, and *Necropolis* stands as a blistering, unforgettable testament to his storytelling craft.
