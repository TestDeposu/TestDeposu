# A Shadow in Summer (Long Price Quartet, #1)

**Author:** Daniel Abraham  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

Daniel Abraham’s *A Shadow in Summer*, the opening volume of the acclaimed *Long Price Quartet*, is a rare triumph in modern fantasy—a novel that deliberately trades the deafening clatter of traditional epic warfare for the quiet, devastating friction of politics, philosophy, and human cost. Published in 2007, this debut established Abraham not merely as a storyteller, but as a master architect of restraint. 

Set in the prosperous, sun-drenched city-state of Saraykeht, the novel immediately subverts familiar genre tropes. Saraykeht does not rely on steel, walls, or conventional armies for its supremacy. Instead, its untouchable economic dominance is anchored by something far more fragile and absolute: the andat. These are abstract concepts—such as planting, thought, or removal—given physical form and bound to human wills known as poets. Seedless, an andat capable of growing cotton without seeds, is the financial lifeblood of Saraykeht, bound for life to the aging poet-sorcerer Heshai. 

This premise introduces the novel’s central theme: the commodification of power and the terrifying price of holding it. In Abraham’s universe, magic is not a whimsical spark or a glowing sword; it is a burden that slowly hollows out the soul. The andat are sentient, resentful manifestations of ideas, trapped in human service, waiting for the inevitable moment when their masters slip up. This dynamic allows Abraham to explore the heavy ethics of imperialism and subjugation. When the aggressively expansionist Galt empire sets its sights on Saraykeht, they realize they cannot conquer the city by force alone. Their strategy is subtle, bureaucratic, and deeply psychological: remove Heshai, kill the andat, and shatter the city's economic foundation from within.

Into this web of geopolitical maneuvering steps Otah, a seemingly unassuming laborer with a complicated, aristocratic past he has spent years trying to shed. When Otah stumbles upon the Galtish conspiracy, he is thrust into a position of impossible responsibility. Unlike the chosen-one archetypes of standard fantasy, Otah is defined by his reluctance, his humility, and his desire to remain invisible. Through Otah, Abraham grounds the sprawling political conflict in raw, human intimacy. 

Stylistically, *A Shadow in Summer* is a masterclass in atmospheric prose and patient pacing. Abraham writes with a measured, deliberate grace that feels closer to literary fiction than high-octane pulp. He eschews massive info-dumps, instead letting the rich cultural textures, architecture, and social hierarchies of Saraykeht unfold organically through sensory details and quiet dialogue. The tension in the novel does not build through sudden explosions of violence, but through the agonizing tightening of a noose—a slow realization that the status quo is unsustainable and that every choice carries an irreversible cost.

The character work is equally nuanced. Heshai is a tragic figure, haunted by his past and crushed by the weight of the entity he commands. The antagonists, too, are driven by logical, imperial ambitions rather than cartoonish malice, making the conflict feel terrifyingly real. 

*A Shadow in Summer* is a thinking reader's fantasy. It challenges the genre's obsession with epic destruction by asking a much harder question: What happens when the things we rely on to build our civilizations are the very things that threaten to crush us? Abraham has crafted a mesmerizing, deeply intelligent narrative that lingers long after the final page. It is a stunning beginning to a series that redefines what speculative fiction can achieve.
