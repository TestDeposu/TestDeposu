# Epic Fail

**Author:** Claire LaZebnik  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Jane Austen’s timeless social comedies have survived centuries because human vanity, class anxieties, and stubborn first impressions never truly go out of style. In her 2011 young adult debut Epic Fail, author Claire LaZebnik takes the bedrock of Pride and Prejudice and relocates it to the sun-drenched, high-stakes ecosystem of a modern Los Angeles private school. The result is a sharp, witty, and deeply charming contemporary romantic comedy that proves Elizabeth Bennet and Fitzwilliam Darcy can trade witty barbs just as easily in designer jeans as they could in Regency-era empire-waist gowns.

At the heart of the novel is Elise Benton, the quintessential outsider by association. As the daughter of Coral Tree Prep’s newly appointed principal, Elise starts her tenure at a distinct disadvantage in a campus hierarchy dictated entirely by Hollywood pedigree. Into her orbit strides Derek Edwards—acting royalty, undisputed school royalty, and the living embodiment of haughty detachment. When Elise’s stunning sister draws the romantic attention of Derek’s best friend, Elise is thrust into the periphery of Derek’s exclusive world. Yet, unlike every other girl on campus, Elise refuses to swoon at his rare, calculated smiles. Instead, she finds herself drawn to Webster Grant, an unapologetic social outcast whose unexpected charm challenges her preconceived notions.

LaZebnik’s greatest triumph lies in how naturally she adapts Austen’s themes to the modern adolescent landscape. Coral Tree Prep is a brilliant stand-in for Longbourn and Netherfield, where the rigid class structures of 19th-century England are seamlessly replaced by the ruthless social castes of a Los Angeles high school. The currency here isn't land or inheritance; it's A-list parents, tabloid presence, and lunchroom real estate. By filtering Austen’s biting social satire through the lens of contemporary teen culture, LaZebnik highlights just how absurd high school politics can be, holding up a mirror to teenage insecurity with affection and a healthy dose of irony.

The author’s style is defined by its crackling, fast-paced dialogue and laugh-out-loud banter. LaZebnik has an ear for how modern teenagers actually speak—or at least how we wish they did, infused with enough clever repartee to satisfy fans of smart romantic comedies. The enemies-to-lovers dynamic is handled with gratifying precision. The friction between Elise and Derek crackles with energy, driven by mutual misunderstandings, bruised egos, and the delicious agony of two stubborn people refusing to admit they are wrong. Elise is a wonderfully grounded protagonist, armed with a sharp tongue and a stubborn streak that makes her missteps feel authentic. Derek, meanwhile, transcends the cliché of the arrogant celebrity brat, slowly revealing layers of vulnerability and loyalty that complicate Elise's neat categorization of him.

While modern retellings of classic literature often run the risk of feeling gimmicky, Epic Fail succeeds because LaZebnik respects the emotional core of Austen’s original masterpiece. She understands that the heart of Pride and Prejudice is not just about falling in love, but about humbling oneself enough to see the truth in others—and in oneself. Pride and prejudice, as it turns out, are universal flaws, whether you are dealing with entailed estates in Hertfordshire or paparazzi on Sunset Boulevard.

For readers who adore classic retellings, sparkling high school banter, and a slow-burn romance where the journey is just as entertaining as the destination, Epic Fail is an absolute triumph. LaZebnik delivers a breezy, engaging, and genuinely funny read that proves some love stories are simply timeless.
