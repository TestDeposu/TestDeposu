# Soul of a Witch (Souls Trilogy, #3)

**Author:** Harley Laroux  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2025

**A Fiery Reckoning: *Soul of a Witch* and the Redemption of Broken Power**

Harley Laroux’s *Soul of a Witch*—the explosive finale to the *Souls Trilogy*—is a masterclass in the alchemy of vengeance, divine hubris, and the cost of reclaiming what was stolen. This isn’t just a story about a witch awakening; it’s a searing meditation on the violence of faith, the illusion of control, and the way power, once wrested from the hands of the oppressor, can either liberate or consume. Laroux, who has spent the trilogy forging a voice as sharp as a blade and as rich as a cursed relic, delivers a conclusion that is as devastating as it is electrifying—a book that doesn’t just end a saga, but burns the old world down to make room for something new.

At its heart, *Soul of a Witch* is a story about the soul’s refusal to be tamed. Everly, the protagonist, has spent her life as a vessel for a god’s cruelty, her magic a leash around her neck, her devotion a cage. The first two books in the trilogy—*Soul of a Saint* and *Soul of a Sinner*—laid the groundwork for her transformation: from a broken acolyte to a woman who sees the god she worshipped as a monster. But it’s here, in the third installment, that Laroux forces Everly—and the reader—to confront the most terrifying question of all: *What happens when the witch finally turns the knife?* The answer is not neat. It’s not glorious. It’s messy, bloody, and utterly necessary. Laroux refuses to let her heroine be a passive agent of change. Everly’s power isn’t just about destruction; it’s about *redefinition*. She doesn’t just kill gods; she unravels the myths that kept her chained. And in doing so, she becomes something far more dangerous than a witch—she becomes a legend.

The prose is a weapon in Laroux’s arsenal, as always. Her writing is dense with the kind of visceral, almost tactile imagery that makes the reader *feel* the weight of Everly’s magic, the stench of hellfire, the cold precision of a blade meant to sever something sacred. There’s a lyrical brutality to her descriptions—lines like *“The air tasted of copper and sin”* or *“Her hands trembled, but not from fear—from the memory of what they could do”* linger long after the page is turned. Laroux’s style is a perfect match for the themes of the book: it’s both a hymn and a dirge, a celebration of defiance and a warning of what comes after. She doesn’t shy away from the grotesque or the sublime; instead, she wields them like a sorceress with a spellbook of contradictions.

But *Soul of a Witch* isn’t just Everly’s story. It’s also the story of Callum, the ancient, brooding dämon who has spent two millennia hunting her—a man who has seen empires rise and fall, who has watched gods die and rebirth themselves like phoenixes from their own ashes. His presence is a constant, looming shadow, a reminder that power, no matter how newly claimed, is never truly alone. Callum’s arc is one of quiet, simmering rage, a man who has spent eternity protecting what he can’t possess. His loyalty to Everly isn’t just about love; it’s about the rare, fragile thing that remains after millennia of solitude: *hope*. And hope, in Laroux’s hands, is a fragile, beautiful, and often fatal thing.

The trilogy’s central theme—the tension between divine authority and mortal rebellion—reaches its crescendo here. Everly’s god was not just cruel; he was *necessary*, a force that shaped the world in its image. To destroy him is to unravel the fabric of reality itself. And yet, Laroux doesn’t let the reader off the hook. She doesn’t make the choice between order and chaos, between faith and heresy, easy. The cost of Everly’s defiance isn’t just personal; it’s cosmic. The book forces its audience to ask: *Is it better to live in a world ruled by monsters, or to risk everything to build one where no one is sacred?* There are no pat answers here. Only the kind of moral grayness that makes the story feel *real*, not just mythic.

What makes *Soul of a Witch* truly unforgettable, though, is its refusal to romanticize power. Laroux understands that liberation is not a clean act—it’s a fire that burns everything in its path, including the hands that wield it. Everly’s magic is not a gift; it’s a curse, a force that demands sacrifice, that corrupts even as it empowers. The book’s climax isn’t a triumphant moment; it’s a reckoning, a moment where the reader is left breathless, wondering if the world will survive the storm Everly has unleashed. And if it doesn’t? Well, that’s the point. Laroux doesn’t write fairy tales. She writes *myths*—stories that are meant to haunt, to provoke, to make the reader question the gods they’ve been taught to worship.

In the end, *Soul of a Witch* is more than the conclusion of a trilogy; it’s a statement. It’s a declaration that power, once stolen, can never be returned to its keepers. It’s a warning that the gods are not benevolent, and the mortals who challenge them are not heroes—they are survivors, and sometimes, even survivors burn the world down to prove they were never meant to be slaves. Harley Laroux has crafted a story that is as darkly beautiful as it is devastatingly real, a tale where the soul of a witch is not just her magic, but her defiance, her pain, and her refusal to kneel. If you’ve followed Everly’s journey, you’ll be rewarded with a finale that is as explosive as it is inevitable. If you haven’t, you’re missing out on one of the most gripping, morally complex, and utterly unforgettable fantasy sagas of the year.
