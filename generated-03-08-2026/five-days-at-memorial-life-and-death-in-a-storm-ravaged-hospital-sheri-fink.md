# Five Days at Memorial: Life and Death in a Storm-Ravaged Hospital

**Author:** Sheri Fink  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

When Hurricane Katrina paralyzed New Orleans in August 2005, it didn't just breach levees; it shattered the fragile infrastructure of modern civilization, exposing the raw, uncomfortable truths of how society values human life under extreme pressure. In Five Days at Memorial, Pulitzer Prize-winning physician and reporter Sheri Fink delivers a monumental work of investigative journalism that reads with the gripping tension of a psychological thriller and the solemn gravity of a Greek tragedy. This is not merely a book about a disaster; it is a profound, unflinching examination of moral collapse, institutional failure, and the harrowing ethical dilemmas of end-of-life care when all choices are stripped away.

Fink’s narrative centers on Memorial Medical Center, where, trapped without power, running water, or air conditioning as rising floodwaters surrounded the building, exhausted doctors, nurses, and staff faced an apocalyptic scenario. As temperatures soared and rescue efforts stalled, decisions about who would be saved first mutated into decisions about who might not survive at all. Months after the waters receded, investigators discovered the bodies of numerous patients, leading to the sensational arrest of a doctor and two nurses accused of administering lethal doses of painkillers and sedatives. 

The core theme of Fink’s investigation transcends the specific tragedy at Memorial, probing deep into the philosophy of triage and the limits of medical ethics. The author refuses to offer simplistic heroes or villains. Instead, she meticulously reconstructs the descent into chaos, forcing the reader to confront the terrifying question: When resources vanish and infrastructure fails, what are the ethical obligations of healthcare professionals? Can a doctor ever be justified in hastening the death of a suffering patient whom they believe cannot be evacuated? By presenting the perspectives of the exhausted caregivers, the administrators, the grieving families, and the prosecutors, Fink creates a complex moral landscape where good intentions collide with horrific actions.

Stylistically, Fink’s background as both a physician and an investigative journalist shines through every page. Her prose is clinically precise yet deeply empathetic, balancing rigorous documentation with narrative momentum. The book is the culmination of six years of painstaking reporting, and that exhaustive research is evident in the vivid, minute-by-minute recreation of the five days inside the hospital, followed by the meticulous legal fallout. Fink does not manipulate the reader’s emotions through sensationalism; rather, the sheer weight of the facts and the complexity of the human dilemmas do the work. She lays bare the systemic failures—from corporate neglect to governmental unpreparedness—that forced medical personnel into an impossible corner, making it clear that the tragedy at Memorial was a failure of society as a whole, not just of the individuals trapped inside.

Ultimately, Five Days at Memorial is an essential, haunting read that challenges our understanding of human nature and our readiness for large-scale catastrophes. It forces us to look past the comforting illusion that our institutions will always protect us, leaving us with lingering, unsettling questions about mercy, justice, and the value we assign to the most vulnerable among us in their darkest hours.
