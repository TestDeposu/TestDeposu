# A Bad Case of Stripes

**Author:** David Shannon  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

A Bad Case of Stripes by David Shannon is a deceptively simple picture book that packs a powerful punch about identity, conformity, and the courage to be oneself. The story follows Camilla Cream, a little girl who adores lima beans but hides her love because she fears the judgment of her classmates. As her anxiety mounts, she literally begins to change—her skin erupts in vivid, ever‑shifting stripes that mirror the expectations and opinions swirling around her. The more she tries to please everyone, the more her appearance spirals out of control, until a moment of genuine self‑acceptance brings the stripes—and the pressure—to a halt.

Shannon’s hallmark style shines throughout the book. His illustrations are bold, saturated, and almost kinetic; each page bursts with color that feels both playful and slightly unsettling, perfectly echoing Camilla’s internal turmoil. The stripes themselves become a character: they twist, stretch, and proliferate in ways that are visually humorous yet unsettlingly apt, turning an abstract emotion into something concrete that young readers can see and feel. Shannon’s use of exaggerated facial expressions and dynamic compositions keeps the pacing brisk, while the sparse, rhythmic text lets the artwork do much of the storytelling. The dialogue is snappy and kid‑friendly, punctuated by the memorable refrain, “What we have here is a bad case of stripes!”—a line that invites read‑along participation and reinforces the book’s central metaphor.

Beyond the visual romp, the narrative tackles a theme that resonates across ages: the peril of letting external opinions dictate inner truth. Camilla’s dilemma is not merely about food preferences; it mirrors the broader social pressure children feel to conform—whether it’s about tastes, hobbies, appearance, or behavior. Shannon treats this pressure with both empathy and levity, showing how anxiety can manifest physically when we suppress who we really are. The resolution is satisfyingly straightforward: Camilla’s courage to eat her beloved lima beans in front of her peers dissolves the stripes, suggesting that authenticity is not only liberating but also contagious. The book never pretends that standing out is easy; instead, it validates the discomfort while offering a hopeful pathway forward.

What makes A Bad Case of Stripes especially effective for its target audience (ages 4‑8) is its ability to turn a complex psychological concept into a visual gag that kids can grasp instantly. The humor disarms any didactic tone, allowing the lesson to emerge organically through laughter and recognition. Parents and educators will appreciate the opening it provides for conversations about self‑esteem, bullying, and the importance of honoring one’s own likes and dislikes, even when they differ from the crowd.

In terms of literary merit, Shannon’s work stands alongside his other Caldecott Honor titles for its seamless marriage of text and illustration. The book’s design—large, full‑bleed spreads, varied typography, and clever use of white space—guides the reader’s eye and amplifies the emotional arc. There is a subtle rhythm to the page turns that mirrors Camilla’s rising anxiety and eventual release, making the reading experience feel almost performative.

Overall, A Bad Case of Stripes is a vibrant, heart‑warming reminder that the bravest thing a child (or anyone) can do is to be unapologetically themselves. Shannon’s wit, vivid artistry, and keen insight into childhood anxieties combine to create a picture book that entertains while it enlightens—a rare balance that ensures it will be revisited on shelves and in storytimes for years to come.
