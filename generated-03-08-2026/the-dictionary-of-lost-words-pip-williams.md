# The Dictionary of Lost Words

**Author:** Pip Williams  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

History is written by the victors, but language is curated by the gatekeepers. In her acclaimed debut novel, The Dictionary of Lost Words, Pip Williams brilliantly interrogates this premise, taking readers into the hushed, ink-stained world of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries to expose the subtle biases woven into the very fabric of our lexicon. Set against the meticulous compilation of the first Oxford English Dictionary, Williams crafts a luminous, slyly subversive narrative that asks a fundamental question: Who decides which words matter, and whose experiences are rendered invisible when language fails to name them?

The story follows Esme Nicoll, a motherless child raised beneath the sorting tables of the Scriptorium—a garden shed in Oxford where her father and a dedicated team of lexicographers labor over slips of paper, gathering every word in the English language. Unseen and unheard, Esme is a quiet observer until the day a slip bearing the word bondmaid flutters to the floor. Rescuing it, she discovers a stark truth: words pertaining to the lives of women, the working class, and the marginalized are frequently deemed unworthy of inclusion by the dictionary men. 

As Esme grows into womanhood, navigating the turbulent eras of the women’s suffrage movement and the approaching shadow of the Great War, she begins her own clandestine collection. She gathers the colloquialisms of the marketplace, the whispers of the servants, the idioms of the rebels, and the poignant vocabularies of everyday women. From these rescued fragments, she builds her own alternative lexicon: the Dictionary of Lost Words. 

Williams’s greatest triumph lies in her masterful exploration of the central theme: the profound power of language to both elevate and repress. Through Esme’s journey, the novel demonstrates that dictionaries are not merely neutral archives of human speech; they are instruments of power. By deciding what is official and what is vulgar, lexicographers historically marginalized the female experience, framing domesticity, emotion, and bodily existence as unworthy of formal recognition. Williams exposes this omission not with polemics, but with a deep, aching tenderness for the human voice. 

The author’s literary style is lyrical, tactile, and deeply immersive. Williams writes with the precision of a scholar and the heart of a poet. Every page smells faintly of old paper, ink, and damp garden earth. The pacing mirrors the deliberate, meticulous nature of lexicography itself, allowing the reader to savor the texture of language, the evolution of idioms, and the emotional resonance of forgotten terms. Williams seamlessly weaves historical milestones—such as the suffragette marches and the devastating human cost of World War I—into the personal awakening of her protagonist, grounding grand historical movements in the intimate, tactile reality of one woman's search for meaning.

Esme is a captivating protagonist—curious, fiercely independent, and endlessly observant. Through her eyes, the rigid class and gender hierarchies of Edwardian England are laid bare. Yet, the novel is never merely a historical critique; it is a celebration of human resilience and the irrepressible urge to communicate. 

The Dictionary of Lost Words is a triumph of historical fiction, offering a vital counter-narrative to a world built on male definitions. Pip Williams has penned a delightful, thought-provoking, and deeply moving tribute to the unsung women whose lives and languages deserved to be preserved. It is a book that will forever change the way you look at a dictionary, reminding us that language belongs to everyone—especially those who have been told to remain silent.
