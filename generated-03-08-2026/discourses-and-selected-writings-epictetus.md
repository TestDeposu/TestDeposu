# Discourses and Selected Writings

**Author:** Epictetus  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2008

If you are looking for soft-spoken comfort or intellectual hand-holding, look elsewhere. Epictetus does not care about your feelings; he cares about your freedom. Born a slave in the Roman Empire and later rising to run a celebrated philosophy school, this ancient Stoic master offers a bracing, uncompromising blueprint for the human condition in *Discourses and Selected Writings*. Originally captured and transcribed by his devoted pupil Arrian, this Penguin Classics edition—expertly translated and introduced by Robert Dobbin—brings the fiery, conversational brilliance of antiquity straight into the turbulence of the twenty-first century. 

At the very heart of the book lies the foundational dichotomy of control, the radical premise that most of our misery does not stem from external events, but from our faulty judgments about them. Epictetus draws a sharp, unyielding line between what is up to us—our desires, impulses, and aversions—and what is not—our bodies, property, reputations, and political fortunes. To confuse the two is to invite tyranny into your own mind. As the philosopher famously challenges us, with a dark, defiant humor that cuts across millennia: I must die. But must I die bawling? This single question encapsulates the Stoic ethos: we cannot dictate the script of our lives, but we have absolute sovereignty over how we play our part.

What makes *Discourses and Selected Writings* uniquely striking is its style. Unlike the dense, academic treatises of other philosophers, or the deeply private, nocturnal journaling of Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus is performative, punchy, and thoroughly alive. Reading this volume feels less like studying ancient text and more like sitting in a crowded lecture hall in Nicopolis, being publicly interrogated by a brilliant teacher who refuses to let you get away with your self-deception. He uses biting sarcasm, vivid metaphors, and direct address. He mocks our cowardice, dismantles our vanity, and strips away our pathetic attachment to temporary comforts. He is part therapist, part drill sergeant, and entirely devoted to the art of moral self-improvement.

Through the *Discourses*, the surviving *Fragments*, and the immortal *Enchiridion* (Manual), Epictetus tackles the raw, unfiltered realities of human existence. He addresses illness and fear, the bonds of family and friendship, the duties of citizenship, and the paradoxes of freedom and imprisonment. He reminds us that a man in chains who has mastered his passions is infinitely freer than a Caesar who trembles at the whisper of a conspiracy. 

In an era defined by endless anxiety, chronic distraction, and a cultural obsession with controlling things entirely out of our hands, Epictetus reads like an urgent, necessary alarm bell. He does not offer a path to getting what you want; he offers a path to wanting what you get. *Discourses and Selected Writings* is not a book to be read once and placed neatly on a shelf. It is a razor-sharp manual for living, a timeless antidote to modern fragility, and a masterclass in radical mental resilience.
