# Fifth Business (The Deptford Trilogy, #1)

**Author:** Robertson Davies  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1970

**A Masterclass in Memory, Myth, and the Weight of the Past: *Fifth Business* by Robertson Davies**

Robertson Davies’ *Fifth Business* is not merely a novel—it is a labyrinthine meditation on fate, the fragility of identity, and the way history lingers like a ghost in the present. Set against the backdrop of early 20th-century Canada, the story follows Paul Dempster, a man who, like the title suggests, is both the protagonist and an observer of his own life—a "fifth business" in the lives of others, a silent architect of destinies he neither seeks nor fully understands. What emerges is a work that blurs the line between realism and the supernatural, where the mundane and the mythic coexist in unsettling harmony.

At its core, *Fifth Business* is a novel about the power of memory and the illusions we weave to make sense of our lives. Paul Dempster, a retired schoolteacher, begins his narrative with the death of his friend, the philosopher Dunstan Ramsay, and the revelation that Ramsay’s life was inextricably tied to Paul’s own. Through flashbacks, Davies unspools a tapestry of small, seemingly insignificant moments—childhood pranks, a snowball fight, a card trick taught to a young boy—that ripple outward, altering lives in ways no one could have predicted. The novel’s genius lies in its suggestion that every action, no matter how trivial, carries weight, that the past is not a static record but a living force that shapes the present.

Davies’ prose is deceptively simple, yet rich with subtlety. His descriptions are precise, almost clinical at times, but beneath the surface lies a deeper current of melancholy and irony. The novel’s structure—nonlinear, fragmented, and deeply introspective—mirrors the unreliable nature of memory itself. Paul’s narration is both candid and self-deceptive, revealing how easily we convince ourselves of our own narratives. There is a haunting quality to his storytelling, as if he is piecing together a puzzle he never fully intended to solve.

The novel’s themes extend beyond personal memory to the broader question of history and myth. Ramsay, the philosopher, is obsessed with the idea of "the marvelous"—the belief that the extraordinary is not separate from the ordinary but woven into it. This idea is central to *Fifth Business*, which constantly teases the reader with the possibility that what we dismiss as coincidence may actually be fate, that the mundane is always pregnant with the supernatural. Davies never fully resolves this tension, leaving the reader to grapple with the unsettling idea that reality is far stranger than we dare to imagine.

The Deptford Trilogy, of which *Fifth Business* is the first installment, is often compared to the works of Henry James for its psychological depth and its exploration of the hidden currents of human behavior. But where James’s characters are often trapped in the complexities of their own minds, Davies’ characters are also deeply embedded in the social and historical fabric of their time. The novel’s setting—small-town Ontario, the aftermath of World War I, the rise of modernism—provides a rich backdrop against which Davies examines the ways in which individuals are shaped by their environments, their choices, and the accidents of fate.

What makes *Fifth Business* so compelling is its refusal to offer easy answers. Paul Dempster is neither a hero nor a villain, but a man caught in the crosscurrents of his own life. His story is one of quiet tragedy, where the weight of the past is carried like an invisible burden. Davies’ writing is both elegant and unflinching, never shying away from the darker implications of his themes. The novel’s title, *Fifth Business*, is a perfect metaphor for the way we are all, in some sense, extras in the lives of others—unseen, unacknowledged, yet inextricably bound to the stories we help to tell.

For readers who appreciate novels that are as much about the spaces between words as the words themselves, *Fifth Business* is a revelation. It is a book that lingers in the mind long after the final page, a testament to the power of storytelling to illuminate the shadows of our existence. Davies’ work is a reminder that the marvelous is not something that happens to us—it is something we create, and it is always already there, waiting to be discovered.
