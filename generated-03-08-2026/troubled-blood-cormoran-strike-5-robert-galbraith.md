# Troubled Blood (Cormoran Strike, #5)

**Author:** Robert Galbraith  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

**Troubled Blood: A Masterclass in Psychological Suspense and Unrelenting Tension**

Robert Galbraith’s *Troubled Blood*—the fifth installment in the Cormoran Strike series—is a tour de force of modern crime fiction, proving once again why this pen name (J.K. Rowling’s secret) has become a titan of the genre. Here, Galbraith weaves a labyrinthine cold case into a gripping, emotionally charged thriller that lingers long after the final page. At its heart, *Troubled Blood* is a meditation on memory, obsession, and the ways the past refuses to stay buried—both literally and metaphorically.

The novel opens with Strike, ever the reluctant hero, in Cornwall, a place that feels like a temporary retreat from the chaos of his London detective agency. But fate, as it often does in Galbraith’s world, intervenes when a woman named Charlotte Bamborough seeks his help in solving her mother’s decades-old disappearance. Margot Bamborough vanished in 1974, leaving behind a trail of cryptic clues—tarot cards, coded letters, and a network of suspects that includes a charismatic but dangerous surgeon, a reclusive artist, and a shadowy figure who may still be active. Strike, who has never tackled a cold case before, is drawn in by the mystery’s sheer audacity, but he soon realizes this is no ordinary disappearance. It’s a puzzle that could unravel into something far darker, something that might even be connected to a serial killer still at large.

What makes *Troubled Blood* so compelling is Galbraith’s ability to blend the meticulousness of a procedural with the raw, visceral dread of a psychological thriller. The case unfolds like a slow-burning fire, with each revelation stoking the tension higher. The author’s research is impeccable—whether it’s the medical details, the psychological profiles of suspects, or the historical context of the 1970s—yet it never feels like an info-dump. Instead, it’s seamlessly woven into the narrative, making the world feel vivid and lived-in. The tarot cards, for instance, aren’t just a red herring; they become a haunting motif, a symbol of fate’s cruel irony, as characters are drawn into their own doom through superstition and misplaced trust.

But *Troubled Blood* is more than just a cleverly constructed mystery. It’s a character study, particularly of Strike and Robin Ellacott, whose relationship remains one of the most nuanced and emotionally complex in contemporary fiction. Robin, ever the sharp-eyed and resourceful partner, is navigating her own turmoil—her divorce, her growing feelings for Strike, and the professional pressures of running the agency. Galbraith doesn’t shy away from depicting her vulnerability, and in doing so, he deepens the emotional stakes of the case. Their dynamic is a delicate balance of professional respect and simmering tension, and the way their personal lives intersect with the investigation adds layers of realism and heartache.

The villain, when he finally emerges, is one of Galbraith’s most chilling creations. A man who operates in the shadows, manipulating others with a mix of charm and brutality, he embodies the kind of evil that thrives on secrecy and control. The reveal is masterfully paced, with red herrings and false leads keeping the reader guessing until the very end. Even when the truth comes to light, Galbraith leaves room for ambiguity, a nod to the way real-life mysteries often defy neat resolutions.

Galbraith’s prose is as sharp and precise as ever. His descriptions are vivid without being florid, his dialogue crackles with authenticity, and his ability to build atmosphere is unmatched. Whether it’s the eerie quiet of a Cornish village or the claustrophobic tension of a London interrogation, he transports the reader into the story with an almost cinematic clarity. The pacing is flawless—never rushed, never dragging—ensuring that the suspense builds inexorably toward a climax that is both shocking and satisfying.

What sets *Troubled Blood* apart from other crime novels is its willingness to confront uncomfortable truths. Galbraith doesn’t shy away from exploring themes of class, power, and the ways institutions—medical, legal, and social—can enable abuse. The case forces Strike and Robin to confront their own biases and the limits of their influence, reinforcing the novel’s central idea: that some wounds never fully heal, and some secrets are too dangerous to uncover. Yet, there’s also a sense of catharsis in the resolution, a reminder that even in the darkest cases, there is justice to be found—if you’re willing to dig deep enough.

For fans of the series, *Troubled Blood* is a triumph, delivering all the hallmarks of Galbraith’s signature style—brilliant character work, meticulous plotting, and a villain who will haunt you long after the last page. But it’s also a standout entry on its own, a testament to why the Cormoran Strike series remains one of the most binge-worthy crime sagas of the 21st century. If you haven’t yet fallen under Galbraith’s spell, this is the perfect place to start—or return to.
