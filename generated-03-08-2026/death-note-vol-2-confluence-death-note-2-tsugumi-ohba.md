# Death Note, Vol. 2: Confluence (Death Note, #2)

**Author:** Tsugumi Ohba  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

**Death Note, Vol. 2: Confluence – A Masterclass in Psychological Warfare and Moral Ambiguity**

Tsugumi Ohba’s *Death Note, Vol. 2: Confluence* doesn’t just continue the story of Light Yagami—it deepens the psychological abyss he’s created for himself and the world around him. Where the first volume established Light as a genius with a god complex, *Confluence* refines his character into something far more unsettling: a man who believes he’s already won, even as the cracks in his carefully constructed empire begin to show. Ohba’s writing here is razor-sharp, blending cerebral strategy with visceral tension, all while maintaining the manga’s signature blend of philosophical depth and relentless pacing.

At its core, *Confluence* is a story about the illusion of control. Light’s initial triumph—eliminating the FBI agents investigating him—seems like a decisive victory, but it’s also the moment his arrogance becomes his greatest vulnerability. The introduction of the fiancée, a former Bureau agent with insider knowledge, forces Light to confront the fact that no matter how many names he writes in the Death Note, he’s still a pawn in a game he doesn’t fully understand. Meanwhile, L’s re-emergence as a direct adversary—now working alongside Light’s father—adds a layer of familial betrayal that turns the narrative into a high-stakes chess match between father and son, both of whom are playing for survival.

Ohba’s strength lies in his ability to make Light’s downfall feel inevitable without undermining his brilliance. The volume’s title, *Confluence*, is perfect—it’s the moment where Light’s path collides with the forces arrayed against him, and the reader is left wondering if he’ll adapt or unravel. The writing is precise, with dialogue that crackles with tension and action sequences that feel both methodical and explosive. There’s a cold efficiency to Light’s actions, but also a creeping paranoia that makes him unpredictable. His internal monologues reveal a man who is both a strategist and a narcissist, unable to separate his own desires from the greater consequences of his actions.

What makes *Confluence* particularly compelling is its exploration of morality in a vacuum. Light believes he’s doing what’s best for humanity by eliminating criminals, but the volume forces us to question whether his methods justify his ends. The introduction of new characters—like the fiancée and the task force—adds moral complexity, showing that even those who oppose Light are not without their own flaws. Ohba doesn’t shy away from ambiguity; there are no clear heroes or villains, only people making choices in the shadow of Light’s godlike power.

The art, as always, complements the narrative perfectly. Takeshi Obata’s illustrations capture the intensity of the story, from the stark contrasts of Light’s office to the chaotic energy of the task force’s investigations. The pacing is relentless, with each panel serving a purpose—whether it’s to build suspense, reveal a character’s thoughts, or escalate the conflict.

By the end of *Confluence*, Light’s position is more precarious than ever. The volume doesn’t just set up the next confrontation—it makes us question whether Light will ever truly be caught, or if he’ll simply adapt, evolving into something even more dangerous. Ohba’s writing here is a testament to the power of psychological thriller storytelling, where the real battle isn’t just between Light and his enemies, but within Light himself.

If you’ve followed *Death Note* from the beginning, *Confluence* delivers the next logical step in Light’s descent. For newcomers, it’s a perfect entry point into a story that’s as much about the mind as it is about the consequences of absolute power. Ohba’s ability to keep the tension high while deepening the themes of free will, justice, and human nature makes this volume a standout in the series.
