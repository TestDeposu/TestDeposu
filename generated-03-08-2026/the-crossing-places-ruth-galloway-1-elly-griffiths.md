# The Crossing Places (Ruth Galloway, #1)

**Author:** Elly Griffiths  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

**The Crossing Places: Where the Past Bleeds into the Present**

Elly Griffiths’ *The Crossing Places*—the debut in her acclaimed Ruth Galloway series—is a masterclass in blending forensic archaeology with psychological horror, a story that digs beneath the surface of a seemingly closed case to uncover something far more sinister. Set in the mist-shrouded, spirit-haunted landscapes of Norfolk, the novel follows Dr. Ruth Galloway, a sharp-witted but emotionally guarded archaeologist whose quiet life is shattered when a child’s bones wash ashore. The discovery isn’t just a routine investigation; it’s a personal reckoning, a collision between the past and the present that forces Ruth to confront the ghosts of her own history.

What makes *The Crossing Places* so compelling is Griffiths’ ability to weave together two seemingly unrelated threads—the cold case of Lucy Downey, a decade-old disappearance, and the sudden reappearance of another missing girl—into a chilling tapestry of obsession and ritual. Detective Chief Inspector Nelson, a man haunted by his failure to solve Lucy’s case, becomes Ruth’s reluctant ally, his frustration and grief palpable as he receives cryptic letters from the killer, each one dripping with biblical and Shakespearean references. The letters aren’t just taunts; they’re clues, a twisted puzzle that hints at a killer who sees himself as a modern-day priest, performing dark rites in the very land Ruth studies.

Griffiths’ prose is precise, immersive, and unflinching. She doesn’t shy away from the grim realities of forensic work—the stench of decay, the quiet devastation of loss—but she balances it with Ruth’s dry wit and sharp observations. The novel’s strength lies in its atmosphere: the Norfolk coast, with its shifting tides and ancient burial mounds, feels like a character itself, a place where the dead refuse to stay buried. The tension builds methodically, each new discovery unearthing something darker, until the reader is left breathless, wondering if Ruth’s own past—her estranged daughter, her complicated relationship with her mother—will become entangled in the killer’s game.

What sets Griffiths apart is her ability to make the past feel alive. The novel isn’t just about solving a murder; it’s about the weight of history, the way it lingers in the bones of the earth and the minds of those who study it. Ruth’s work as an archaeologist isn’t just a job—it’s a way of understanding the world, of making sense of the chaos. But when the present intrudes, forcing her to confront the living dead, she must decide how much of her own history she’s willing to risk.

The pacing is relentless, the mystery layered with psychological depth, and the characters—especially Ruth and Nelson—are richly drawn, their flaws and vulnerabilities making their bond feel earned. Griffiths doesn’t offer easy answers; instead, she invites the reader to sit with the unease, to question what we think we know about the past and the monsters that dwell in it.

*The Crossing Places* is more than a crime novel; it’s a meditation on memory, guilt, and the places where the dead refuse to stay buried. Griffiths’ writing is sharp, her storytelling immersive, and her ability to blend forensic detail with emotional depth makes this the perfect introduction to a series that continues to haunt and fascinate.
