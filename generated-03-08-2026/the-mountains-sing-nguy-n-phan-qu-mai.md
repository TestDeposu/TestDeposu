# The Mountains Sing

**Author:** Nguyễn Phan Quế Mai  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

The Mountains Sing by Nguyễn Phan Quế Mai is a breathtaking tapestry of war, memory, and the quiet, stubborn hope that sustains a family—and a nation—through decades of upheaval. Set against the tumultuous backdrop of the Việt Nam War and its aftermath, the novel follows the Trần family across three generations, weaving together the harrowing flight of Trần Diệu Lan during the violent Land Reform of the 1950s and the coming‑of‑age of her granddaughter, Hương, in Hà Nội as her loved ones disappear down the Hồ Chí Minh Trail. In doing so, Quế Mai accomplishes something rare: she renders the enormity of historical trauma intimate, allowing readers to feel the weight of loss not as a distant statistic but as the trembling heartbeat of a grandmother’s lullaby and the fierce whisper of a granddaughter’s resolve.

At its core, the book is an exploration of how violence reverberates through bloodlines, shaping identity, language, and the very notion of home. Yet Quế Mai refuses to let despair have the final word. Instead, she foregrounds kindness—small, everyday acts of generosity, the sharing of food, the preservation of songs—as the antidote to hatred. The narrative’s emotional engine is the interplay between sorrow and solace; each chapter that plunges into the horrors of bombings, forced migrations, or ideological purges is balanced by moments of tender solidarity: a neighbor’s hidden rice, a mother’s whispered prayer, a child’s drawing of a mountain that refuses to be erased. This duality gives the novel its rhythmic pulse, mirroring the ebb and flow of the Vietnamese landscape that both wounds and heals.

Stylistically, Nguyễn Phan Quế Mai brings the sensibility of a poet to prose, and the result is a language that sings. Her sentences are lyrical yet unflinching, often slipping into the cadence of traditional Vietnamese folk verses without ever sacrificing clarity. She peppers the text with Vietnamese phrases and idioms, not as exotic ornament but as organic threads that root the story in its cultural soil. The effect is immersive: readers hear the rustle of rice paddies, smell the incense of ancestral altars, and feel the humid press of Hanoi’s streets. This linguistic texture serves a dual purpose—it honors the voices that history has often silenced, and it creates a sensory bridge that makes the past palpably present.

The novel’s structure reinforces its thematic concerns. Alternating between Diệu Lan’s first‑person recollections and Hương’s present‑day perspective, Quế Mai creates a dialogue across time that underscores how memory is both inherited and reshaped. Diệu Lan’s recollections are raw, fragmented, and tinged with the trauma of displacement; Hương’s chapters, while still touched by war’s shadow, are marked by a restless curiosity and an emergent agency. Through this interplay, the author suggests that healing is not a linear process but a conversation—one that requires listening to the silences as much as to the spoken word.

Critically, The Mountains Sing avoids the pitfalls of grandiose battle‑centric war narratives. Instead of focusing on tactics or geopolitics, it zeroes in on the human cost: the loss of land, the erosion of language, the fracturing of kinship. Yet it never reduces its characters to mere victims. Diệu Lan is a matriarch of fierce resilience, whose decision to protect her children propels the family forward; Hương embodies a new generation’s quest to reconcile inherited pain with a desire for peace. Their journeys illustrate the author’s central thesis—that hope is not passive optimism but an active, often painful, choice to plant seeds in scorched earth.

Quế Mai’s debut novel in English arrives with the gravitas of a seasoned storyteller, yet it retains the fresh immediacy of a poet’s first utterance. The novel’s accolades—New York Times Editors’ Choice, Lannan Literary Awards Fellowship, praise from Viet Thanh Nguyen—are well‑earned, testifying to its ability to transcend genre boundaries and speak to universal themes while remaining firmly anchored in a specific historical moment. For readers who have been moved by the multigenerational sagas of Pachinko or Homegoing, The Mountains Sing offers a similarly expansive vision, but one distinguished by its poetic lyricism and an unyielding faith in the restorative power of compassion.

In a literary landscape often saturated with retellings of Western war experiences, Nguyễn Phan Quế Mai’s work is a necessary corrective—a reminder that the true cost of conflict is measured in the whispered lullabies of mothers, the stubborn growth of bamboo after a storm, and the enduring song of mountains that, though scarred, continue to stand.
