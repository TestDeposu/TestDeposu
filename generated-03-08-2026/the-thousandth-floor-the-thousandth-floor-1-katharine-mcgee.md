# The Thousandth Floor (The Thousandth Floor, #1)

**Author:** Katharine McGee  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

In "The Thousandth Floor," Katharine McGee masterfully crafts a dystopian world that is as captivating as it is unsettling. Set in the year 2118, the novel takes place in a futuristic Manhattan, where a thousand-story tower stands as a symbol of innovation and dreams. However, beneath its glittering facade, the tower is home to a complex web of secrets, lies, and addictions that threaten to destroy the lives of its privileged inhabitants.

Through the eyes of five teenagers, each with their own unique voice and perspective, McGee weaves a narrative that is both a mystery and a commentary on the human condition. The story centers around the death of a girl on the thousandth floor, and as the investigation unfolds, the characters' dark secrets and lies begin to surface. Leda's addiction, Eris's family drama, Rylin's newfound romance, Watt's espionage, and Avery's genetic perfection all serve as threads in a rich tapestry that explores the consequences of ambition and the corrupting influence of power.

McGee's writing style is reminiscent of a masterful puppeteer, expertly manipulating the strings of her characters' lives to create a sense of tension and suspense that propels the reader forward. Her prose is engaging and accessible, making it easy to become fully immersed in the world she has created. The characters, while flawed and often unlikable, are multidimensional and relatable, making it impossible not to become invested in their stories.

One of the most striking aspects of "The Thousandth Floor" is its exploration of the human condition. McGee raises important questions about the nature of perfection, the dangers of unchecked ambition, and the consequences of playing with fire when it comes to technology and power. The novel is a commentary on the societal pressures that drive individuals to pursue their goals at any cost, often sacrificing their relationships, their integrity, and even their sanity in the process.

The futuristic world McGee has created is equally impressive, with its advanced technology and luxurious skyscrapers serving as a backdrop for the characters' dramas. The tower itself becomes a character, its grandeur and majesty hiding the dark secrets and lies that lurk within its walls.

In conclusion, "The Thousandth Floor" is a gripping and thought-provoking novel that will appeal to fans of science fiction, mystery, and young adult fiction. McGee's writing style is engaging and suspenseful, and her characters are complex and relatable. The novel's exploration of the human condition and its commentary on the dangers of unchecked ambition make it a must-read for anyone looking for a compelling and thought-provoking story.
