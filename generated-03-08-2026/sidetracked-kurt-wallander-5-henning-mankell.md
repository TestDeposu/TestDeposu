# Sidetracked (Kurt Wallander, #5)

**Author:** Henning Mankell  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1999

Henning Mankell’s *Sidetracked* thrusts Inspector Kurt Wallander into a maelstrom of violence that feels both intensely personal and starkly emblematic of a society unraveling at the seams. The novel opens with two shocking spectacles—a young woman’s self‑immolation in a field and the savage murder of a former minister of justice—events that, on the surface, share nothing except their grotesque brutality. Yet Mankell wastes no time in revealing the thin, corroded threads that bind them: a killer who scalps his victims, a web of secrets stretching from the corridors of power to the shadowy world of art dealing, and a restless, almost vengeful fury that seems to spring from the very rot at the heart of contemporary Sweden.

What makes *Sidetracked* stand out in the Wallander canon is not merely its intricate puzzle‑box plotting, though the mystery is undeniably deft, but the way Mankell uses the crime as a lens to scrutinize the moral decay lurking beneath Sweden’s polished façade. The former minister, once a guardian of the law, becomes a victim of the very corruption he once fought; the art dealer, a purveyor of beauty, is implicated in a trade that commodifies pain; the petty thief, a mere cog in the machine, is dragged into a saga that exposes how easily innocence can be co‑opted by systemic malaise. The scalping motif—a visceral, almost ritualistic signature—serves as a grotesque metaphor for the stripping away of identity, dignity, and truth in a world where institutions no longer protect but prey.

Wallander himself is the novel’s moral compass, a weary yet relentless figure whose personal life mirrors the external chaos. His long‑anticipated vacation, a brief promise of normalcy, is repeatedly sabotaged by the case, forcing him to confront not only the killer’s mind but also his own doubts about justice, family, and the possibility of redemption. Mankell renders Wallander’s inner turmoil with a quiet, almost lyrical precision: the ache of a failed marriage, the strain of fatherhood, the creeping sense that his efforts may be futile. This psychological depth elevates the procedural beyond a mere chase; it becomes a study of how individuals endure when the world around them seems to be sidetracked by greed, indifference, and moral compromise.

Mankell’s prose is spare yet evocative, favoring crisp, declarative sentences that mirror the Scandinavian landscape—cold, clear, and occasionally pierced by sudden, shocking flashes of color. He avoids ornamental flourishes, letting the starkness of the setting and the gravity of the events speak for themselves. Dialogue is terse, often laden with subtext, and the narrative shifts fluidly between Wallander’s meticulous police work and the broader societal critique, never allowing one to overwhelm the other. The pacing is deliberate; moments of tension are interspersed with reflective pauses that let the reader feel the weight of each revelation, making the eventual climax hit with a resonant, unsettling force.

Critics have rightly praised *Sidetracked* as a “seven‑course banquet” for aficionados of the genre, and the accolade is well earned. The novel satisfies the craving for a tightly plotted whodunit while simultaneously offering a rich, layered commentary on the disintegration of familial bonds, the exploitation of women, and the corrosive influence of power. Mankell does not shy away from depicting the uncomfortable truths of modern life; instead, he invites the reader to sit with them, to feel the discomfort, and to consider what it means to uphold justice when the very foundations of law appear compromised.

In sum, *Sidetracked* is a masterful blend of classic police procedural and incisive social critique. It showcases Mankell at his most assured—balancing the gruesome details of a scalping murderer with a profound empathy for his protagonist and a clear‑eyed vision of a society teetering on the edge. For readers who crave crime fiction that challenges both the intellect and the conscience, this fifth installment in the Wallander series is an essential, unforgettable read.
