# Murder Must Advertise (Lord Peter Wimsey, #10)

**Author:** Dorothy L. Sayers  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

Murder Must Advertise is a brilliantly twisted puzzle that marries the glitter of 1930s London’s advertising world with the grim undercurrents of vice and murder. When Victor Dean tumbles down the stairs of Pym’s Publicity, the scene at first reads like a clumsy mishap, but Lord Peter Wimsey’s razor‑sharp eye soon spots the inconsistencies that turn a simple fall into a web of blackmail, cocaine, and illicit liaisons. Sayers uses the advertising agency not merely as a backdrop but as a character in its own right—a place where slogans are crafted to sell products, yet the same persuasive language is turned inward to conceal deadly secrets. The novel’s central theme, therefore, is the power of language: how copy can both reveal and obscure truth, how the art of persuasion can be weaponized for both commerce and crime.

Sayers’ style shines in this installment with her trademark blend of erudition and wit. Her prose is lively, peppered with period‑appropriate slang and a keen eye for the absurdities of the ad trade—think jingles that double as alibis and copywriters who moonlight as informants. Yet beneath the sparkling dialogue lies a meticulously constructed plot. Each murder is presented as a “fixed fee” for advertising a deadly secret, a macabre twist that turns the mercenary nature of the industry into a literal ledger of blood. Wimsey’s methodical deduction, combined with his aristocratic charm and occasional bouts of melancholy, provides a steady moral compass amid the murk. The detective’s interactions with the agency’s eclectic staff—ranging from the harried copy chief to the temptresses who flirt with danger—allow Sayers to explore class tensions, gender dynamics, and the moral ambiguity of a profession built on manipulation.

What truly distinguishes Murder Must Advertise from other Golden Age mysteries is Sayers’ willingness to let the setting dictate the tone. The buzzing offices, the clatter of typewriters, the scent of ink and tobacco—all become atmospheric cues that heighten suspense. Rather than relying solely on gothic tropes or country‑house clichés, she grounds the horror in the mundane, making the revelation that a seemingly innocuous advertisement could signal a contract for murder all the more shocking. The novel also offers a subtle commentary on the era’s burgeoning consumer culture: the promise of happiness sold through slick slogans masks a darker reality where desire can be exploited for profit—or for murder.

At its core, the book asks readers to consider who really controls the narrative. Is it the copywriter who crafts the message, the client who pays for it, or the consumer who buys into it? Wimsey’s investigation peels back layers of deception to reveal that, in the world of advertising, the most dangerous product is often the secret being sold.

In sum, Murder Must Advertise is a compelling blend of smart detective work, period charm, and a sharp, satirical look at the power of words. Sayers proves once again that a mystery can be as much about the society that produces it as about the crime itself.
