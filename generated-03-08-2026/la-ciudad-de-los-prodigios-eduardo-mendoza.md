# La ciudad de los prodigios

**Author:** Eduardo Mendoza  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

La ciudad de los prodigios, originally Eduardo Mendoza’s exuberant picaresque masterpiece, returns in 2020 as a vivid graphic novel illustrated by Claudio Stassi. Set between Barcelona’s two Universal Expositions of 1888 and 1929, the story follows Onofre Bouvila—a penniless immigrant who hawks anarchist flyers and miracle hair‑growth tonics—on his dizzying climb from street‑level hustler to titan of finance and crime. Mendoza’s original novel is already a carnival of satire, myth‑making, and social observation; Stassi’s adaptation translates that carnival into ink and color, preserving the narrative’s restless energy while giving readers a new way to wander the city’s labyrinthine alleys.

At its heart, the work interrogates the promise and peril of upward mobility in a rapidly modernizing metropolis. Onofre’s trajectory is less a straight line than a series of grotesque vignettes: each scheme, each alliance, each betrayal reveals how personal ambition intertwines with the city’s own myth‑making. Barcelona itself becomes a character—a bustling, contradictory stage where grand expositions showcase technological marvels while the underbelly thrives on smuggling, anarchist agitation, and outright fraud. Mendoza’s narrative treats these extremes not as opposing forces but as two sides of the same coin, suggesting that the spectacular progress celebrated at the world’s fairs is built upon the very same opportunism and deceit that fuels Onofre’s rise. The graphic format heightens this tension: Stassi’s panels juxtapose the gleaming façades of the 1888 and 1929 pavilions with the cramped, shadow‑filled workshops where Onofre concocts his “crecepelo” and plots his next move. The visual contrast forces the reader to feel the city’s duality rather than merely read about it.

Mendoza’s style, already noted for its playful erudition and picaresque verve, shines through in the dialogue and narration that accompany Stassi’s artwork. He employs a lush, almost baroque prose that revels in digression—detailing the peculiarities of a street vendor’s cart, the eccentricities of an anarchist pamphlet, the ostentatious luxury of a newly minted financier—all while maintaining a razor‑sharp satirical edge. The humor is both affectionate and merciless; Mendoza laughs at Onofre’s gullibility and at the gullibility of those who profit from him, exposing the absurdity of systems that reward chicanery as readily as ingenuity. In the graphic adaptation, this tonal balance is preserved through Stassi’s expressive line work: characters are rendered with exaggerated, almost caricatured features that highlight their moral ambiguities, yet the backgrounds are meticulously researched, inviting the reader to linger over period‑accurate architecture, fashion, and street life.

One of the most striking achievements of this edition is how it transforms Mendoza’s expansive, episodic novel into a tightly paced visual saga without sacrificing the author’s love for digression. Stassi wisely chooses key episodes—Onofre’s first encounter with the anarchist distributor, his ill‑fated partnership with a counterfeit‑hair‑tonic magnate, his eventual entanglement with a corrupt banking consortium—to serve as narrative anchors. Between these milestones, filler panels provide atmospheric vignettes: a bustling market, a protest rally, the glittering inauguration of the 1929 Expo. These interludes act as breathing spaces, letting the reader absorb the texture of Mendoza’s Barcelona while the main plot surges forward. The result is a rhythm that feels both episodic and cumulative, mirroring the picaresque tradition where each adventure contributes to a larger portrait of societal flux.

Critically, the adaptation does face the inevitable challenge of condensing a work renowned for its digressive richness. Some secondary characters and subplots—particularly those that delve into the cultural folklore of Catalonia—receive less page time than they merit in the original. Readers unfamiliar with Mendoza’s novel might miss the subtle references to local legends that enrich the text’s tapestry. However, Stassi’s visual storytelling often compensates for these omissions; a single panel depicting a traditional gegants (giant) parade, for instance, can convey the festive spirit that a paragraph of prose might elaborate. In this way, the graphic novel serves not as a replacement but as an invitation: it entices newcomers to seek out the fuller text while rewarding longtime fans with a fresh, visually immersive perspective.

Ultimately, La ciudad de los prodigios in its graphic form succeeds as a vivid meditation on how myth, ambition, and urban transformation intertwine. Mendoza’s satire remains biting, his affection for Barcelona’s quirks undiminished, and Stassi’s artwork provides a kinetic canvas that lets the city’s contradictions pulse on the page. The work reminds us that the line between legend and opportunism is often drawn in the same ink that sketches a city’s skyline—both are products of the same restless human imagination.
