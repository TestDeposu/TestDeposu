# This Thing of Darkness

**Author:** Harry Thompson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

"This Thing of Darkness" by Harry Thompson is a sweeping historical epic that delves into the very fabric of our modern world, weaving an intricate tapestry of exploration, discovery, and the irreconcilable tensions that arise when humanity's grasp for knowledge clashes with ancient traditions. As the author deftly navigates the high seas and the uncharted territories of the human heart, we are brought face to face with one of history's most pivotal friendships: that between Robert FitzRoy and Charles Darwin.

Thompson's masterful storytelling plunges the reader into the tempestuous world of the HMS Beagle, a vessel that bore the brunt of the 19th century's tempests - both literal and metaphorical. It is here, in the unforgiving expanse of the ocean, that FitzRoy and Darwin forge an unbreakable bond, their debates and discoveries shaping the very contours of our understanding of the world and our place within it. As we embark upon this literary journey, Thompson's prose skillfully excavates the intricate web of moral and philosophical convictions that undergird the lives of these two men, and the historical epoch in which they lived.

One of the striking aspects of Thompson's style is his capacity to breathe life into his characters, imbuing them with a depth and complexity that is rarely found in historical fiction. FitzRoy, the stalwart Christian Tory, and Darwin, the liberal naturalist, are more than mere puppets on the stage of history; they are multidimensional beings, driven by a rich inner world of conviction, passion, and doubt. As their friendship evolves, we see them struggle to reconcile their fundamental differences, their conversations and disagreements providing a compelling backdrop to the author's nuanced exploration of the tensions between tradition and progress, faith and science.

Thompson's narrative is, in turn, undergirded by a richly detailed recreation of 19th-century life on the high seas. From the grueling hardships faced by the crew of the HMS Beagle to the breathtaking beauty of the landscapes they encountered, the author provides a visceral and immersive portrait of a bygone era, one that is both captivating and unflinching in its portrayal of human frailty and resilience.

If there is a criticism to be leveled at Thompson's work, it is that the novel's focus on the relationship between FitzRoy and Darwin occasionally threatens to overshadow the larger historical canvas upon which they moved. The book's three volumes, which span the globe from the southern tip of South America to the Galapagos Islands, are meticulously researched and meticulously conveyed, but at times, one cannot help but feel that the sheer breadth of the narrative sometimes results in a certain fragmentation of the narrative's overall cohesion.

That being said, such is the power of Thompson's storytelling, and the richness of his characters, that even the most egregious errors or lacunae in the narrative are readily forgiven. And so, as we navigate the treacherous waters of "This Thing of Darkness," we find ourselves drawn into a world of seafaring adventure, scientific discovery, and philosophical introspection, one that is both deeply rooted in the past and hauntingly relevant to our own era.

In short, "This Thing of Darkness" is a masterpiece, a testament to the human capacity for exploration, discovery, and the boundless complexity of the human heart. With its richly detailed recreation of 19th-century life on the high seas, its nuanced portrayal of the tensions between tradition and progress, and its multidimensional characters, Thompson's novel is an absolute must-read for anyone with an interest in history, science, and the enduring power of the human spirit.
