# Something Rotten (Thursday Next, #4)

**Author:** Jasper Fforde  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2004

In Jasper Fforde's "Something Rotten," the fourth installment in the Thursday Next series, the esteemed detective returns to our world from the realms of Jurisfiction, armed with the dithery Danish prince Hamlet. This is a bold move, as it sets the stage for an exploration of the intricate relationships between fiction and reality. Fforde masterfully weaves together the boundaries of these two worlds, immersing us in his unique vision of a literature-obsessed England.

Fforde's signature wit and inventive spirit are on full display as we follow Thursday's attempts to navigate the complexities of her return to reality. Her responsibilities as a SpecOps agent, coupled with her duties as a parent, make for a delightful balancing act. Amidst the mayhem of her life, Thursday must confront the sinister forces of outlaw fictioneer Yorrick Kaine, who seeks to exploit the fragile equilibrium between fiction and reality.

The author's prose is characterized by its playful irreverence, and Fforde is unafraid to subvert our expectations and play with the notion of literary classics as living, breathing entities. Hamlet's arrival on the scene adds a new layer of complexity to the narrative, and Fforde cleverly uses the prince's presence to explore themes of identity, free will, and the power of storytelling.

Throughout the novel, Fforde's affection for literature is palpable, and the reader is treated to a series of clever references and cameos from some of literature's most iconic figures. Fforde's affection for the absurd and the surreal also makes an appearance, and he does not shy away from tackling the more esoteric corners of literary theory and criticism.

If there is one criticism to be levied, it is that Fforde's pacing can be occasionally uneven, and the narrative threatens to get mired in the intricacies of his alternate reality. However, this is a minor quibble, and the author's clever dialogue and engaging characters usually manage to keep the narrative on track.

As is the case with Fforde's previous works, "Something Rotten" is a delightfully entertaining romp through the worlds of literature and imagination. With its witty dialogue, inventive plot twists, and memorable characters, this novel is a must-read for fans of the series and newcomers alike.

Ultimately, Fforde's unique voice and style prove to be the perfect blend of intellectual curiosity and literary ingenuity. "Something Rotten" is a testament to the power of storytelling to reshape our understanding of the world and our place within it.
