# Days of Air and Darkness (Deverry, #8; The Westlands, #4)

**Author:** Katharine Kerr  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1994

Days of Air and Darkness, the eighth installment in Katharine Kerr’s Deverry saga and the fourth of the Westlands sub‑series, thrusts readers back into a world where the veil between the mortal and the astral is thin enough for gods to vie over the destiny of a single child. The besieged city of Cengarn becomes a crucible: armies of flesh and spirit converge, the goddess Alshandra mobilizes her priestesses to thwart a prophesied birth, and Jill, the dweomermaster sworn to protect Princess Carra, must wield both spell and sword to safeguard a future already written in the stars. Meanwhile, Rhodry’s dragon‑borne flight toward the fray promises a clash that will test not only his mettle but the very fabric of fate that has guided Deverry for generations.

At its core, the novel explores the tension between destiny and agency—a theme Kerr has nurtured since the first volume. The prophecy that haunts Carra’s unborn child is not a static decree but a living current, shaped by the choices of those who seek to either fulfill or subvert it. Kerr treats fate as a river: it can be diverted, dammed, or overflowed, but never wholly erased. This fluid conception allows her to examine how belief, fear, and love intersect with cosmic forces, turning what could be a deterministic plot into a meditation on responsibility. The astral siege mirrors the internal struggles of her protagonists: Jill’s doubts about her own power, Rhodry’s lingering guilt over past betrayals, and Raena’s twisted devotion to a goddess who demands absolute obedience. By grounding the epic stakes in personal turmoil, Kerr ensures that the magical spectacle never eclipses the human heart of the story.

Kerr’s style remains a hallmark of immersive, lyrical fantasy. Her prose is rich without being ostentatious, weaving Celtic‑inspired cadences into a tapestry that feels both ancient and immediate. She excels at layered world‑building: the astral planes are rendered with the same tactile detail as the stone‑cobbled streets of Cernarn, making the supernatural feel as real as the clang of steel. Dialogue is crisp, often laced with proverbial wisdom that hints at the deeper mythic currents running beneath the surface action. Moreover, Kerr handles multiple viewpoints with deft precision, shifting from Jill’s introspective magic‑craft to Rhodry’s visceral dragon‑riding adrenaline, and then to Raena’s chilling, fanatical resolve. Each perspective enriches the thematic dialogue, showing how the same event can be perceived as salvation, damnation, or inevitable turning point depending on one’s allegiance and inner scars.

What distinguishes Days of Air and Darkness from its predecessors is the heightened sense of immediacy. The siege is not a backdrop but a living, breathing entity that presses on every character, forcing rapid decisions and exposing fault lines in alliances that have been cultivated over earlier books. Kerr’s pacing mirrors this pressure: scenes of quiet magical study are punctuated by sudden, brutal skirmishes, and the narrative never allows the reader to settle into complacency. The result is a novel that feels both a continuation of a beloved saga and a stand‑alone epic in its own right—accessible to newcomers who will be drawn in by the vivid conflict, yet rewarding for long‑time fans who will appreciate the subtle callbacks to earlier prophecies and character arcs.

One minor critique lies in the occasional density of exposition concerning the astral mechanics. Kerr’s ambition to delineate the rules of her cosmology sometimes slows the momentum, especially in passages where Jill explains the nuances of spell‑weaving amidst battle. While these details enrich the lore, they can momentarily distract from the visceral urgency of the siege. Nevertheless, such moments are infrequent and often serve to deepen the reader’s investment in the magical system that underpins the world’s conflict.

In sum, Days of Air and Darkness delivers a potent blend of mythic grandeur and intimate character study, anchored by Kerr’s elegant prose and her enduring fascination with how mortals navigate the currents of fate. It is a testament to her ability to evolve a long‑running saga without losing the soul that first captivated readers—a tale where magic, war, and destiny collide beneath a sky that seems both endless and intimately close.
