# Dark of the Moon (Kencyrath, #2)

**Author:** P.C. Hodgell  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

P.C. Hodgell’s Dark of the Moon, the eagerly anticipated second installment in the sprawling Kencyrath epic, proves that fantasy can be both fiercely savage and deeply intimate. Picking up the smoldering threads of God Stalk, Hodgell plunges her readers back into a universe where geography is perilous, history is a physical weight, and divine destiny feels less like a blessing and more like a terminal sentence. If the first book was an intoxicating, chaotic plunge into the city of Tai-tastigon, Dark of the Moon is the breathless, high-altitude scramble across jagged mountain passes—a journey that tests the literal and spiritual endurance of its unforgettable protagonists.

At the heart of the novel lies a masterfully handled theme of fractured identity and the inescapable gravity of the past. For Jamie, whose memory is a blank slate of dark and horrid years spent in the clutches of the traitorous house of Gerridon, survival means wrestling with ghosts she cannot name. Hodgell brilliantly explores how memory forms the architecture of the self. As hints of Jamie’s forgotten trauma claw their way to the surface, she is hunted not merely by physical assassins and the monstrous changers of her family's dark lineage, but by the very concept of who she used to be. Every step forward is a confrontation with a legacy of betrayal, forcing Jamie to navigate a razor’s edge between reclaiming her history and being consumed by it.

Meanwhile, her twin brother Tori anchors the narrative's other axis as the precarious Highlord of the Kencyrath. Where Jamie is visceral, impulsive, and wild, Tori must navigate the suffocating corridors of politics, holding together a volatile coalition of wayward lords. Hodgell crafts Tori’s chapters with a tense, psychological claustrophobia. He is a compromise leader walking a tightrope over an abyss, forced to muster troops while knowing that power inevitably draws the very nightmares and assassins he is trying to outrun. The duality between the siblings—one fighting for physical survival in the wild, the other battling for political and mental equilibrium in the halls of power—creates a dynamic narrative rhythm that never allows the reader to catch their breath.

Hodgell’s literary style is a rare hybrid of dense, world-building richness and razor-sharp character work. Her prose is unapologetically complex, refusing to hold the reader’s hand as it introduces ancient feuds, catlike Arrin-ken, and the looming, existential threat of Perimal Darkling. Yet, for all its epic scope, the novel succeeds because of its piercing focus on internal friction. Hodgell writes characters who are remarkably hard on themselves and everyone around them. Jamie and Tori are not glossy, easily digestible heroes; they are volatile, scarred, and fiercely defensive, learning the heavy cost of their actions in real-time. Their bond—straddling love, fear, and a desperate, telepathic longing—serves as the emotional anchor of a world tearing itself apart at the seams.

Dark of the Moon is a striking testament to Hodgell’s distinct voice in high fantasy. It moves away from tired genre tropes in favor of a gritty, mythic realism where divine mandates are burdensome and survival requires a ferocious, unyielding will. For fans of intricate world-building, high-stakes political maneuvering, and complex protagonists who refuse to break under unimaginable pressure, this novel is an absolute triumph. It challenges, it dazzles, and it leaves an indelible mark long after the final page is turned.
