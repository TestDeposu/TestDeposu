# Labyrinth: A Novel Based on the Jim Henson Film

**Author:** A.C.H. Smith  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

Step into the twilight realm where dreams and childhood anxieties collide. A.C.H. Smith’s novelization of Jim Henson’s cinematic masterpiece, Labyrinth, is far more than a mere page-by-page translation of a cult-classic film. Published in 2014 as an enhanced collector's edition, this book breathes fresh literary life into the eerie, glittering world of the Goblin King, while serving as a poignant artifact of dark fantasy. At its core, the novel explores the timeless theme of the precarious bridge between childhood and adulthood—a psychological threshold that must be crossed not by abandoning imagination, but by confronting the intricate mazes of our own fears and desires.

Smith’s writing style is a masterclass in atmospheric translation. He captures the tactile, slightly decaying grandeur of Henson’s practical-effects universe through prose that is simultaneously whimsical and menacing. Where the film relies on visual spectacle and David Bowie’s magnetic, androgynous menace, Smith’s narrative leans inward, giving voice to Sarah Williams’ inner turmoil. Her descent into the labyrinth is rendered as an internal coming-of-age odyssey. The shifting corridors, the talking walls, and the treacherous Bog of Eternal Stench are not just physical obstacles, but manifestations of adolescent insecurity, pride, and the bittersweet abandonment of childish things. Smith’s prose dances gracefully between fairy-tale cadence and modern psychological insight, making the fantastical encounters with Hoggle, Ludo, and Sir Didymus feel both delightfully bizarre and profoundly grounded.

What elevates this 2014 edition from a standard tie-in to an essential literary artifact, however, is its profound contextual framing. This volume bridges the gap between literary narrative and artistic genesis. Readers are treated to unpublished goblin illustrations by the legendary Brian Froud, whose tactile, mossy, and delightfully grotesque concept art visually anchors Smith’s textual descriptions. Furthermore, the inclusion of fifty never-before-seen pages from Jim Henson’s personal journals offers a rare, intimate peek into the visionary director’s creative process. Witnessing the raw, initial conception of Labyrinth—scrawled notes, early character concepts, and philosophical musings on the nature of control and surrender—adds a haunting meta-narrative layer to the reading experience. 

Ultimately, A.C.H. Smith’s Labyrinth succeeds because it respects the intelligence of its audience. It refuses to sanitize the shadows of Henson’s imagination, opting instead to illuminate them with rich prose and rare archival treasures. For lifelong fans of the film, this book is a nostalgic pilgrimage; for newcomers, it is an invitation into a brilliantly structured psychological wonderland. It reminds us that while the labyrinth may be full of trickery, the ultimate power—the power of will, imagination, and self-discovery—remains entirely within ourselves.
