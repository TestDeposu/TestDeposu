# The Hour of the Gate (Spellsinger, #2)

**Author:** Alan Dean Foster  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Stepping back into the whimsical, unpredictable realm of Alan Dean Foster’s Spellsinger series feels much like tuning a thoroughly battered guitar: you expect a few discordant notes, but you stay entirely for the rhythm. The Hour of the Gate, the second installment in this cult-classic portal fantasy, takes the delightfully absurd premise established in its predecessor and stretches it across a broader, more perilous canvas. Published initially in the early eighties and retaining its vibrant retro charm in subsequent editions, this novel solidifies Foster’s distinct literary signature—a seamless blend of high-stakes epic fantasy and self-aware, pop-culture-infused comedy.

At the heart of the narrative lies a deeply resonant theme of displacement and adaptation. Our reluctant protagonist, Jon-Tom, is an ordinary American college student marooned in a world populated by intelligent, talking animals, profound magic, and towering geographical threats. While many portal fantasies utilize their alternate realities merely as scenic backdrops, Foster uses this dislocation to explore how identity shifts when everything familiar is stripped away. Jon-Tom does not yearn for grand heroism; he yearns for the mundane comfort of a dorm room and the predictable hum of everyday life. Yet, it is precisely his outsider status—and his bizarre magical instrument, which translates musical chords into literal spells—that makes him indispensable. Foster plays with the motif of music as a universal language, elevating it here to a literal force of creation and chaos. Jon-Tom's inability to fully control his musical magic serves as a wonderful metaphor for youth itself: powerful, unpredictable, and prone to explosive misfires.

Stylistically, Foster is in peak form, showcasing the breezy, conversational prose that made him a staple of speculative fiction. Where other fantasy authors bog their worlds down in exhaustive, impenetrable lore, Foster keeps the pacing brisk and the tone refreshingly lighthearted. He possesses a rare talent for balancing impending doom with disarming humor. The looming menace of the Plated Folk—a terrifying, insectoid army led by a ruthless queen bent on the eradication of all warm-blooded mammals—creates genuine narrative tension. Yet, this darkness is brilliantly counterbalanced by the picaresque nature of Jon-Tom's journey. 

The author’s true genius shines in his character chemistry and world-building through dialogue. Jon-Tom is far from a solitary wanderer; he is saddled with a fellowship that gleefully subverts traditional fantasy tropes. There is Clothahump, the ancient, cantankerous wizarding turtle whose wisdom is often overshadowed by his sheer exhaustion; Mudge, an irrepressible otter endowed with a penchant for schemes and a delightfully filthy mind; and Robert, a chronically cowardly bat whose neuroses provide constant comic relief. This motley crew transforms what could have been a standard trek across uncharted mountains and rivers into a masterclass in comedic character dynamics. Foster writes dialogue with a snappy, rhythmic cadence that makes every banter-filled campfire scene just as engaging as the high-stakes skirmishes against the insect hordes.

The Hour of the Gate does not reinvent the wheel of epic fantasy, nor does it pretend to. Instead, it offers a loving, sharply witty homage to the genre while keeping the emotional stakes surprisingly grounded. It is a story about finding purpose in the most absurd of circumstances, proving that sometimes, saving two worlds requires little more than a mismatched band of travelers, an impossible journey, and a hopelessly out-of-tune musician. For readers seeking an imaginative escape that refuses to take itself too seriously while still delivering genuine heart and adventure, Foster’s sophomore Spellsinger novel remains an absolute joy to read.
