# House of Blades (Traveler's Gate, #1)

**Author:** Will Wight  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

**House of Blades: A Dagger’s Edge into a World of Blood and Betrayal**

Will Wight’s *House of Blades* is not a book you read—it’s a wound you open, a blade you draw, and a world you step into with your boots already caked in blood. This is the first installment in the *Traveler’s Gate* series, a dark, visceral fantasy that blends the brutal efficiency of *Game of Thrones* with the psychological depth of *The Witcher*, all while dripping with the kind of morally gray storytelling that makes you question whether the real monsters are the ones with fangs or the ones with crowns.

At its core, *House of Blades* is a tale of survival in a world where loyalty is a currency and trust is a liability. The story unfolds through the eyes of **Kael**, a mercenary with a past as bloody as his present, and **Veyra**, a noblewoman whose family’s downfall has left her with nothing but a dagger and a thirst for vengeance. Their paths collide in a city where the streets run red with more than just rain, and every shadow hides a knife waiting to be drawn. Wight doesn’t shy away from the grimy, gory reality of power—political intrigue here isn’t played out in grand halls with wine and whispers; it’s a game of chess where the pieces are bodies, and the board is littered with corpses.

What makes *House of Blades* stand out isn’t just its relentless pacing or its vivid, almost tactile world-building, but Wight’s unflinching style. His prose is sharp as a blade, each sentence a strike that either cuts deep or leaves a bruise. There’s no fat here—no unnecessary flourishes, no softening of the blows. The dialogue crackles with the tension of a room full of assassins, and the action unfolds like a nightmare you can’t wake from. Wight has a knack for making even the most brutal moments feel earned, whether it’s a fight scene where every punch is a life or a political maneuver where every word is a blade.

The world itself is a masterclass in atmospheric dread. The city of **Veythar** is a labyrinth of spies, backstabbers, and warring factions, where the air smells of iron and the streets are slick with betrayal. Magic isn’t some ethereal force—it’s a tool, a weapon, a last resort when bullets and blades fail. And the characters? They’re not heroes or villains; they’re people who’ve been broken by the world, and now they’re breaking it back. Kael is a man who’s seen too much and feels too little, while Veyra is a woman who’s been taught to hate but is learning to love the chaos of survival. Their dynamic is electric, a mix of reluctant partnership and simmering tension that keeps you guessing whether they’ll end up as allies or adversaries.

What *House of Blades* lacks in traditional fantasy tropes, it makes up for in raw, uncompromising storytelling. There’s no grand prophecy to chase, no chosen one to save the world—just a group of people fighting to stay alive in a world that wants them dead. It’s a refreshing departure from the usual fantasy fare, a book that doesn’t sugarcoat the cost of power or the price of survival.

If you’ve ever wanted a fantasy novel that feels like a punch to the gut—one that doesn’t just entertain but haunts you long after the last page—then *House of Blades* is your blade. Will Wight doesn’t just write stories; he carves them into your memory, leaving scars that linger long after you’ve turned the final page.
