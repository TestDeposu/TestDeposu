# The Warlock in Spite of Himself (Warlock, #1)

**Author:** Christopher Stasheff  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1969

When Christopher Stasheff published *The Warlock in Spite of Himself* in 1969, the landscape of science fiction was undergoing a seismic shift, dominated by the gritty, sociological world-building of the New Wave. Yet, Stasheff chose a different path, effortlessly wedding the high-tech tropes of space exploration with the whimsical tropes of Arthurian fantasy. The result is a delightful genre mashup that feels both timeless and charmingly retro, anchoring its narrative in a clever exploration of perception versus reality.

At the heart of the novel lies a compelling theme: the friction between rigid scientific materialism and the malleable nature of human belief. Protagonist Rod Gallowglass is an operative for SCENT, a galactic agency dedicated to sheeding lost colonies toward democracy. Armed with a staunchly cynical worldview, Rod lands on Gramarye—a planet teeming with witches, warlocks, elves, and monsters. Stasheff brilliantly uses Rod’s steadfast refusal to believe in magic as both a comedic device and a philosophical anchor. Rod insists his energy beams are spells, his anti-gravity field is levitation, and his faithful, epileptic robot horse, Fess, is a familiar. Through this dynamic, the author explores how advanced technology, when left uncomprehended, inevitably blurs into sorcery. The theme suggests that human societies will always mythologize what they do not understand, transforming science into folklore just as quickly as they might decode it.

Stasheff’s authorial style is defined by a breezy, self-aware wit that balances high-stakes political intrigue with swashbuckling adventure. Written against the backdrop of the turbulent late sixties, the novel subtly mirrors contemporary anxieties regarding government interference, ideological subversion, and the fragility of democracy. The power struggle on Gramarye—featuring a young girl-queen teetering on the edge of civil war—is orchestrated by shadowy off-world interests. Stasheff handles these heavy political maneuverings with a light touch, infusing the prose with snappy dialogue, literary allusions, and rapid pacing. There is a theatricality to his writing; scenes read almost like a briskly staged Renaissance Faire script, elevated by the deadpan, pragmatic internal monologue of our sci-fi protagonist.

While some contemporary readers might find the narrative structure episodic or the world-building leaning toward nostalgic pastiche, Stasheff’s charm lies in his unabashed commitment to the premise. He does not mock fantasy; rather, he builds a plausible, pseudo-scientific bridge to it. The supporting cast—ranging from rebellious teenagers masquerading as a coven to a ragtag army of beggars—adds color and humanity to a plot that could easily have bogged down in political exposition. 

Ultimately, *The Warlock in Spite of Himself* succeeds because it never takes itself too seriously, yet maintains a beating heart regarding the value of freedom and democratic self-determination. It is a witty, inventive romp that reminds us that whether you call it a blaster or a magic wand, the human struggle for power and autonomy remains entirely the same. For anyone looking to escape into a universe where Arthurian legend meets space-age bureaucracy, Stasheff’s classic remains an essential, highly entertaining portal.
