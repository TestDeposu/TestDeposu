# The Bedlam Stacks (The Watchmaker of Filigree Street, #1.5)

**Author:** Natasha Pulley  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

Natasha Pulley has carved out a distinct literary niche for herself, one where the rigid gears of the Victorian world brush dangerously against the soft, unpredictable moss of magic. In The Bedlam Stacks, she returns to the mesmerizing universe of The Watchmaker of Filigree Street, yet pivots away from the foggy parlors of London to thrust her readers deep into the suffocating, hallucinatory heat of Peru. The result is a dazzling, deeply atmospheric adventure that lingers in the mind like a fever dream.

The narrative follows Merrick Tremayne, an India Office worker with a wooden leg and a complicated past, who is dispatched on a clandestine mission. His objective is to smuggle Cinchona seeds—the literal cure for malaria—out of a remote, isolated Peruvian valley known as New Bedlam. But this is no standard historical expedition. The valley is governed by an eerie, unpredictable botanical phenomenon: trees that move of their own accord, a landscape that seems to possess a volatile consciousness, and a community of exiled priests guarding secrets that defy the laws of the British Empire. 

At its core, Pulley’s novel is an exploration of the heavy, often destructive burden of colonialism and the arrogance of empire. The central theme examines the friction between a world that demands order, quantification, and extraction, and a natural world that is inherently untamable, magical, and indifferent to human ambition. Merrick’s wooden leg serves as a brilliant physical metaphor for this friction: he is a man literally held together by manufactured parts, trying to navigate a living, breathing jungle that refuses to be categorized or conquered. 

What elevates Pulley’s work above standard historical fantasy is her unmistakable authorial style. Her prose is deceptively quiet, marked by a dry, understated wit and a meticulous attention to sensory detail. She does not overwhelm the reader with exposition; instead, she drops you directly into the humid air, the scent of crushed leaves, and the clinking porcelain of distant tea sets. Her characters speak with a polite, stiff-upper-lip British reserve that brilliantly juxtaposes the bizarre, surreal horrors unfolding around them. There is a tender, slow-burn emotional core to the story as well, driven by Merrick’s hesitant, profound bond with the enigmatic priest, Esteban. Their relationship develops with a delicate subtlety that feels entirely earned, grounded in mutual vulnerability and quiet loyalty.

The Bedlam Stacks is a triumph of imagination and tone. It is a book that smells of damp earth, quinine, and old paper, moving effortlessly between the mundane bureaucracy of the nineteenth century and the breathtaking heights of magical realism. Pulley has crafted a story that is as intellectually stimulating as it is emotionally resonant, proving once again that she is one of the most distinctive and enchanting voices in modern speculative fiction.
