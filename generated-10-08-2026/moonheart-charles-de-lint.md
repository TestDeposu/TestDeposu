# Moonheart

**Author:** Charles de Lint  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Title: Weaving Myth into Asphalt: The Timeless Magic of Charles de Lint’s Moonheart

In the crowded landscape of modern fantasy, true milestones are rare. Far rarer still are the works that quietly redefine the boundaries of a genre without losing their intimacy. Charles de Lint’s Moonheart, originally published in 1984 and presented here in a definitive Triskell Press edition featuring Charles Vess’s evocative illustrations and an insightful retrospective afterword by the author, is precisely such a milestone. Long before urban fantasy became synonymous with gun-toting paranormal investigators and gritty supernatural crime dramas, de Lint was forging a different kind of magic—one where ancient myth bleeds seamlessly into the concrete and coffee shops of the modern city.

At its core, Moonheart is an exploration of the thin veil separating the mundane world from the primordial. The catalyst is disarmingly simple: Sara Kendell, working in the dusty storeroom of the Merry Dancers Old Book and Antique Emporium, uncovers a trio of seemingly innocuous artifacts—a painting, a ring, and a flat bone disc. With this discovery, de Lint drops his characters down a rabbit hole that bridges the misty, timeless forests of ancient Wales with the bustling, contemporary streets of Ottawa. 

What elevates Moonheart far above a standard portal-fantasy narrative is de Lint’s masterful thematic preoccupation with memory, heritage, and place. The city of Ottawa is not merely a backdrop; it is a living, breathing ecosystem where old gods and folkloric entities walk alongside mortals. Tamson House itself—the legendary focal point of the narrative—stands as a character in its own right, a repository of history and arcane energy that anchors the entire mythos. De Lint suggests that magic is not something foreign imported from afar, but rather a forgotten layer of our own reality, waiting just beneath the surface of the asphalt, accessible only to those willing to look with wonder.

De Lint’s stylistic prowess lies in his remarkable tonal versatility and his deeply human characterization. The author weaves a sprawling, complex tapestry involving an eclectic cast: Sara and her beloved uncle Jamie; Blue, a rough-around-the-edges biker; Kieran, the soulful folk musician; Inspector Tucker of the RCMP; the enigmatic Tom Hengwr; and the puckish trickster Pukwudji. Despite the high-stakes battle against a restless, ancient evil, the heartbeat of the novel remains intensely personal. De Lint writes with a poetic lyricism that feels closer to folk music than high adventure. His prose is rhythmic, melancholy, and deeply atmospheric, capturing the chill of an autumn wind and the haunting strains of a fiddle tune with equal dexterity.

It is little wonder that readers once placed Moonheart on Modern Library’s list of the 20th Century's best novels, nor that it earned de Lint the prestigious William Crawford Award. This is a foundational text of contemporary mythic fiction—a genre-defining achievement that proved fantasy does not need to take place in secondary medieval worlds to be profound. 

For newcomers, this edition offers a peerless entry point into de Lint’s signature universe, enriched by Vess’s stunning visual accompaniment. For returning readers, it is a welcome homecoming to Tamson House. Moonheart is a spellbinding reminder that the old stories never truly die; they merely wait for us to turn the page, open the right box, and remember who we used to be.
