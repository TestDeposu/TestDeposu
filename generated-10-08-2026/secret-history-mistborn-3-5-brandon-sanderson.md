# Secret History (Mistborn, #3.5)

**Author:** Brandon Sanderson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

**Brandon Sanderson’s *Mistborn: Secret History*—A Masterclass in Deception and Revelation**

Brandon Sanderson’s *Mistborn: Secret History* isn’t just a companion story—it’s a deconstruction. A masterful, bone-chilling unraveling of the very foundations of the *Mistborn* trilogy, delivered with the same meticulous craftsmanship that defines Sanderson’s work. This novella isn’t for the casual reader; it’s for those who have already walked through the ruins of the Final Empire, who have felt the weight of Vin’s burden and the hollow victory of the Well’s ascension. For them, *Secret History* is a revelation—one that forces them to question everything they thought they knew.

At its core, the story is about the nature of truth, the fragility of memory, and the ways in which history is rewritten by those in power. The novella follows a character who, in the aftermath of the Well’s ascension, discovers a hidden truth so devastating it could shatter the very mythos of the trilogy. Sanderson doesn’t just drop this bomb—he builds the tension with surgical precision, weaving in subtle hints and deliberate omissions that make the reader’s skin prickle with dread. By the time the truth is unveiled, it’s not just a spoiler; it’s a gut punch.

Sanderson’s prose remains his signature—clear, immersive, and rich with worldbuilding detail. Even in a short story, he maintains the lush, atmospheric quality of his novels, from the gritty streets of Elendel to the arcane intricacies of Allomancy. But where his novels often balance grand spectacle with intimate character moments, *Secret History* is leaner, more focused. It’s a story about perception, where the real magic isn’t in the Allomancy or the Surgebinding—it’s in the way the narrative itself manipulates the reader’s expectations.

The novella’s brilliance lies in its ambiguity. Sanderson doesn’t offer easy answers. The truth he uncovers isn’t a clean resolution but a messy, human one—flawed, contradictory, and deeply unsettling. It forces the reader to confront the idea that even the most carefully constructed myths can be undone by a single, overlooked detail. This is particularly chilling when applied to the *Mistborn* trilogy, where the stakes were never just about saving the world, but about who gets to decide what history remembers.

For those who have read the original trilogy, *Secret History* is a haunting experience. It doesn’t just retcon—it recontextualizes. Characters you thought you understood take on new dimensions, and the events that shaped the world feel suddenly less certain. Sanderson doesn’t just play with the reader’s expectations; he dismantles them, piece by piece, until the only thing left is the question: *What else are we missing?*

This is the kind of storytelling that lingers long after the last page. It’s not just a companion story—it’s a testament to Sanderson’s ability to make even the most familiar world feel fresh, dangerous, and alive with secrets. And in a genre where worldbuilding is often the star, *Secret History* proves that sometimes, the most powerful magic isn’t in the spells—it’s in the way the story itself is told.
