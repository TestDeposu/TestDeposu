# The Unlikely Ones (Pigs Don't Fly, #1)

**Author:** Mary Brown  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

Title: Closet Cliches and Corporate Chemistry: A Closer Look at Julia Kent’s Shopping for a CEO

When readers pick up a romantic comedy, they are generally looking for a predictable yet comforting escape: snappy banter, palpable sexual tension, and characters who bicker their way into happily-ever-afters. In Shopping for a CEO—part of the wildly popular series often erroneously attributed or conflated across various publishing listings—author Julia Kent delivers precisely this brand of frothy, low-stakes entertainment. But beyond the cheeky title and the trope-heavy premise lies a specific execution of contemporary romance that relies heavily on formula, charm, and the sheer comedic timing of its creator.

The central narrative revolves around Amanda Warrick, a professional "fixer" and mystery shopper, and Andrew McCormick, a chauvinistic CEO with a God complex who also happens to be the brother of the groom. Thrust together as maid of honor and best man for a high-society Boston wedding, Amanda and Andrew find themselves locked in a classic battle of stubborn wills. The main theme of the novel explores the friction between control and chaos. Amanda prides herself on being able to fix anything, yet her life—and her composure—implodes whenever she finds herself locked in random closets making out with a man she supposedly can't stand. 

Kent’s writing style is unapologetically light, fast-paced, and engineered for maximum readability. She leans heavily into the enemies-to-lovers trope, utilizing rapid-fire dialogue and internal monologues that highlight her heroines' neurotic yet endearing qualities. Comparisons to Sophie Kinsella and Janet Evanovich are frequently tossed around by enthusiastic fans, and it is easy to see why. Kent shares a penchant for putting ordinary (or quirkily employed) women into extraordinary, humiliating, and ultimately hilarious situations. 

However, the critique of such a work must also acknowledge its limitations. Readers seeking profound character development or gritty realism will likely find the narrative thin. Andrew’s "God complex" is more of a comedic label than a deeply explored psychological flaw, and the conflict surrounding his sudden reluctance to participate in the wedding feels manufactured primarily to drag out the romantic tension. Yet, to criticize the book for lacking deep realism is somewhat missing the point. Shopping for a CEO is comfort food literature. It is the literary equivalent of a rom-com movie you put on a rainy Sunday afternoon because you already know it will end well.

Where Kent truly shines is in her understanding of romantic pacing. The physical chemistry between Amanda and Andrew is undeniable, and the running joke of their impulsive closet rendezvous provides a structural anchor that keeps the comedic energy high. Furthermore, Kent's dialogue sparkles with a witty, self-aware sarcasm that prevents the romance from ever feeling entirely too syrupy. 

Ultimately, Julia Kent cements her reputation as a queen of contemporary romantic comedy with this installment. While it may not reinvent the wheel of the romance genre, it provides an effervescent, laugh-out-loud escape. For fans of smart-mouthed heroines, corporate alpha males with a soft spot, and relentless banter, this book is a thoroughly enjoyable trip to the altar.
