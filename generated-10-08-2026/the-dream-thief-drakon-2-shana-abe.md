# The Dream Thief (Drakon, #2)

**Author:** Shana Abe  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Shadows, Secrets, and Soaring Scales: A Critical Look at Shana Abé’s The Dream Thief

In the landscape of young adult fantasy, historical settings often serve as little more than decorative wallpaper for modern teenage angst. Yet, in The Dream Thief (the second installment in her captivating Drakon series, widely recognized by its U.S. title, The Sweetest Dark), Shana Abé achieves something far more ambitious. She weaves a delicate, atmospheric tapestry where post-Victorian England's rigid societal constraints collide seamlessly with ancient, fiery magic. Abé crafts a world where historical weight and mythical wonder do not merely coexist; they ignite one another.

At the heart of the narrative is Lora Jones, a protagonist whose very existence vibrates with duality. Raised in the gritty, unforgiving shadows of a London orphanage, Lora is a girl marked by the supernatural. She is haunted by inner voices, phantom melodies, and visceral, burning dreams of flight and smoke. When she is thrust into the elite, gothic confines of Iverson boarding school on England’s southern coast, Abé uses the setting to masterfully mirror Lora’s internal fragmentation. The ivy-choked stone and eerie corridors of Iverson are ripe with gothic tradition, yet they pulse with a contemporary, breathless urgency. 

Abé’s greatest triumph in this novel lies in her lyrical prose. The author writes with an elegance that elevates the genre, eschewing clunky exposition in favor of sensory-rich storytelling. When Lora hears songs no one else can hear, the reader hears them too. When she yearns for normalcy while secretly craving the boundless freedom of the sky, her ache is palpable. The style is romantic in the truest sense of the word—evocative, slightly melancholic, and deeply invested in the emotional topography of its characters. 

The central conflict is anchored by a compelling dynamic that will satisfy fans of classic YA love triangles, though Abé elevates the trope through the lens of identity and destiny. In Jesse, the soulful groundskeeper who sees Lora for her true self, and Armand, the arrogant, darkly alluring aristocrat hiding his own monstrous truths, Abé presents two distinct pathways to self-actualization. They are not merely romantic interests; they are catalysts. Through them, Lora must confront her heritage—a heritage inextricably bound to dragons. 

What makes The Dream Thief resonate is its overarching theme of self-discovery disguised as a supernatural thriller. Lora’s journey from a marginalized orphan hiding in plain sight to a young woman learning to harness her terrifying, magnificent power is a potent metaphor for coming into one’s own. Abé handles the introduction of mythical elements into a grounded historical era with a deft hand, ensuring the fantasy never overpowers the human core of the story. 

Ultimately, Shana Abé delivers a spellbinding reading experience. For readers seeking an escape into a richly textured world where history and myth entwine, where romance is laced with danger, and where a girl’s greatest challenge is learning how to fly under the heavy weight of the past, this novel is an absolute must-read. It is atmospheric, deeply romantic, and wonderfully refreshing—a testament to the enduring magic of finding where you truly belong.
