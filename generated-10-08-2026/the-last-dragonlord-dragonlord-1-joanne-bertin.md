# The Last Dragonlord (Dragonlord, #1)

**Author:** Joanne Bertin  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1999

Review: The Last Dragonlord by Joanne Bertin

In a genre perpetually crowded with clanking armor, predictable magic systems, and weary tropes, Joanne Bertin’s 1999 debut, The Last Dragonlord, carves out a remarkably lush and resonant space. At its core, Bertin’s work is an ambitious meditation on mortality, connection, and the heavy burden of stewardship. By introducing a dying breed of immortal shape-shifters tasked with safeguarding fragile humanity, the novel immediately establishes a poignant thematic tension: those who live forever must constantly witness the fleeting, fragile tragedy of human existence.

The narrative anchors itself around Linden Rathan, the last-born of the weredragons, whose six-century quest for his prophesied "soultwin" elevates the romance beyond mere genre convention. Bertin uses the concept of the soultwin not as a cheap emotional shortcut, but as a profound exploration of completion and vulnerability. When Linden is thrust into the volatile political arena of Cassori following the queen's mysterious death, his internal yearning for permanence clashes sharply with the messy, shifting tides of mortal ambition. The political intrigue—driven by rival claimants to the regency and the insidious threat of the Fellowship, a secret society dead set on eradicating the immortal race—serves as a high-stakes crucible that forces Linden out of his centuries-long isolation.

Stylistically, Bertin writes with a sensory richness that leans into the classic, sweeping traditions of epic fantasy while maintaining an intimate character focus. Her prose is patient, evocative, and deeply atmospheric, painting the Five Kingdoms with vibrant strokes that make the world feel lived-in and historically layered. She deftly balances the grand scale of impending civil war with the delicate, evolving dynamic between Linden and Maurynna, the sharp-witted ship captain whose strength matches his own. Maurynna is no mere damsel; she is a capable force of nature who anchors Linden just as much as he protects her, bringing a grounded, nautical pragmatism to the elevated fantasy realms.

What makes The Last Dragonlord endure as a compelling read is Bertin's refusal to let her immortals feel detached or unfeeling. Their power comes with a cost, and their guardianship of humanity is fraught with danger, misunderstanding, and sacrifice. The pacing moves with deliberate grace, allowing readers to savor the world-building, the romantic tension, and the creeping menace of the Fellowship in equal measure. 

For readers seeking a fantasy that seamlessly weaves together high-stakes political maneuvering, deep-seated romance, and the majestic allure of dragon lore, Bertin’s debut is a rewarding journey. It reminds us that even beings meant to last an eternity are utterly lost without someone to share it with.
