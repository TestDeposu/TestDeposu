# Empire of Grass (The Last King of Osten Ard, #2)

**Author:** Tad Williams  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

**Empire of Grass: A Masterclass in Epic Fantasy’s Quiet Descent into Chaos**

Tad Williams’ *Empire of Grass*—the second installment in *The Last King of Osten Ard*—is a book that understands the weight of silence. In a world where war is not yet declared but already breathing, Williams crafts a narrative where the real battle isn’t fought with swords or spells, but with the slow, creeping dread of inevitability. This is fantasy as a study in tension, where the threat isn’t just external but a creeping rot beneath the surface of civilized kingdoms. It’s a testament to Williams’ ability to weave political intrigue, mythic dread, and personal stakes into a tapestry so rich it feels like history unfolding in real time.

At its core, *Empire of Grass* is a meditation on the fragility of peace. Simon and Miriamele, the royal couple from the first book, are no longer just survivors—they are the last bulwarks of a world unraveling. The Norns’ armies march under the guise of an alliance, Nabban teeters on the edge of civil war, and the Thrithings, those fierce nomadic tribes of the grasslands, stir with a hatred so ancient it feels like a curse. Williams doesn’t just tell us these threats exist; he makes us *feel* the way they press in from every side, like the slow encroachment of a storm. There’s no grand battle cry here, no immediate showdown—just the quiet, suffocating realization that resistance is futile unless the right pieces fall into place.

What makes this book so compelling is Williams’ refusal to rush. He doesn’t need to spell out every conspiracy or reveal every villain’s hand. Instead, he trusts the reader to piece together the threads as they unravel, much like Simon and Miriamele themselves. The Witchwood Crown, that deadly puzzle at the heart of the story, isn’t just a MacGuffin—it’s a metaphor for the entire book. It’s a riddle that demands patience, intuition, and the willingness to look beyond the obvious. Williams, a master of layered storytelling, knows that the most terrifying threats are often the ones we don’t see coming.

His prose is a delight in this regard. Williams has always been a wordsmith who balances lyrical beauty with sharp precision, and *Empire of Grass* is no exception. His descriptions of the grasslands, with their endless horizons and shifting sands, are hypnotic, evoking a sense of vastness that contrasts with the intimate, claustrophobic tension of court politics. Yet, for all its grandeur, the book never loses its groundedness. Williams grounds his mythic world in human emotion—Simon’s exhaustion, Miriamele’s quiet strength, the fear of the common folk who know war is coming but can’t stop it. It’s this blend of the epic and the personal that makes the story so gripping.

The real strength of *Empire of Grass*, though, lies in its themes. Williams isn’t just writing about war; he’s writing about the ways power corrupts, how fear binds people together, and how even the smallest acts of defiance can ripple into something far greater. The Norns, those ancient and terrifying figures, aren’t just villains—they’re a reminder of the forces that shape history, indifferent to human suffering. The Thrithings’ hatred of the city-dwellers isn’t just bigotry; it’s a cycle of violence that has been passed down for generations, and breaking it will require more than just strength. Williams challenges his readers to ask: What would you do if the world you knew was ending? And more importantly, what would it take to save it?

For fans of *Memory, Sorrow, and Thorn* and *The Dragonbone Chair*, *Empire of Grass* is a natural progression—a deeper dive into the world Williams has spent decades building. But it’s also a book that stands on its own, a masterclass in how to make fantasy feel lived-in, how to make the slow burn of a coming storm feel more terrifying than any battle ever could. It’s a reminder that the greatest threats aren’t always the ones we see coming; sometimes, they’re the ones we don’t notice until it’s too late.

In the end, *Empire of Grass* is more than just a sequel—it’s a chapter in a larger story about the cost of power, the weight of legacy, and the quiet courage it takes to stand against the tide. Williams doesn’t just tell us what’s coming; he makes us *feel* the way the world is holding its breath, waiting to see if anyone will have the strength to change its course.
