# Into the Labyrinth (The Death Gate Cycle, #6)

**Author:** Margaret Weis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1993

Into the Labyrinth, the sixth installment of Margaret Weis and Tracy Hickman’s monumental Death Gate Cycle, stands as a masterclass in epic fantasy world-building and character evolution. Published in 1993, this penultimate volume plunges the reader back into the most terrifying construct of a shattered world: the Labyrinth itself. While the series as a whole explores the consequences of cosmic hubris and historical trauma, this specific volume narrows its focus to the intimate, painful redemption of its central figures, Haplo and Alfred.

At the heart of Into the Labyrinth lies the profound theme of inescapable legacy versus conscious transformation. The Labyrinth is not merely a physical setting; it is a manifestation of the Sartan race’s arrogant, flawed attempt at divine justice, a lethal prison maze meant to reform the rebellious Patryns through sheer brutality. By forcing Haplo—the quintessential anti-hero marked by the trauma of his upbringing—back into the nightmare of his youth, Weis confronts the scars of the past head-on. The labyrinthine corridors serve as a psychological mirror reflecting the inner turmoil of both Haplo and his unlikely companion, Alfred. 

The dynamic between Haplo and Alfred remains the beating pulse of the narrative. Alfred, representing the seemingly gentle and hesitant Sartan, and Haplo, the hardened Patryn assassin, embody the historical rift between their warring peoples. Yet, as they navigate the lethal traps and mutated horrors of the maze, their relationship evolves from uneasy alliance to profound brotherhood. Weis uses this dynamic to explore a central philosophical question: Can those forged in cruelty ever unlearn it, or are they doomed to perpetuate the cycle of violence? Through their harrowing journey, the author argues that true strength is found not in magical dominance or defensive walls, but in vulnerability, empathy, and the courage to forgive.

Weis’s literary style in Into the Labyrinth is characterized by a seamless blend of gritty realism and high-fantasy grandeur. Unlike the sprawling, disconnected world-hopping of earlier books in the cycle, this novel benefits from a tighter narrative focus. The pacing is relentless, driven by the constant threat of assassins and the ticking clock of the cosmic apocalypse. Weis excels at sensory descriptions, painting the Labyrinth not just as a geographic location, but as a living, breathing entity that hungers for the souls of those trapped within its walls. The atmosphere is claustrophobic, tense, and melancholic, yet consistently punctured by moments of unexpected humor and deeply moving emotional resonance.

Furthermore, Weis avoids the trap of black-and-white morality that often plagues traditional fantasy. The antagonists are products of broken systems, and even the heroic figures are heavily flawed. This moral complexity elevates Into the Labyrinth from a standard adventure story into a poignant meditation on survival, identity, and the high cost of healing. 

For returning fans of the Death Gate Cycle, this volume delivers the crucial revelations and emotional payoffs necessary to set the stage for the grand finale. For newcomers, it serves as a testament to Margaret Weis’s ability to craft intricate, character-driven fantasy that lingers long after the final page is turned. It is a dark, gripping, and ultimately hopeful journey into the heart of darkness, proving that even the most labyrinthine mistakes of the past can be undone by the light of genuine human connection.
