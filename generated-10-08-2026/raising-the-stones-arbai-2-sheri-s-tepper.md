# Raising the Stones (Arbai, #2)

**Author:** Sheri S. Tepper  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

Sheri S. Tepper’s Raising the Stones, the mesmerizing second volume in the Arbai series, stands as a towering achievement in speculative fiction. Eschewing the easy tropes of traditional space opera, Tepper crafts a narrative that is both an intimate anthropological study and a sweeping ecological warning. At its core, the novel grapples with a profound and urgent theme: the delicate, fraught relationship between humanity, divinity, and the natural world, and whether sentient species can ever truly coexist with the ancient, indifferent forces of the universe.

The story unfolds on the newly colonized world of Hobbs Land, a planet seeded with the mysterious artifacts of the long-extinct Arbai. Here, human settlers attempt to build a society free from the oppressive dogmas and patriarchal violence of their homeworlds. Yet, Hobbs Land is not a passive canvas. The planet possesses a quiet, pervasive agency, nurtured by the remnants of Arbai technology and the indigenous biology. Tepper uses this setting to explore how human social structures—specifically greed, religious fanaticism, and the urge to dominate—clash with a planetary ecology that demands reverence, reciprocity, and balance. 

Where many science fiction writers treat ecology as mere set dressing, Tepper makes it the beating heart of her narrative. The central conflict arises from the divergence of two human communities: the "Sensitives," who live in harmony with the land and listen to its subtle rhythms, and the "Abasement" traditionalists, who bring with them the baggage of patriarchal control, misogyny, and spiritual tyranny. Through this dichotomy, Tepper dissects the toxicity of domination—over women, over communities, and ultimately, over the earth itself. The novel posits that the survival of humanity depends on abandoning the illusion of mastery and embracing an ethic of radical connection.

Tepper’s style is uniquely her own: lyrical, uncompromising, and fiercely moral. She writes with the righteous fury of a prophet and the meticulous eye of a botanist. Her prose seamlessly blends the wondrous with the visceral, moving from moments of breathtaking ecological beauty to scenes of chilling psychological and physical cruelty. Tepper does not coddle her readers; she presents complex, morally grey characters who must navigate the consequences of their ancestors' sins and their own daily choices. The narrative voice is authoritative and deeply empathetic, driven by a palpable urgency that makes the fate of Hobbs Land feel terrifyingly immediate.

Furthermore, Tepper’s world-building is masterfully layered. She populates her universe with rich mythologies, complex socio-political systems, and non-human entities that genuinely feel alien rather than merely exoticized humans. The lingering ghost of the Arbai hangs over every page, serving as a silent benchmark for what an enlightened civilization might look like. 

Raising the Stones is not a comfortable read, nor is it meant to be. It is a challenging, deeply reflective work of speculative fiction that forces us to look in the mirror and question the foundations of our own civilization. Tepper challenges us to imagine a world where reverence replaces exploitation, and where the stones themselves might bear witness to our choices. For fans of thoughtful, politically engaged, and ecologically conscious science fiction, this novel is nothing short of essential.
