# The Dragon and the Unicorn (Arthor, #1)

**Author:** A.A. Attanasio  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

A History of the Future: Geopolitics and Dynastic Intrigue in A.A. Attanasio’s The Dragon and the Unicorn

In a literary landscape frequently saturated with recycled tropes and predictable dystopian futures, A.A. Attanasio’s The Dragon and the Unicorn emerges as a rare, intellectually formidable achievement. Though officially functioning as the first installment in the Arthor series, this 2016 release transcends the boundaries of standard genre fiction. It operates instead as a grand, sweeping geopolitical thriller—a speculative history of tomorrow that feels astonishingly urgent and tethered to the anxieties of the present. 

The core theme of the novel is the seismic shift of global hegemony. As American might recedes and the influence of Asia ascends, the world teeters on the precipice of a cataclysmic war between two entrenched superpowers. Yet, Attanasio wisely avoids reducing this monumental friction to mere cold statistics or broad ideological warfare. Instead, he grounds the macrocosm of international conflict in the intimate, fragile lives of four great families: the cosmopolitan McAffreys of New York and Singapore; the haughty, fractured Chans of Malaysia; the brilliant, enigmatic Cheungs of Hong Kong; and the fallen illustriousness of the Baos of China. 

Through these dynasties, the author explores how personal ambition, generational trauma, and familial loyalty become inextricably weaponized within a clandestine Davos plot to radically remake the world order. The central thesis is both profound and chilling: the future is not merely stumbled into; it is actively forged by the collision of ancient prejudices, modern economic forces, and the relentless human hunger for dominance.

Attanasio’s narrative style is the true engine driving this sprawling premise. A Nebula and World Fantasy Award nominee, the author brings a poetic yet fiercely disciplined prose style to a genre typically dominated by utilitarian writing. His action sequences play out with a choreographic elegance that marries kinetic energy with psychological depth. Nothing feels weightless. Whether tracing the shadowy machinations within the forbidding halls of Zhongnanhai, navigating the Gothic quads of Yale, or capturing the glittering, claustrophobic gilded cage of Singapore, Attanasio renders every setting with vibrant, atmospheric precision. 

Furthermore, the author’s construction of his political scenario possesses a verisimilitude as tensile and reactive as current evening news broadcasts. He understands the friction points of modern globalization—the trade routes, the digital surveillance, the cultural anxieties, and the fragile alliances that keep the peace by a thread. The scions of the McAffrey, Chan, Cheung, and Bao clans do not feel like mere chess pieces moving across a geopolitical board; they are fully realized, deeply flawed humans caught in the gears of history. Their private griefs and public triumphs mirror the instability of the nations they represent, creating a brilliant thematic resonance between the individual and the empire.

Ultimately, The Dragon and the Unicorn is more than just a thriller; it is an examination of how yesterday’s ghosts dictate tomorrow’s battlefields. Attanasio challenges his readers to look beyond the headlines and recognize the human cost of shifting power dynamics. For anyone seeking a sophisticated, globally expansive narrative that respects the reader's intellect while delivering relentless suspense, this novel is an essential, striking read.
