# The Dreaming Tree (Arafel, #1-2)

**Author:** C.J. Cherryh  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1997

C.J. Cherryh is a master of atmosphere, a writer who builds worlds not merely through exposition, but through the heavy, palpable air of her settings. In The Dreaming Tree, an omnibus combining her classic fantasy novels The Dreamstone and The Tree of Swords and Jewels, Cherryh turns her formidable talents to a poignant, elegiac exploration of a world caught in the painful throes of transition. This is not the high fantasy of grand armies and triumphant light; rather, it is a delicate, melancholic twilight where the old magic of the Sidhe recedes before the inevitable, smoking advance of mortal iron and human ambition.

At the heart of this duology is a profound central theme: the tragic inevitability of cultural obsolescence and the heavy cost of progress. Cherryh captures a transitional epoch of the world, a poignant dawn for humanity that simultaneously marks the quiet, agonizing death of magic. Ealdwood stands as the last untouched sanctuary, guarded by Arafel, a being of the Fair Folk whose deep pride and lingering love for the mortal realm anchor her to a dying age. Arafel is no benevolent, distant fairy godmother; she is fierce, melancholic, and bound to a world that is steadily forgetting her kind. Opposite her stands Ciaran Cuilean, Lord of Caer Wiell, a man caught agonizingly between two worlds. With elvish blood in his veins yet bound to the treacherous politics of mortal keeps, Ciaran embodies the friction of divided loyalties. When suspicion and fear drive him from his own people, he must return to Ealdwood, seeking refuge in the very shadows humanity has learned to distrust.

What makes The Dreaming Tree a striking achievement in speculative fiction is Cherryh’s distinctive stylistic approach. Eschewing the fast-paced, action-heavy conventions often found in epic fantasy, Cherryh crafts a narrative that moves with the deliberate, haunting rhythm of a ballad. Her prose is dense, poetic, and deeply internal, prioritizing the psychological landscapes of her characters over bombastic spectacle. The world-building feels ancient and tactile; readers can practically smell the reek of mortal smoke and feel the chill of faery silver. Cherryh excels at capturing the quiet moments—the hesitation before a drawn sword, the weight of inherited destiny, and the sorrow of a parting that spans centuries. 

The relationship between Arafel and Ciaran is handled with a subtle, bittersweet nuance. Their alliance is forged not out of sweeping romantic fantasy, but from a shared necessity to face a newly awakened, encroaching evil that threatens both the warm hearthstones of men and the silvery heart of the forest. As Ciaran is forced to reclaim his haunted weapons from the Tree of Swords, the narrative asks a timeless question: can humanity coexist with the wonders it seeks to conquer, or is progress destined to consume the very magic that gives life its deeper meaning?

Ultimately, The Dreaming Tree is a rewarding, immersive experience for readers who appreciate literary depth, atmospheric world-building, and character-driven fantasy. Cherryh does not offer easy resolutions or comfortable happy endings; instead, she offers a masterclass in mood and tone, preserving a vanishing world within the amber of her prose. For anyone willing to step beneath the boughs of Ealdwood, this omnibus is a timeless reminder of the magic that slips away the moment we stop believing in it.
