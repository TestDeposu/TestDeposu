# Janitors (Janitors, #1)

**Author:** Tyler Whitesides  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

**A Whimsical, Wickedly Clever Adventure: *Janitors* by Tyler Whitesides**

Tyler Whitesides’ *Janitors*—the first book in a series that’s already sparking laughter and wonder—is the kind of middle-grade fantasy that feels like stumbling into a secret world where magic isn’t just possible, it’s *deliciously* chaotic. At its heart, this book is a high-stakes, fast-paced romp about consequence, creativity, and the kind of wish you’d make if you knew the universe owed you a do-over. But what truly sets *Janitors* apart isn’t just its premise—it’s Whitesides’ razor-sharp wit, his refusal to take anything too seriously, and his knack for turning ordinary (or absurd) moments into something extraordinary.

The story follows Ace, a 12-year-old whose life takes a left turn when he accidentally frees Ridge, a genie with a penchant for sarcasm and a rulebook that’s as strict as it is unfair: every wish comes with a *consequence*, and the bigger the wish, the bigger the fallout. Want to save the world? Fine. But be prepared for your legs to turn into pogo sticks, your clothes to vanish, or your best friend to turn into a sentient, judgmental toaster. The stakes couldn’t be higher—if Ace fails, the world ends in a *cuddly* apocalypse—but the tone never wavers. Whitesides balances heart-pounding urgency with a humor so sharp it’s almost painful, like watching a comedy show where the punchline is also the cliffhanger.

What makes *Janitors* so compelling isn’t just the magic system (though it’s clever—think *Aladdin* meets *The Twilight Zone* with a dash of *Stranger Things* nostalgia) but the way Whitesides treats his characters. Ace isn’t a chosen one; he’s a kid who’s just trying to survive middle school, and his journey is less about destiny and more about *adapting*. Ridge, meanwhile, is the kind of genie you’d love to hate—if he weren’t so endlessly entertaining. Their dynamic is electric, a mix of bickering, reluctant teamwork, and the kind of banter that makes you laugh out loud. Even the side characters, like Ace’s quirky friends and the world’s doomed (but oddly lovable) fate, feel vivid and alive.

Whitesides’ writing style is a masterclass in pacing and tone. The prose is lean, the dialogue crackles, and the plot never lingers in the mud. One minute, you’re laughing at a ridiculous consequence (how many times can a kid’s legs turn into pogo sticks before it becomes a running gag?), and the next, you’re on the edge of your seat wondering how Ace will pull off his impossible quest. The book’s humor is universal—there’s nothing here that relies on niche references or overly complex jokes—but it’s also *smart*. Whitesides understands that middle-grade readers aren’t just looking for laughs; they’re looking for stories that make them think, feel, and maybe even question the world around them. And while *Janitors* is far from preachy, it does a great job of exploring themes of responsibility, creativity, and the power of choices—even when those choices seem small.

The magic system itself is one of the book’s strongest assets. Unlike traditional wish-granting stories where the rules are vague or the consequences are vague, Whitesides’ setup is *deliberate*. The bigger the wish, the bigger the consequence, and the consequences are often absurd, physical, and *immediate*. This creates a tension that’s both hilarious and tense—will Ace wish for world peace and end up with a pet dragon? Will he try to fix his broken friendship and accidentally turn his friend into a walking dictionary? The possibilities are endless, and Whitesides never lets the reader forget that every choice has weight. It’s a brilliant way to teach kids about cause and effect without ever feeling like a lecture.

Visually, *Janitors* would be a dream to adapt—imagine the chaos of Ace’s legs turning into pogo sticks, or Ridge’s dramatic sighs as he’s forced to grant yet another ridiculous wish. The book’s energy is infectious, and Whitesides’ ability to blend humor, heart, and high stakes makes it a standout in a crowded middle-grade market. It’s the kind of book that feels like a breath of fresh air, a story that doesn’t take itself too seriously but still manages to leave a lasting impression.

For fans of *Escape from Mr. Lemoncello’s Library* or *The Last Kids on Earth*, *Janitors* is a must-read. It’s fast, funny, and full of heart, with a magic system that’s as inventive as it is fun. And if you’re looking for a book that makes you laugh, think, and maybe even wish for a do-over of your own life, this is it. Just don’t wish for pogo sticks for legs—you’ll thank me later.
