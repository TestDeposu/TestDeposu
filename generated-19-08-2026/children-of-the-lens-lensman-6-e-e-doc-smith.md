# Children of the Lens (Lensman, #6)

**Author:** E.E. "Doc" Smith  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2023

Children of the Lens, the sixth and penultimate volume of E. E. “Doc” Smith’s Lensman saga, throws the reader straight into the maelstrom of a galaxy‑spanning conflict where the fate of civilizations hinges on the mettle of a single Lens‑wielding hero. Kimball Kinnison, now a seasoned Galactic Co‑ordinator, has traded the raw vigor of youth for a steely, contemplative resolve; his physical presence remains imposing, yet the narrative lingers on the quiet maturation of his mind—a man who has learned that true power lies not just in the force of his arms but in the clarity of his purpose.

At its core, the novel wrestles with the theme of responsibility versus destiny. Kinnison’s Lens, a semi‑sentient artifact granting him extraordinary perception and telepathic reach, becomes both a blessing and a burden. Smith uses this device to explore how immense power can isolate its wielder, forcing him to confront moral ambiguities that no simple battle can resolve. The Lens thus operates as a metaphor for the burden of leadership: the more one can see, the heavier the weight of choosing when to act and when to restraint. The story’s tension derives less from laser‑blast spectacles and more from Kinnison’s internal calculus—whether to trust the enigmatic Arisians, to sacrifice personal love for the greater good, or to gamble on a desperate plan that could either save the universe or usher in its annihilation.

Smith’s hallmark style shines through in this installment, though it is tempered by the series’ progression toward a more philosophical register. His prose remains unmistakably pulp‑era: rapid‑fire sentences, grandiose descriptors, and a penchant for coining technobabble that feels both plausible and wonderfully absurd. Yet, amid the familiar tropes of dashing space fleets, alien monstrosities, and heroic last‑stands, there is a discernible shift toward introspection. Dialogue, while still peppered with the classic “By the Lens!” exclamations, occasionally pauses for moments of quiet reflection, allowing the reader to feel the gravitas of Kinnison’s decisions. The author’s world‑building continues to dazzle: sprawling star systems, ancient alien races, and the looming menace of the Boskone empire are rendered with a vividness that makes the cosmos feel both intimate and infinite.

What sets Children of the Lens apart from its predecessors is the way Smith balances spectacle with substance. The climactic confrontations are still larger‑than‑life—fleets clash in nebulous fireworks, psychic duels crackle across light‑years—but each action is underscored by a clear moral stakes. The Lens, rather than being a mere plot coupon, becomes the narrative’s conscience, prompting Kinnison (and the reader) to weigh the cost of victory against the preservation of liberty. This interplay elevates the novel from a straightforward space opera to a meditation on the evolution of consciousness—a theme that resonates strongly with contemporary readers who appreciate both nostalgic adventure and thoughtful speculation.

In sum, Children of the Lens delivers the high‑octane thrills that have defined the Lensman series while inviting its audience to ponder the ethical dimensions of omniscient power. Smith’s signature blend of breakneck pacing, inventive sci‑fi concepts, and earnest heroism remains intact, yet he layers it with a maturity that reflects both the character’s growth and the series’ own literary ascent. For longtime fans, it is a satisfying culmination of arcs seeded decades earlier; for newcomers, it offers a compelling gateway into a universe where the greatest battles are fought not only among stars but within the soul of those who dare to wield the Lens.
