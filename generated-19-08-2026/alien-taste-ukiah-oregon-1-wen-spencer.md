# Alien Taste (Ukiah Oregon, #1)

**Author:** Wen Spencer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2001

**Alien Taste: A Haunting Odyssey of Instinct and Blood**

Wen Spencer’s *Alien Taste* is a novel that lingers like the scent of wet earth after a storm—rich, unsettling, and impossible to ignore. Set in the misty forests of Ukiah, Oregon, this debut novel (2001) is more than a thriller; it’s a psychological descent into the raw, primal forces that bind us to our past, our predators, and the very nature of survival. At its core, *Alien Taste* is a story about the fragility of humanity, the allure of the wild, and the terrifying realization that some instincts run deeper than blood—or law.

Ukiah, the novel’s protagonist, is a man carved from contradictions. Abandoned as a child and raised by a wolf pack, he is a creature of duality: a private investigator with a nose for the truth, yet a killer who moves through the world with the silent precision of a predator. His heightened senses—his "alien taste," as the title suggests—are both his greatest asset and his most dangerous flaw. Spencer doesn’t just write about Ukiah’s abilities; he *immerses* the reader in them. The world becomes a symphony of smells, a landscape where every scent carries weight, where the air itself whispers secrets. This sensory immersion isn’t just a gimmick; it’s a narrative choice that forces the reader to experience the world through Ukiah’s eyes, making his journey visceral and inescapable.

The novel’s strength lies in its exploration of identity and belonging. Ukiah is neither fully human nor fully wolf, a liminal figure who exists in the space between civilization and the wild. His relationship with the FBI’s most wanted—the Pack—isn’t just a plot device; it’s a mirror held up to his own fractured psyche. The Pack isn’t just a gang; they are a family, a bond forged in violence and blood. Spencer doesn’t shy away from the grotesque or the poetic in his portrayal of these characters. The Pack’s leader, for instance, is a figure who oscillates between terrifying and almost tragic, a man who understands Ukiah in ways no one else does. This duality is what makes *Alien Taste* so compelling—it refuses to let its characters (or its readers) settle into easy moralities.

The prose is lean, almost sparse, but never cold. Spencer’s writing has the grit of a man who has spent too many nights in the woods, listening to the wind howl through the trees. His descriptions are evocative without being overwrought; a single sentence can convey the weight of a lifetime of solitude. The dialogue crackles with tension, sharp and immediate, as if every word is a blade’s edge. There’s a raw energy to the book, a sense that it was written in the heat of the moment, as if Spencer couldn’t wait to get these characters and their story down on paper.

Yet, for all its intensity, *Alien Taste* isn’t without its flaws. The pacing in some sections feels uneven, particularly in the buildup to the confrontation with the Pack. There are moments where the plot stumbles, where the tension doesn’t quite hold, and where the reader is left waiting for the next revelation rather than being pulled forward by the momentum of the story. These are minor quibbles, however, in a novel that otherwise delivers a visceral, thought-provoking experience.

At its heart, *Alien Taste* is a story about the cost of survival. Ukiah’s journey is one of self-discovery, but it’s also a descent into the abyss of what it means to be human—or something else entirely. Spencer doesn’t offer easy answers. There is no neat resolution, no triumphant return to civilization. Instead, he presents a world where the line between hunter and prey is blurred, where the past is never truly buried, and where the only certainty is that some wounds run too deep to heal.

For readers who crave stories that challenge them, that make them question their own nature, *Alien Taste* is a must-read. It’s a novel that will leave you breathless, unsettled, and perhaps a little afraid of what lurks in the shadows of your own instincts. Spencer’s work is a testament to the power of the uncanny, a reminder that the wild doesn’t just live outside us—it lives in the bones of every one of us.
