# River of Gods (India 2047, #1)

**Author:** Ian McDonald  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

Welcome to 2047: a fractured, technocratic, and utterly intoxicating subcontinent celebrating its first centennial of independence. In River of Gods, visionary science fiction master Ian McDonald paints a staggering, kaleidoscopic portrait of a future India bifurcated by climate catastrophe, staggering geopolitical shifts, and a radical technological revolution. One hundred years after shaking off the shackles of the British Empire, the nation of Bharat and its dozen splintered successor states stand on the precipice of oblivion and rebirth, locked in the shadow of a failing monsoon and a severe, civilization-threatening drought. 

At its core, McDonald’s masterwork is driven by a profound and urgent theme: the violent, beautiful friction between ancient traditions and hyper-advanced futures. This is a world where nine million gods coexist with sapient artificial intelligences—known locally as the ayas—and where a terrifying demographic imbalance has left males outnumbering females four to one. Amidst this pressure cooker, McDonald traces the lives of nine diverse characters whose destinies violently intertwine. From ambitious politicians and world-weary cops to genetically modified children who age at half the human rate, gangster elements, and wandering stand-up comics, the novel uses its sprawling ensemble to dissect how massive, impersonal waves of history crash against intensely personal human desires, betrayals, and survival. 

Stylistically, River of Gods reads like William Gibson channeling the sensory overload of modern Mumbai and Varanasi. McDonald employs a high-octane, immersive prose style that plunges the reader headfirst into the deep end of a culturally saturated future. He avoids the westernized, homogenized futures typical of lazy science fiction, instead constructing a densely layered linguistic and sociological tapestry. The text is alive with native vernacular, staggering technological jargon, philosophical musings on faith and consciousness, and vivid contrasts between the squalor of devastated slums and the dizzying, neon-lit heights of corporate opulence. It is a style that demands patience and rewards close attention, offering a form of literary tourism that is as intellectually stimulating as it is pulse-pounding.

Like the great river Ganges that flows relentlessly through its pages, carrying both ash and prayer, River of Gods is a magnificent, overwhelming torrent of a novel. It does not offer easy answers or comforting resolutions, but rather an authentic, messy, and breathtakingly realized vision of a superpower redefining itself at the end of the world. For readers hungry for science fiction that breaks away from western tropes to explore the complex, multi-polar realities of the twenty-first century and beyond, McDonald’s masterwork remains an absolute essential read.
