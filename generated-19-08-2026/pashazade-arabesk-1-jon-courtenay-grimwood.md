# Pashazade (Arabesk, #1)

**Author:** Jon Courtenay Grimwood  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2005

Imagine a twentieth century where history took a sharp, intoxicating turn—where the United States brokered the end of the First World War, the Ottoman Empire never fell, and North Africa pulses as the throbbing, exotic heart of global power. This is the vividly rendered playground of Jon Courtenay Grimwood’s Pashazade, the breathtaking opening salvo of the Arabesk series. Published in 2005, this genre-defying novel effortlessly fuses hard-boiled noir with speculative fiction, delivering a sensory-overload experience that lingers long after the final page.

At the center of this sun-scorched, blood-slicked metropolis known as El Iskandryia stands Ashraf Bey, or Raf to those who know him best. Raf is a man caught in the ultimate identity crisis. To society, he is a wealthy Ottoman aristocrat; to the underworld, he is a former street criminal once tied to Chinese Triads. In reality, Raf possesses only a vague surname hinting at a noble past and an arranged marriage to a woman who openly despises him. Grimwood uses Raf not just as a vessel for a detective thriller, but as a lens to explore the central theme of the book: the slippery, malleable nature of identity in a world built on political and personal deceit. 

When Raf is suddenly accused of a brutal murder he has no memory of committing, the narrative shifts into high gear. Now a hunted fugitive, saddled with the care of a remarkably precocious young girl, he must navigate the labyrinthine alleys of El Isk. The city itself becomes a living, breathing character—all brilliant light, suffocating heat, and ancient shadows. Grimwood’s world-building is seamless, avoiding the heavy-handed infodumps that often bog down alternative history. Instead, the alternate geopolitics are woven effortlessly into the fabric of daily life, language, and culture.

Stylistically, Grimwood writes with a razor-sharp economy of language that mirrors the cynicism and grit of classic noir fiction, yet he injects it with a rich, literary lyricism. The prose snaps with energy, perfectly capturing the chaotic rhythm of El Iskandryia. It is a mature balancing act, marrying the adrenaline-fueled pacing of a crime thriller with the introspective depth of a rite-of-passage story. 

Pashazade is not merely a mystery where the facts diverge from the truth; it is a profound exploration of how a man reinvents himself when everything he thought he knew about his past turns out to be a lie. For readers hungry for speculative fiction that refuses to stay neatly inside the box—blending alternate history, mystery, and deep character study—Grimwood’s masterpiece is an absolute triumph.
