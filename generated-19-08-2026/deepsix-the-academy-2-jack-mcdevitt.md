# Deepsix (The Academy, #2)

**Author:** Jack McDevitt  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

Jack McDevitt is a rare breed in contemporary science fiction, a writer more interested in the profound implications of discovery than in the cheap thrills of interstellar warfare. In Deepsix, the second installment of his acclaimed Academy series, McDevitt proves once again that he is a master of archaeological science fiction. Published in 2010, the novel takes the signature existential dread and sense of wonder that defined *The Engines of God* and amplifies them into a pulse-pounding, planetary-scale race against time.

The narrative hook is deceptively simple: Maleiva III, a rare and vibrant life-bearing world, is about to be obliterated by a rogue moon. With a mere three weeks until cataclysm, superluminal pilot Priscilla "Hutch" Hutchins finds herself pressed into service to lead a frantic, eleventh-hour archaeological expedition to the doomed surface. But what begins as a desperate salvage mission quickly devolves into a nightmare of survival when the team is stranded on a world that is not merely dying, but actively going mad under the gravitational assault of its impending doom.

McDevitt’s central theme in Deepsix is not humanity's conquest of space, but rather our humbling insignificance in the face of deep time and cosmic indifference. The planet Maleiva III—subsequently dubbed Deepsix—is a marvel of world-building, presented as a complex, fragile biosphere peppered with tantalizing clues of a lost civilization. Yet, as the rogue moon draws closer, the planet itself seems to rebel. McDevitt uses this impending apocalypse to explore how different scientists, ideologues, and bureaucrats react when confronted with terminal crisis. The ticking clock mechanism creates an escalating claustrophobia, shifting the tone from an intellectual exploration of alien anthropology to a raw, visceral struggle for existence.

Stylistically, McDevitt writes with a clean, unpretentious prose that prioritizes intellectual engagement and atmospheric tension over stylistic flash. He channels the spirit of Arthur C. Clarke and Isaac Asimov, treating science not as magic, but as a rigorous, sometimes frustrating process of deduction. The pacing is deliberate, building a slow-burn mystery before plunging the characters—and the reader—into chaotic, survival-driven desperation. Hutch remains the beating heart of the story: competent, weary, fiercely protective of her crew, and driven by an insatiable curiosity that repeatedly puts her in harm's way. She is not a superhero; she is a professional pushed past her breaking point by a universe that doesn't care about human deadlines.

If the novel has a flaw, it lies in the sheer breadth of its ambition, occasionally scattering its focus across the large ensemble of scientists and accompanying media personalities. Yet, these character dynamics ultimately serve McDevitt’s thematic exploration of human nature under extreme pressure. As political interference from Earth threatens to complicate an already impossible rescue, the book morphs into a biting critique of institutional hubris and media sensationalism.

Deepsix is a triumphant blend of hard science fiction and planetary disaster thriller. It captures the melancholic beauty of lost civilizations and the heart-stopping terror of a world tearing itself apart at the seams. For readers who crave intelligent, atmospheric science fiction where the greatest mysteries are written in the dirt of alien worlds, McDevitt delivers an unforgettable voyage to the edge of the abyss.
