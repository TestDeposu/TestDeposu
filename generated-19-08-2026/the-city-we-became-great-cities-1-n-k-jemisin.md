# The City We Became (Great Cities, #1)

**Author:** N.K. Jemisin  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

**The City We Became: Where Myths Walk and History Breathes**

N.K. Jemisin doesn’t just write fantasy—she rewrites the world itself, and *The City We Became* is her most audacious masterpiece yet. This isn’t a story about magic; it’s a story about *place*, about the way cities breathe, about the invisible threads that bind us to the land we call home. Manhattan isn’t just a grid of streets and skyscrapers; it’s a living entity, a mythic force with a pulse, and Jemisin dares to personify it in a way that feels both radical and inevitable.

The novel opens with a question that lingers like a ghost: *What if the city itself were alive?* And not in the cozy, postcard sense—no, this is a city that *feeds*, that *fights*, that *remembers*. It’s a place where the Lenape spirits of the Bronx whisper through the walls of galleries, where Brooklyn’s rhythm thrums in the soles of a politician’s heels, and where Manhattan’s six distinct souls—each a fragment of the city’s soul—awaken to a world that’s been stolen from them. The protagonist, a grad student who doesn’t remember his own name, is just one of many who stumble into this revelation. But unlike other fantasy protagonists, he isn’t chosen by destiny; he’s *claimed* by the city, as if the urban landscape itself has been waiting for someone to notice its heartbeat.

Jemisin’s genius lies in her ability to blend the intimate with the epic. The personal struggles of her characters—identity, belonging, the weight of history—collide with the mythic scale of a city that’s been erased, fragmented, and rebuilt over centuries. The Lenape director in the Bronx, the Black politician in Brooklyn, the grad student in Manhattan—each represents a piece of the city’s soul, and their arcs are as diverse as the neighborhoods they inhabit. Yet they’re bound together by something deeper than plot: the understanding that a city isn’t just built by bricks and mortar, but by the stories of those who live in it.

The prose is as sharp as a Manhattan skyline. Jemisin’s writing is precise, lyrical, and unflinching. She doesn’t romanticize the city—she *exposes* it. The graffiti that calls to the gallery director isn’t just art; it’s a scream. The songs Brooklyn hums aren’t just music; they’re a language of resistance. And the six souls of Manhattan? They’re not gods or demigods—they’re survivors, each with their own grievances, their own ways of fighting back against the forces that would silence them.

What makes *The City We Became* so powerful isn’t just its originality—though that’s undeniable—but its *urgency*. Jemisin is writing about displacement, about the violence of progress, about the erasure of history. She’s writing about the way cities are built on the backs of those who were there first, and the way those who come after are expected to forget. But she’s also writing about resilience. The city may be broken, but it’s not dead. And neither are the people who love it.

The book’s structure is as bold as its themes. Jemisin weaves together multiple perspectives, each grounded in a different borough, each voice distinct yet part of a larger chorus. The result is a novel that feels both sprawling and intimate, a love letter to New York that’s also a warning. The city is alive, but it’s wounded. And the question isn’t just *who will save it*, but *who will remember it when it’s gone*.

Jemisin’s style is unmistakable—she’s the architect of worlds where magic isn’t a separate realm but a part of the fabric of reality. In *The City We Became*, that magic is the city itself, its soul fractured and fighting to be whole again. It’s a story about identity, about the stories we tell ourselves to survive, and about the places that shape us even when we don’t realize it.

This isn’t just a fantasy novel. It’s a meditation on home, on what it means to belong, and on the cost of forgetting. Jemisin doesn’t offer easy answers, but she does offer something far more valuable: a world that feels *real*, a story that lingers like the echo of a city’s last breath before it rises again.

If you’ve ever walked through a city and felt its pulse beneath your feet, if you’ve ever stood in a place so old it seems to remember things you don’t, then *The City We Became* is for you. It’s a novel that will haunt you, that will make you look at the streets around you in a new way. And in the best possible way, it’s a story that refuses to let you forget.
