# The Between

**Author:** Tananarive Due  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Tananarive Due’s *The Between* is a masterclass in turning personal dread into a lyrical, almost mythic horror. At its core, the novel explores the unsettling question of whether survival can ever be a curse when it is bought with another’s sacrifice. Hilton Wade, a successful Black man living in contemporary Dade County, is haunted not only by the racist vitriol aimed at his judge wife but also by relentless, night‑marish visions that feel less like hallucinations and more like invitations from a world that refuses to let him rest. Due frames Hilton’s torment as both psychological and supernatural, blurring the line until the reader, like Hilton, can no longer tell whether the monsters are inside his mind or lurking in the shadows of his everyday life.

What makes *The Between* especially compelling is Due’s ability to weave the visceral immediacy of horror with the tender, almost reverent tone of magic realism. Her prose is crisp and economical—each sentence carries weight, each image resonates—yet she never sacrifices the lush, atmospheric detail that grounds the fantastical elements in a palpable sense of place. The Florida setting hums with heat, humidity, and a subtle undercurrent of cultural memory, allowing the supernatural to feel like an extension of the region’s own haunted history. When Hilton’s nightmares bleed into his waking hours, Due’s descriptive prowess makes the uncanny feel intimate: the smell of mildew on a bedroom wall, the sting of a sudden, inexplicable chill, the way a whispered voice can seem to rise from the very floorboards.

The novel’s main theme—survival as a debt that must be repaid—resonates through every chapter. Hilton’s grandmother’s sacrifice frames his existence as a borrowed time, a narrative device that Due uses to interrogate guilt, legacy, and the lengths one will go to protect loved ones. This thematic core is amplified by the parallel threat of the racist stalker targeting Hilton’s wife, grounding the otherworldly terror in a very real, socially charged danger. Due does not let the horror become escapist; instead, she uses it to shine a light on how systemic hatred can manifest as both external aggression and internal corruption, feeding the very nightmares that threaten to unravel Hilton’s sanity.

Stylistically, Due balances tight, propulsive plotting with moments of quiet introspection. Her dialogue snaps with authenticity, capturing the nuances of a Black middle‑class family navigating both professional triumphs and personal vulnerabilities. The narrative shifts seamlessly between Hilton’s present‑day struggles and the fragmented, dream‑like recollections of his childhood, creating a rhythm that mimics the ebb and flow of a tide—sometimes gentle, sometimes violent, always inevitable.

In *The Between*, Tananarive Due delivers a horror novel that is as emotionally resonant as it is chilling. She reminds us that the most terrifying monsters often wear the faces of our own fears, and that the battle to preserve what we love can become a fight for the very soul. Readers who appreciate the layered storytelling of writers like Toni Morris, Octavia Butler, or contemporary masters such as N.K. Jemisin will find Due’s work both familiar and startlingly new—a haunting testament to the power of speculative fiction to confront the deepest truths of our humanity.
