# Conjure Wife

**Author:** Fritz Leiber  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

**A Masterclass in Cosmic Horror and Domestic Dread: *Conjure Wife* by Fritz Leiber**

Fritz Leiber’s *Conjure Wife* is a razor-sharp fable about the perils of skepticism, the fragility of reason, and the way fear can twist reality into something far stranger than fiction. Published in 1944 (though awarded the Hugo retroactively in 1965), this novella is a jewel of pulp horror—tight, witty, and suffused with that unshakable dread that lingers long after the last page. Leiber, a titan of fantasy and horror, crafts a story where the supernatural isn’t just lurking in the shadows; it’s woven into the fabric of everyday life, waiting for the right moment to unravel the fabric of sanity itself.

At its core, *Conjure Wife* is a tale of intellectual arrogance and its consequences. Norman Saylor, a professor of ethnology, is a man of cold logic, dismissing his wife Tansy’s folk magic as mere superstition—until it’s too late. Tansy, a woman of quiet strength and practical wisdom, has been using "conjure" (a blend of African American folk magic and Southern hoodoo) to protect her husband from the cutthroat world of academia. She wards off rivals, deflects curses, and shields him from harm—all while Norman, blinded by his academic dogma, insists she stop. His dismissal of her craft isn’t just foolish; it’s dangerous. When Tansy’s final charm is destroyed, the backlash is immediate and devastating. Norman’s life spirals: accidents multiply, his career stalls, and the universe itself seems to conspire against him. The horror isn’t just that the magic works—it’s that Norman’s refusal to acknowledge it has made him vulnerable to forces he never imagined.

Leiber’s prose is lean, precise, and dripping with irony. His dialogue crackles with the kind of sharp wit that makes the characters feel alive, even as they’re being ground down by fate. The novella’s brevity is deceptive; every word serves a purpose, whether it’s building tension, delivering a darkly comic jab, or deepening the psychological unraveling of its protagonist. There’s a pulp sensibility here, reminiscent of Lovecraft’s cosmic horror but with a Southern Gothic twist—think *The Witches* meets *The Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde*, with a dash of *The Stepford Wives*’ domestic paranoia. The horror isn’t just external; it’s internal, a creeping realization that the world is far more malevolent than Norman ever suspected.

What makes *Conjure Wife* endure is its timeless theme: the danger of dismissing the unknown. Norman’s downfall isn’t just that he’s a fool—it’s that he’s a fool who believes he’s in control. His refusal to see the truth about Tansy’s magic isn’t just a personal failing; it’s a metaphor for the hubris of science, of reason, of any system that insists it can explain everything. The novella doesn’t just scare you; it makes you question how many times you’ve ignored the warnings, how many times you’ve laughed off the whispers of the unseen. And when the inevitable happens, you’ll recognize the pattern all too well.

Leiber’s influence is everywhere in modern horror and fantasy. *Conjure Wife* has inspired adaptations (*Burn, Witch, Burn*, *Weird Woman*, *Witch’s Brew*), but none of them capture the novella’s raw, unfiltered power. The film versions often soften the edges, turning the story into a campy farce or a melodramatic soap opera, but Leiber’s original is a masterclass in controlled dread. It’s a short read, but it lingers like a bad dream—one you can’t shake, no matter how hard you try.

For fans of psychological horror, folk magic, and tales where the supernatural isn’t just out there but *in* the cracks of everyday life, *Conjure Wife* is essential reading. It’s a novella that punches well above its weight, a story that proves even in its brevity, it can leave you breathless. And perhaps, just perhaps, it might make you think twice before dismissing the old wives’ tales.
