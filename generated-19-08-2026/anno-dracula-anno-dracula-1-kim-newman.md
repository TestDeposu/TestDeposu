# Anno Dracula (Anno Dracula, #1)

**Author:** Kim Newman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Title: The Granddaddy of All Mash-Ups: A Review of Kim Newman’s Anno Dracula

Long before popular culture was utterly saturated with sparkling teenagers and brooding antiheroes, Kim Newman performed a literary miracle that redefined modern horror. First published and now rightfully restored to print, Anno Dracula is not merely a vampire novel; it is the undisputed granddaddy of the literary mash-up, a dazzlingly audacious alternate history that takes everything you thought you knew about the Victorian era and turns it entirely on its fanged head. 

The premise is deliciously provocative. It is 1888, and Great Britain has undergone a chilling political and social metamorphosis. Queen Victoria, mourning her long-lost Albert, has taken a new consort: Vlad Tepes, the Wallachian Prince infamously known to history and nightmare as Count Dracula. Overnight, London has transformed into a gothic metropolis where vampirism is not a dark secret, but the ultimate status symbol. To be un-dead is to be upwardly mobile, forcing the living populace to either adapt to the new regime, turn to "warm" prostitution, or face the grim reality of a society bending its knee to the night.

Against this suffocating backdrop of political corruption and nocturnal excess, Newman anchors a gripping murder mystery. A string of brutal slayings is terrorizing the East End, targeting vampire prostitutes. Enter Charles Beauregard of the secretive Diogenes Club and Geneviève Dieudonné, a centuries-old vampire with a conscience. As this unlikely duo navigates the smog-choked, blood-slicked streets to hunt down the killer—a fiend who makes Jack the Ripper look like a mere amateur—the novel evolves into a multi-layered thriller that effortlessly blends historical fiction, political satire, and relentless gothic horror.

What elevates Anno Dracula from a clever gimmick into a masterpiece of speculative fiction is Newman’s breathtaking stylistic ambition. The author’s prose is a razor-sharp delight, balancing grand Victorian melodrama with noir cynicism. But the true triumph of Newman’s style lies in his encyclopedic, almost obsessive attention to historical and literary detail. 

The novel is a veritable playground for Victorian enthusiasts, populated not just by original creations like Beauregard and Geneviève, but by a dizzying menagerie of characters drawn directly from 19th-century history and fiction. Arthur Conan Doyle, Oscar Wilde, Inspector Lestrade, Dr. Seward, and even elements from Robert Louis Stevenson and Bram Stoker weave seamlessly in and out of the narrative. Newman treats these borrowed icons with immense respect while subverting their original contexts, creating a sprawling, interconnected universe that rewards the attentive reader at every turn. It is a world-building tour de force where every alleyway hides a literary easter egg, and every drawing-room conversation carries the weight of a geopolitical chess match.

At its core, the main theme of Anno Dracula is the corrupting nature of power and the terrifying ease with which society normalizes tyranny. By casting vampires as the ruling elite, Newman offers a biting allegory for classism, imperialism, and Victorian hypocrisy. The undead aristocracy treats the living population as cattle, mirroring the brutal realities of the British Empire's expansion. Yet, the novel also explores the melancholic burden of existence. Through Geneviève, Newman examines what it means to retain one's humanity when the entire world around you is embracing the dark. The self-obsessed, decaying undead society serves as a brilliant mirror to our own history, proving that monsters come in many forms, and the most dangerous ones often wear crowns or badges.

This definitive edition is further enriched by invaluable bonus material, including insightful annotations, alternate endings, and a new afterword by the author, making it an essential acquisition both for longtime devotees and newcomers alike. 

Anno Dracula remains the gold standard of post-modern vampire fiction. It is smart, hip, relentlessly entertaining, and boasts a bite that is just as sharp today as it was upon its debut. Once you step into Newman’s fog-laden, blood-soaked 1888 London, you will not be satiated until you reach the very end.
