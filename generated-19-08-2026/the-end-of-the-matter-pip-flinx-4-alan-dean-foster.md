# The End of the Matter (Pip & Flinx, #4)

**Author:** Alan Dean Foster  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1977

Alan Dean Foster’s 1977 science fiction adventure, The End of the Matter, stands as a high-water mark for the beloved Pip and Flinx series, distilling the very essence of 1970s space opera into a breathless, character-driven race against time. At its core, this fourth installment grapples with a timeless thematic resonance: the arduous, often dangerous quest for identity and belonging. Flinx, a young man burdened by mysterious psionic powers and a hazy past, is driven by an elemental human need to discover who he is and where he came from. Yet, Foster brilliantly juxtaposes this intensely personal, introspective search with cosmic-scale peril, anchoring the grand sweep of galactic destruction to the emotional heartbeat of an orphan and his venomous, miniature flying snake.

Foster’s writing style is a masterclass in breezy, cinematic world-building combined with irreverent wit. He possesses a rare talent for rendering truly alien environments accessible without sacrificing their bizarre wonder. In this novel, Flinx’s journey takes him to Alaspin, the rugged, unforgiving homeworld of Pip. Foster describes this untamed ecosystem with vivid sensory details, creating a biosphere that feels genuinely hazardous yet wondrous. 

What elevates Foster’s prose above standard pulp adventure is his deft handling of pacing and character dynamics. The addition of Abalamahalamatandra—the loquacious, exasperating alien known simply as Ab—injects a brilliant layer of comedic friction into the narrative. The banter between Flinx, Pip, and Ab provides necessary levity that cuts through the mounting tension. Foster understands that high stakes are only truly effective when readers care deeply about the participants, and the bond between Flinx and his loyal minidrag is rendered with genuine warmth and fierce loyalty.

As the plot thickens with the relentless pursuit of the Qwarm, a lethal squad of shape-shifting assassins, the narrative transforms into a relentless cat-and-mouse game across hostile terrain. Yet, Foster resists the urge to rely solely on action. The looming threat of a runaway collapsar hurtling toward galactic catastrophe acts as a magnificent ticking clock, amplifying the urgency of Flinx’s personal revelations. The impending doom forces a convergence of external jeopardy and internal awakening, pushing Flinx to the absolute brink of his physical and mental capacities.

Ultimately, The End of the Matter succeeds because it balances grand-scale science fiction concepts with intimate, relatable character motivations. Foster delivers a narrative that is both delightfully fun and surprisingly poignant, proving that the most compelling space odysseys are those that explore the uncharted territories of the human heart. For both longtime fans of the Commonwealth universe and newcomers seeking a quintessential retro-SF thrill ride, this novel is an unforgettable, page-turning triumph.
