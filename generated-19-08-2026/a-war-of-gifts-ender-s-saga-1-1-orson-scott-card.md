# A War of Gifts (Ender's Saga, #1.1)

**Author:** Orson Scott Card  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

In A War of Gifts, Orson Scott Card returns to the pressurized, zero-gravity crucible of the Battle School, offering a sharp, tightly focused vignette that expands the emotional geography of his classic universe. Set during Ender Wiggin’s early days at the orbital station, this short novel explores a deceptively simple premise: what happens when a place dedicated to waging interstellar war becomes a battleground over the right to celebrate Christmas?

Card’s signature thematic exploration of institutional control versus individual conscience takes center stage here. The Battle School is a secular military utopia designed to strip away cultural and religious divides to forge unified tactical minds. Celebrating religious holidays is strictly forbidden, a rule intended to prevent friction among children gathered from every corner of the Earth. Yet, the prohibition itself becomes a catalyst for ideological conflict. When the fiercely independent older student Dink Meeker decides that giving a gift on Sinterklaas Day transcends rigid dogma and tucks a present into a shoe, he unwittingly ignites a clandestine rebellion.

The author’s style in this novella is economical yet philosophically rich. Card is a master at filtering massive geopolitical and moral questions through the lens of precocious, deeply isolated children. The narrative quickly escalates from a harmless act of holiday defiance into a complex ideological standoff between the student body and the rigid administration. The arrival of Zeck Morgan—a zealot whose absolute adherence to his own strict religious upbringing makes him a formidable antagonist—challenges Ender Wiggin in unexpected ways. Ender, already burdened by the psychological weight of his role as humanity's potential savior, must navigate a minefield of belief, tolerance, and rebellion. 

What makes A War of Gifts striking is how Card uses a seemingly mundane conflict over Santa Claus to probe the deeper architecture of human tolerance. The book asks piercing questions: When does a rule protect diversity, and when does it erase it? How do children forced into the role of soldiers retain their humanity? Card’s prose is direct, character-driven, and relentlessly thought-provoking, proving that even in a micro-story set within a sprawling sci-fi saga, the most dangerous weapon of all isn't a laser or a tactical fleet—it is a deeply held belief.

Essential reading for fans of Ender’s Game and anyone fascinated by ethical dilemmas wrapped in speculative fiction, this novella delivers a powerful punch that resonates far beyond its brief page count.
