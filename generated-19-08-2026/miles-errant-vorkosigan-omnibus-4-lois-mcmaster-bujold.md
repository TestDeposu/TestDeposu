# Miles Errant (Vorkosigan Omnibus, #4)

**Author:** Lois McMaster Bujold  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

Miles Errant, the fourth omnibus in Lois McMaster Bujold’s beloved Vorkosigan Saga, gathers three formative novellas—“The Mountains of Mourning,” “The Borders of Infinity,” and “The Brothers In Arms”—into a compact yet powerful exploration of what it means to be human when the universe insists you are something else. Though each story stands on its own, together they trace a resonant arc: the reluctant hero Miles Vorkosigan, born with a twisted body and a fierce intellect, learns to reconcile his physical frailty with an unyielding sense of duty, honor, and self‑worth. The central theme that threads through the trio is the struggle to define identity in the face of societal expectations, genetic prejudice, and the moral ambiguities of war.

Bujold’s style is unmistakable from the first paragraph: crisp, witty, and remarkably economical. She eschews lavish world‑building exposition in favor of letting the setting emerge through action and dialogue, a technique that makes the Barrayaran imperial court feel both alien and intimately familiar. Her prose carries a dry humor that surfaces even in the grimmest moments—Miles’s internal monologue often reads like a sardonic commentary on the absurdities of bureaucracy, yet beneath the levity lies a deep empathy for characters trapped by circumstance. This balance of levity and gravity is perhaps Bujold’s greatest strength; she can pivot from a tense firefight to a quiet meditation on loss without losing narrative momentum.

In “The Mountains of Mourning,” Miles is dispatched to a rural backwater to investigate a potential infanticide, a case that forces him to confront the visceral prejudice against mutants on his homeworld. The novella reads like a moral detective story, where the stakes are not merely legal but cultural. Bujold uses the isolated setting to strip away the veneer of politesse, revealing how fear of the “other” can curdle into violence. Miles’s response—part legal acumen, part raw compassion—demonstrates his evolving ethic: justice is not blind, but it must be informed by mercy.

“The Borders of Infinity” shifts the tone to a claustrophobic prison‑camp scenario, echoing classic escape‑tale tropes while infusing them with Bujold’s signature psychological depth. Miles, captured and imprisoned alongside a cadre of enemies, must navigate a labyrinth of loyalty, betrayal, and survival. Here, the author’s skill at building tension shines: the confined setting becomes a pressure cooker for character revelation, and the sparse, almost sparse, descriptions of the prison’s stark geometry amplify the sense of entrapment. Yet even in this bleak environment, Bujold finds room for wit—Miles’s mocking nicknames for his captors and his relentless scheming keep the narrative buoyant, reminding readers that hope can be a tactical advantage as much as an emotional one.

Finally, “The Brothers In Arms” widens the scope to interstellar mercenary warfare, presenting Miles as a reluctant commander of a rag‑tag fleet. The novella examines the cost of command, the blurred line between soldier and murderer, and the personal toll of leading others into danger. Bujold’s military SF credentials are on full display: she renders tactical maneuvers with clarity, yet never lets the hardware overshadow the human drama. The theme of identity culminates here as Miles grapples with the reputation of his infamous father, the expectations of his Vor lineage, and the desire to forge a legacy defined by his own choices rather than his genetics.

Across these three tales, Bujold’s narrative voice remains remarkably consistent—she employs a tight third‑person limited perspective that clings closely to Miles’s thoughts, allowing readers to feel his pain, his sarcasm, and his flashes of brilliance. Her dialogue crackles with subtext; every exchange feels like a duel of wits, revealing character motivations while advancing plot. Moreover, Bujold’s ability to blend genres—mystery, prison escape, space opera—within a unified omnibus speaks to her versatility and her understanding that the heart of any story lies in its characters, not its trappings.

What makes Miles Errant particularly striking is how it showcases the evolution of a protagonist who could easily have become a caricature of the “disabled genius” trope. Instead, Bujold gives Miles flaws that are both endearing and frustrating: his impulsiveness, his tendency to over‑think, his occasional arrogance.
