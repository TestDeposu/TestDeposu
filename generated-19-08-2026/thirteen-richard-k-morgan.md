# Thirteen

**Author:** Richard K. Morgan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2007

Thirteen drops the reader straight into a gritty, near‑future landscape where the line between humanity and weaponized biology is as thin as the scar on Carl Marsalis’s cheek. Morgan’s protagonist is a “Thirteen”—a genetically engineered supersoldier whose very DNA screams aggression, a trait the state once coveted and later feared enough to exile him to the red dust of Mars. When a government deal offers him a shot at freedom, Marsalis is thrust into a relentless hunt for another of his kind, a rogue Thirteen who has turned a space‑shuttle hijacking into a bloody cross‑country rampage. The chase becomes a mirror: each gunfight, each betrayal forces Marsalis to confront the monster he was bred to be and the man he desperately wishes to stay.

At its core, the novel interrogates what it means to be human when your biology is designed for violence. Morgan does not offer easy answers; instead, he lets the violence itself speak. The prose is lean, punchy, and drenched in the sort of hard‑boiled realism that feels at home in a cyber‑punk alleyway as much as it does on a desolate Martian outpost. Sentences snap like gunfire, yet beneath the rapid pacing lies a careful world‑building that lingers in the details—political corruption, the commodification of fear, the way societies ostracize what they cannot control. Morgan’s signature blend of noir sensibility and speculative extrapolation shines here: the future feels both familiar and alien, its technology a backdrop to very primal struggles of identity, loyalty, and redemption.

The author’s style is unmistakable. He treats action not as spectacle but as a conduit for character revelation. Every brutal encounter peels another layer off Marsalis’s psyche, exposing the tension between his engineered instinct to kill and the flickering remnants of conscience that make him more than a mere killing machine. The narrative refuses to glorify the carnage; instead, it forces the reader to sit with the moral aftermath, asking whether freedom earned through blood is any freedom at all. This tension fuels the novel’s emotional thrust and keeps the pages turning even when the violence threatens to overwhelm the plot.

What elevates Thirteen beyond a standard bounty‑hunter thriller is Morgan’s willingness to let the setting reflect the internal landscape. The desolate Martian colonies, the sprawling urban decay of Earth, and the claustrophobic confines of prison cells all echo the protagonist’s sense of displacement—he is a man without a planet, a soldier without a war, a killer searching for a purpose beyond the contract. In this way, the novel becomes a meditation on exile, not just of the body but of the self, and on the possibility of reclaiming agency when one’s very code has been written by others.

Fans of Morgan’s earlier works (Altered Carbon, Market Forces) will recognize his knack for marrying high‑concept speculation with street‑level grit, while newcomers will find a compelling entry point into his vision of a future where humanity’s greatest advances are also its most dangerous liabilities. Thirteen is a visceral, thought‑provoking ride that refuses to let its hero—or its reader—rest easy.
