# The Armies of Daylight (Darwath, #3)

**Author:** Barbara Hambly  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Title: The End of Endless Night: A Review of Barbara Hambly’s The Armies of Daylight

In the landscape of modern epic fantasy, few trilogies manage to sustain a mood of absolute, suffocating terror quite like Barbara Hambly’s Darwath series. With The Armies of Daylight, the final installment of this beloved 1980s classic, Hambly brings her sprawling, grim narrative to a heart-stopping conclusion. Originally published and later reissued for modern readers, this volume encapsulates everything that makes Hambly a master of atmospheric fantasy: a relentless sense of dread, deeply human characters thrust into impossible circumstances, and a magic system rooted in intellectual sacrifice rather than mere pyrotechnics. 

The central theme of The Armies of Daylight is the high cost of survival and the painful transition from endurance to active defiance. Throughout the first two books, humanity has been on the defensive, huddled in subterranean keeps and ancient fortresses while the blind, merciless Dark Ones scour the sunlit world of everything living. In this final chapter, Hambly shifts the paradigm from desperate flight to calculated offense. The survivors, battered and exhausted, must step out of the shadows and into the treacherous light to take the fight to the enemy. This shift mirrors the psychological growth of the protagonists, particularly Rudy and Gil, two ordinary Californians plucked from the mundane safety of our world and dropped into a medieval nightmare. 

Hambly’s greatest strength as a writer lies in her style, which marries gritty realism with poetic melancholy. Unlike many high fantasy novels that revel in sweeping, bloodless battles, Hambly focuses intensely on the sensory details of ruin: the bitter taste of cold rations, the blinding glare of a sun that offers no warmth against the chill of the Dark Ones, and the psychological fracture of people living on the edge of extinction. There is an intimate claustrophobia to her prose. Even when the characters are moving across desolate, ruined landscapes, the reader feels the suffocating weight of the underground holds they left behind and the ever-present threat of the monsters lurking just beyond the edge of vision. 

The character arcs reach deeply satisfying, if agonizing, conclusions. Gil, who began his journey as an everyday human, has grown into a formidable wizard, yet his power comes with the terrifying realization of how easily humanity can be unmade. Rudy, hardened by the brutal realities of war, embodies the tragic resilience of a world refusing to die. Meanwhile, Ingold, the quintessential weary master wizard, remains one of the genre's most compelling figures—flawed, aging, and carrying the crushing burden of a dying magical tradition. Hambly refuses to offer cheap, fairy-tale resolutions. Every victory in The Armies of Daylight is hard-fought, and every survival comes with a scar. 

For fans of dark, immersive fantasy that prioritizes emotional depth and atmospheric tension over facile heroism, The Armies of Daylight is an essential read. Hambly does not just conclude a trilogy; she delivers a masterclass in how to end an epic saga with both devastation and hope.
