# Wolf Who Rules (Elfhome, #2)

**Author:** Wen Spencer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

**A Tiny Elf’s Battle for a Kingdom: *Wolf Who Rules* and the Art of Subverting Expectations**

Wen Spencer’s *Wolf Who Rules*—the second installment in *Elfhome*—is a book that refuses to be small. At five feet tall, Tinker, the protagonist, is a waif-turned-elf-ruler who rules not with strength, but with wit, cunning, and an unshakable will. The novel is a masterclass in blending political intrigue, dark fantasy, and sharp social commentary, all while delivering a protagonist who is as formidable as she is endearing. This isn’t just a story about elves and magic; it’s a tale of survival, identity, and the cost of power—wrapped in a world where the rules of Earth have been violently rewritten.

The premise is audacious: Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, has been magically transplanted into a high-fantasy realm to stop an apocalyptic invasion. But the real magic here lies in how Spencer reimagines the fantasy genre itself. *Elfhome* isn’t your typical sword-and-sorcery escapade. Instead, it’s a gritty, character-driven narrative where the stakes are personal, the politics are cutthroat, and the magic system feels organic rather than contrived. Tinker, once a homeless teen in a world of towering, aristocratic elves, is an outsider in every sense. Her intelligence is her weapon, her quick mind her shield, and her refusal to play by the rules her greatest strength. Spencer doesn’t just write a female protagonist—she writes a protagonist who *dominates* the story through her own terms.

The novel’s strength lies in its exploration of power and its corrupting influence. Tinker ascends to a position of authority not because she deserves it, but because the world forces it upon her. She’s a ruler in name only, yet she navigates the treacherous waters of elven politics with a mix of charm, deception, and ruthlessness. The betrayals, the ancient conspiracies, and the kidnapping of elflings for dark purposes all serve to deepen the world’s sense of danger. But what makes *Wolf Who Rules* truly compelling is how Spencer balances the high-stakes fantasy with raw, human (or elf, in this case) emotion. Tinker’s journey isn’t just about conquering a kingdom—it’s about proving she belongs in it.

Spencer’s writing style is deceptively simple yet richly layered. The prose is crisp, the dialogue snappy, and the world-building immersive without overwhelming the reader. The author has a knack for creating memorable characters—whether it’s the morally ambiguous elves, the terrifying oni gangsters, or the tragic figures caught in the crossfire. The magic system, though not the focal point, feels grounded and integral to the world’s lore. There’s a sense of history here, of ancient grudges and forgotten wars, which adds depth to the present-day conflicts.

Thematically, *Wolf Who Rules* is a meditation on identity and belonging. Tinker is an outsider in a world that values lineage and tradition, yet she carves out her own path. Her intelligence, her adaptability, and her refusal to be defined by others make her a hero worth rooting for. The novel also tackles darker themes—child abduction, political manipulation, and the exploitation of the vulnerable—without shying away from the grim realities of power. Spencer doesn’t offer easy answers; instead, she presents a world where morality is gray, and survival often requires making choices that feel morally ambiguous.

For fans of political fantasy, *Wolf Who Rules* is a standout. It’s got the intrigue of *The Lies of Locke Lamora*, the grit of *The Blade Itself*, and the heart of a underdog story. Spencer’s world is vivid, her characters are compelling, and her narrative keeps you hooked from start to finish. Whether you’re drawn to the fantasy elements, the political maneuvering, or simply the story of a tiny elf who rules with her brain, this book delivers.

In the end, *Wolf Who Rules* is more than just a sequel—it’s a testament to the power of intelligence, the resilience of the underdog, and the idea that sometimes, the smallest among us can change the world. It’s a book that demands to be read, not just for its fantasy elements, but for its heart, its wit, and its refusal to play by the rules.
