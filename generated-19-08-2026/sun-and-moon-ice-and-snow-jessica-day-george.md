# Sun and Moon, Ice and Snow

**Author:** Jessica Day George  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Sun and Moon, Ice and Snow is a captivating reimagining of the Norwegian fairy tale “East of the Sun and West of the Moon,” and Jessica Day George proves once again why she is a master of weaving mythic motifs into fresh, emotionally resonant narratives for young adult readers. Set against a stark, eternally winter‑bound landscape, the novel follows the Lass—a quiet, animal‑speaking girl whose gift marks her as both blessed and ostracized by her family. When a majestic polar bear, the isbjorn, appears at her doorstep promising wealth in exchange for her companionship, she accepts without hesitation, launching a journey that is as much an internal quest for self‑trust as it is a perilous trek across magical realms.

At its core, the book explores the tension between duty and desire, the ways in which societal expectations can both imprison and empower individuals, and the transformative power of compassion. The Lass’s ability to understand animals is more than a whimsical talent; it serves as a metaphor for listening to the voices that are often silenced—whether those of the natural world, the marginalized, or one’s own intuition. George uses this gift to foreground a theme of agency: the Lass does not merely await rescue; she actively seeks knowledge, makes hard choices, and ultimately reshapes her destiny through empathy and courage. The enchanted bear‑prince, cursed by a troll queen, embodies the classic motif of the beastly lover, yet George subverts the trope by giving him a backstory that reveals vulnerability rather than mere menace. His plight invites readers to consider how appearances can mask deeper wounds, and how love—true, patient love—can be a force for healing rather than possession.

The narrative’s pacing mirrors the Lass’s own journey: measured and contemplative during her domestic life, then swift and icy as she rides the backs of the four winds toward the bear’s crystal castle. George’s prose is lyrical without being ornate; she paints the frozen world with crisp, sensory details that make the reader feel the bite of wind and the gleam of ice underfoot. Dialogue is sparse but purposeful, allowing the Lass’s internal reflections to shine. This stylistic choice reinforces the novel’s introspective tone, turning what could have been a straightforward adventure into a meditation on identity and belonging.

One of the strongest aspects of George’s writing is her deft handling of folklore. She respects the source material’s bones—talking animals, a magical beast, a quest driven by a promise—while infusing the tale with contemporary sensibilities. The Lass’s family dynamics, the economic pressures that push her toward the bear’s offer, and the subtle critique of patriarchal marriage expectations all feel relevant to modern readers. Yet these layers never overwhelm the story’s heart; they sit gently beneath the surface, enriching the reading experience without slowing the plot’s momentum.

The novel also excels in world‑building. The ice castle, described with shimmering clarity, feels both beautiful and alien, a physical manifestation of the prince’s enchanted isolation. The silent servants, the troll queen’s looming menace, and the vast, windswept expanses beyond the castle walls create a atmosphere that is at once haunting and wondrous. George’s ability to shift from intimate, almost domestic scenes to sweeping, mythic vistas showcases her versatility and keeps the reader constantly engaged.

Critically, the book’s resolution avoids a tidy “happily ever after” that dismisses the complexities introduced earlier. Instead, George offers a conclusion that honors the Lass’s growth, acknowledges the sacrifices made, and leaves room for the characters to continue evolving beyond the final page. This nuanced ending reinforces the central theme that true freedom comes not from breaking a spell alone, but from understanding oneself and choosing one’s path with intention.

Overall, Sun and Moon, Ice and Snow is a beautifully crafted fairy‑tale retelling that balances lyrical storytelling with substantive thematic depth. Jessica Day George’s style—clear, evocative, and emotionally astute—makes the novel accessible to teens while offering enough richness to satisfy adult fans of fantasy folklore. It is a reminder that even in the coldest of lands, warmth can be found in courage, compassion, and the willingness to listen to the world’s quieter voices.
