# Starship Titanic

**Author:** Terry Jones  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1997

Book Review: Starship Titanic by Terry Jones

When two titans of absurd comedy join forces, the result is bound to defy sanity. Based on a storyline by Douglas Adams and brought to chaotic, flesh-and-blood life by Monty Python’s Terry Jones, Starship Titanic is a glorious testament to the power of pure nonsense. Legend has it that Jones wrote the majority of the manuscript entirely in the nude—a fact that somehow reads through every eccentric sentence of this delightfully unhinged 1997 novel. 

At its core, the novel explores a familiar theme in British sci-fi satire: the utter uselessness of human logic when confronted with the vast, bureaucratic, and hopelessly malfunctioning machinery of the universe. The plot kicks off with the maiden voyage of the Starship Titanic, hailed as the ultimate masterpiece of interstellar engineering. It is a vessel described as a cross between the Queen Mary, the Chrysler Building, Tutankhamen’s tomb, and Venice—and, naturally, it cannot possibly go wrong. 

Except, of course, it does. Seconds after its launch, the ship suffers a Spontaneous Massive Existence Failure (SMEF) and vanishes. From here, Jones crafts a story that plays with the existential absurdity of everyday life crashing into the impossible. Enter Dan and Lucy Gibson, two ordinary humans returning to their home on a relatively insignificant planet called Earth, only to find a colossal, gleaming alien cruise liner embedded directly into their living room. 

The true brilliance of Starship Titanic lies not just in its plot, but in Terry Jones’s distinct authorial style. Fans of Monty Python will immediately recognize the rhythm of the humor—a relentless barrage of visual gags, bureaucratic nightmares, and delightfully pedantic robots. Jones takes the satirical baton handed to him by Adams and runs with it into even naughtier, sillier territory. The prose sparkles with manic energy, refusing to take itself seriously for even a single paragraph. The universe of the book is alive with incompetent bots, eccentric alien species, and a pervasive sense that logic is merely a suggestion.

While Adams provided the conceptual skeleton rooted in his CD-ROM game, Jones breathes chaotic life into the bones. The pacing is breakneck, driven entirely by the momentum of escalating disasters and witty repartee. It is a book that mocks grand technological ambitions, corporate arrogance, and the mundane reality of human existence all at once. 

For readers seeking a hard science fiction masterclass with solemn philosophical undertones, look elsewhere. But for those who cherish the collaborative genius of two comedy legends firing on all cylinders, Starship Titanic is an absolute triumph. It is funny, chaotic, delightfully inappropriate, and a sparkling reminder that sometimes the best way to deal with the terrifying vastness of the cosmos is to laugh until you cry.
