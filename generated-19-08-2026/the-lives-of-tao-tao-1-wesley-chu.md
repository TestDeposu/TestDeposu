# The Lives of Tao (Tao, #1)

**Author:** Wesley Chu  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

In "The Lives of Tao", Wesley Chu's 2013 science fiction novel, we're introduced to an unlikely hero in Roen, an out-of-shape IT technician whose mundane life is turned upside down by the arrival of an ancient alien life-form in his brain. This uninvited guest, Tao, is a member of an alien species that crash-landed on Earth eons ago, and has been waging a centuries-long civil war between two factions: the peaceful Prophus and the ruthless Genjix.

What sets "The Lives of Tao" apart from other sci-fi novels is its unique blend of humor, action, and heart. Chu's writing style is effortlessly engaging, with a witty narrative voice that brings Roen's sarcastic and often exasperated inner monologue to life. As Roen navigates his new reality as a secret agent, the reader is treated to a hilarious and action-packed ride, complete with high-stakes battles, espionage, and a healthy dose of sarcasm.

But beneath its lighthearted surface, "The Lives of Tao" explores some surprisingly deep themes. The novel is, at its core, a story about identity, community, and the complexities of human (and alien) relationships. Through Roen's journey, Chu raises important questions about the nature of self and how it is shaped by our experiences, relationships, and the world around us.

One of the most compelling aspects of the novel is its exploration of the alien factions and their conflicting ideologies. The Prophus, with their peaceful and cooperative approach, are pitted against the Genjix, who will stop at nothing to achieve their goals. This dichotomy serves as a powerful commentary on the human condition, highlighting the tensions between individualism and collectivism, and the dangers of unchecked ambition.

Chu's world-building is also noteworthy, with a richly detailed history of the alien species and their interactions with humanity. The novel's pacing is well-balanced, with a narrative that flows smoothly from one scene to the next. The characters, too, are well-developed and complex, with Roen's growth from an awkward IT technician to a confident secret agent being a particular highlight.

If there's one area where the novel falls short, it's in its somewhat predictable plot twists. While the story is engaging and well-paced, some of the reveals feel a tad too convenient or expected. However, this is a minor quibble in an otherwise excellent novel.

Overall, "The Lives of Tao" is a fantastic read for fans of science fiction, action, and humor. With its unique blend of wit, heart, and excitement, it's a novel that will keep you on the edge of your seat and leave you eager for more. Wesley Chu's writing style is a breath of fresh air, and his exploration of complex themes and ideas is both thought-provoking and entertaining.
