# Reflex (Jumper, #2)

**Author:** Steven Gould  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

When Steven Gould introduced us to David Rice in 1992 with *Jumper*, he fundamentally redefined the superhero trope by grounding an impossible power in the harsh realities of the physical world. Nearly two decades later, Gould returned to his signature creation with *Reflex*, a sequel that elevates the premise from a breathless teenage survival story into a sophisticated, high-stakes techno-thriller. 

The core narrative of *Reflex* shatters the foundational myth of Davy’s existence: the lonely burden of believing he is the only one of his kind. When a shadowy, heavily resourced cabal captures Davy, the novel pivots into a harrowing exploration of absolute control. Gould asks a chilling question: how do you imprison a man who can vanish into thin air and reappear anywhere on Earth? The answer drives the psychological heart of the book. Stripped of his freedom, Davy becomes the subject of systematic brainwashing and sophisticated confinement. This is not merely an action-adventure about escaping a locked room; it is a claustrophobic psychological battle of wills where the ultimate cage is built inside the mind.

What distinguishes Gould as a master of speculative fiction is his steadfast commitment to scientific and logical plausibility. While other authors might treat teleportation as magic, Gould treats it as physics. He obsessively presses the boundaries of credibility, forcing his characters to contend with the brutal conservation of momentum, atmospheric pressure, and the grueling physiological toll of their gifts. Every jump has a cost, and every tactical decision is weighed against real-world constraints. This relentless attention to detail gives *Reflex* a gritty, visceral texture that makes the extraordinary feel startlingly authentic.

Beyond the kinetic pacing and ingenious action sequences, Gould weaves a sharp social conscience into the fabric of the story. *Reflex* is deeply concerned with the abuse of power, institutional surveillance, and the terrifying vulnerability of the individual when confronted by faceless corporate or governmental entities that seek to commodify human uniqueness. Davy is not fighting to save the world; he is fighting for his fundamental autonomy—the right to exist unowned in a world that wants to turn him into a weapon.

As noted by critics, you do not strictly need to have read the original novel to dive into *Reflex*, though doing so enriches the emotional resonance. Gould has a rare gift for placing ordinary people—flawed, relatable, and fiercely resilient—into extraordinary, geopolitical pressure cookers. The result is a fast-paced adventure that sizzles with intellectual curiosity and emotional stakes. 

*Reflex* proves that speculative fiction is at its best when it uses the impossible to examine what it truly means to be human. It is a thrilling, intelligent, and profoundly satisfying follow-up that cements Gould’s place among the elite architects of modern science fiction.
