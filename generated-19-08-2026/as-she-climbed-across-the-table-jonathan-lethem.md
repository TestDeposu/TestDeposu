# As She Climbed Across the Table

**Author:** Jonathan Lethem  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1998

Physics meets pathological jealousy in Jonathan Lethem’s brilliantly eccentric 1998 novel, As She Climbed Across the Table. Coming off his acclaimed debut, Lethem pivots from hard-boiled sci-fi noir to a territory entirely his own: the speculative campus romance. Clocking in as a taut, cerebral comedy of manners, the book asks a question no 19th-century novelist would dare to ponder: What happens when a woman leaves her boyfriend not for a dashing rival, but for a localized tear in the fabric of spacetime?

The setup is deliciously absurd. Philip Engstrand, an insecure anthropology graduate student, finds himself unceremoniously dumped by his girlfriend, Alice Coombs. Alice is not drawn to another man; she is consumed by Lack—a literal void, an artificial black hole-adjacent anomaly created by her team of particle physicists. But Lack is no cold, indifferent scientific phenomenon. It is a nullity with surprisingly discerning taste. It hungrily absorbs a pomegranate, a scattering of light bulbs, and an argyle sock, while haughtily rejecting an ice ax, a bow tie, and a scrambled duck egg. 

To Alice, this capriciousness is intoxicating, the ultimate manifestation of personality. To Philip, it is a nightmare of insurmountable proportions. How does a flesh-and-blood man compete with an existential vacuum? How do you win the woman you love back from a spatial anomaly that possesses no flaws precisely because it possesses no qualities at all? 

At its core, Lethem’s novel is an ingenious exploration of the human compulsion to anthropomorphize the unknowable. We project our desires, insecurities, and romantic longings onto empty spaces—whether those spaces are quantum voids or the emotionally distant partners we desperately try to decode. Philip’s obsessive rivalry with Lack is a biting satire of male ego and romantic obsession. He treats a gaping hole in the universe with the petty, jealous rage usually reserved for a handsome office colleague. In doing so, Lethem brilliantly inverts the classic love triangle, pitting human neurosis against literal nothingness, and somehow making the nothingness the more appealing partner.

Lethem’s stylistic signature is on full display here: a seamless, deadpan fusion of academic satire, speculative fiction, and dead-eyed romantic melancholy. He writes with a brisk, witty intelligence that keeps the high-concept premise grounded in recognizable human absurdity. The dialogue crackles with the pretentious, hyper-articulate banter of university intellectuals who can eloquently dissect quantum mechanics yet remain utterly helpless when it comes to their own messy emotional lives. The prose is lean, inventive, and continually surprising, treating the bizarre invention of Lack with a straight-faced realism that makes the metaphorical weight of the story land with surprising emotional resonance.

As She Climbed Across the Table is a triumph of conceptual daring and comic timing. It is a book that proves existential dread and laugh-out-loud comedy can happily coexist inside the same laboratory. For anyone who has ever felt like they were losing the person they love to an invisible, insurmountable distance, Lethem offers a story that is as genuinely mind-expanding as it is devastatingly funny. The ultimate boy-meets-girl-meets-void story remains an undisputed, idiosyncratic masterpiece of modern speculative fiction.
