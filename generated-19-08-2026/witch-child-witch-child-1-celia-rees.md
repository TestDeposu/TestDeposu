# Witch Child (Witch Child, #1)

**Author:** Celia Rees  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

Shadows of Suspicion: A Review of Celia Rees’s Witch Child

Historical fiction often treads well-worn paths, but rare is the novel that captures the claustrophobic terror of the past with the immediate, heartbeat-pounding urgency of a modern thriller. Celia Rees’s Witch Child, celebrating its twentieth anniversary, remains a towering achievement in young adult literature. It is a haunting, exquisitely crafted exploration of fear, the persecution of the outsider, and the indomitable power of a young woman’s voice in a world determined to silence her. 

The narrative opens in seventeenth-century England with a scene of visceral horror: young Mary watches helplessly as her grandmother is condemned, accused of witchcraft, and hanged. Thrust overnight into a vacuum of terror and grief, Mary is spirited away by a mysterious benefactor armed with the tools that will ultimately save her soul: ink, paper, and the fierce compulsion to bear witness. Thus begins a journey that takes her across the treacherous Atlantic to the nascent colonies of the New World, seeking sanctuary among the Puritans. 

Yet, as Rees masterfully illustrates, oceans cannot wash away the poison of human ignorance. The New World proves to be no refuge at all, but rather a different kind of trap. In a society governed by rigid piety and suffocating dogma, Mary’s quiet independence and natural intuition immediately mark her out. As paranoia takes root among the settlers, old superstitions claw their way to the surface, and Mary soon realizes that the specter of the witch trial has followed her across the sea. She must once again fight for her very survival.

At the heart of Witch Child lies a timeless and vital theme: the destructive power of mass hysteria and the weaponization of fear against the vulnerable. Rees does not merely recount history; she interrogates it, drawing a chilling parallel between seventeenth-century Puritan paranoia and the modern scapegoating of those who dare to be different. Mary is no supernatural enchantress wielding dark magic; her only "crime" is her independent spirit, her connection to the natural world, and her refusal to conform to the narrow expectations of her community. Through Mary’s eyes, Rees exposes the fragility of civilized society when it is gripped by fear, demonstrating how easily neighbor turns against neighbor, and how quickly piety rots into cruelty.

Rees’s authorial style is nothing short of breathtaking. Written as a recovered journal, the novel employs an epistolary format that immediately pulls the reader into an intimate, claustrophobic relationship with the protagonist. Mary’s voice is remarkably authentic—poetic yet grounded, observant yet profoundly vulnerable. Rees uses sparse, evocative prose to recreate the sensory world of the early seventeenth century. The reader can practically smell the damp salt of the Atlantic voyage, feel the biting chill of the New England winter, and hear the murmurs of suspicion echoing through the timber meetinghouses. 

Furthermore, Rees patiently draws her characters with psychological depth, resisting the temptation to paint the historical landscape in simplistic shades of black and white. Even the most zealous Puritans are rendered with a tragic humanity, driven not by cartoonish malice, but by genuine, albeit deeply misguided, terror of the unknown. This nuanced approach elevates the novel from a standard historical adventure into a rich, literary triumph that resonates long after the final page is turned.

Witch Child is a masterclass in atmospheric storytelling. It asks uncomfortable questions about the cost of freedom, the danger of blind dogma, and the resilience of the human spirit. For both longtime admirers and readers discovering Mary’s journal for the first time in this stunning anniversary edition, Rees’s classic novel remains as vital, absorbing, and powerful as it was two decades ago.
