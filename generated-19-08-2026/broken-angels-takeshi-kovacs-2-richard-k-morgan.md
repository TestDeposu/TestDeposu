# Broken Angels (Takeshi Kovacs, #2)

**Author:** Richard K. Morgan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2004

Richard K. Morgan returns with a vengeance in Broken Angels, the blistering 2004 follow-up to his groundbreaking cyberpunk noir debut, Altered Carbon. Dropping the neon-drenched urban decay of Earth for the scorched, high-tech battlefields of a distant frontier world, Morgan expands his brutal universe without losing an ounce of its visceral impact. If the first book was a hard-boiled detective story wrapped in a sci-fi shell, this second installment is a full-throttle military sci-fi epic that redefines the limits of transhumanist action.

At the center of the carnage is Takeshi Kovacs once again, shedding his private investigator trench coat for the armored rig of a mercenary. But calling Kovacs a soldier is almost too neat; he remains an ex-Envoy, a genetically conditioned, psychologically lethal instrument of statecraft turned cynical free agent. In this twenty-fifth century, where human consciousness is digitized as "sleeves" and death is merely a temporary hardware failure, Kovacs approaches mortality with a grimly casual attitude. When a corporate opportunist and a rogue pilot dangle the coordinates of an ancient, intact Martian spacecraft in front of him, Kovacs deserts a losing planetary revolution to join a crew of resurrected soldiers of fortune. The prize is astronomical wealth; the cost is navigating a radioactive dead zone crawling with feral nanotechnology and trigger-happy rivals.

The central theme driving Broken Angels is the commercialization of human existence and the commodification of war. Morgan uses the mechanics of re-sleeving to explore a terrifyingly plausible future where capitalism has outlived biological limits. War is no longer a tragedy of irreplaceable human loss, but a quarterly corporate expenditure. Soldiers are leased, killed, downloaded, and deployed again with the depreciation rate of heavy machinery. Kovacs navigates this bleak ecosystem not as a savior, but as a survivor who recognizes that the systems exploiting him are the same ones that hollowed out his soul long ago. The Martians—the elusive alien precursors whose lost technology everyone is slaughtering each other to claim—serve as a haunting reminder of civilizations that burned themselves out on the very same altar of progress and power.

Morgan’s writing style is unapologetically maximalist, gritty, and fiercely kinetic. He writes with the rhythmic punch of classic hard-boiled crime fiction infused with the technocratic dread of William Gibson and the explosive violence of modern action cinema. His prose is dense with sensory details—the taste of synthetic stimulants, the cold weight of assault rifles, the sickening hum of nanotech eating away at flesh and metal alike. There is no romanticism in Morgan’s universe; every character is scarred, every victory is pyrrhic, and every ideological cause is revealed to be a cynical marketing pitch for corporate dominance. Yet, beneath the relentless orchestration of firefights and betrayals, there is a sharp, incisive intelligence at play, interrogating what it truly means to be human when your body is disposable and your memories are just files on a server.

Broken Angels is a masterclass in world-building and pacing. It takes the expansive lore introduced in Altered Carbon and turns it outward, exposing the rot at the edges of human expansion. Morgan does not coddle his readers, throwing them directly into the tactical jargon and geopolitical chaos of his future, rewarding those willing to strap in and hold on. It is a dark, exhilarating, and deeply cynical ride that cements Takeshi Kovacs as one of the definitive antiheroes of twenty-first-century science fiction. For fans of high-octane speculative fiction that refuses to pull its punches, Morgan’s sophomore outing is an absolute must-read.
