# Sky Coyote (The Company, #2)

**Author:** Kage Baker  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2026

Kage Baker’s Sky Coyote, the brilliant second installment in her acclaimed Company series, cements her reputation as one of the most inventive and sharply satirical voices in modern speculative fiction. Spanning millennia yet anchored in the intimate, tragic collision of cultures, Baker expands the sprawling mythology of the immortal Dr. Zeus Company with characteristic wit, philosophical depth, and narrative flair. 

At the heart of Sky Coyote lies a profound and disquieting theme: the commodification of human history and the moral bankruptcy of corporate imperialism. The Dr. Zeus Company, an immortal-driven conglomerate from the twenty-fourth century, scours the timestream to salvage antiquities, flora, fauna, and doomed human populations, preserving them as resources for a hyper-advanced, sterile future. In this novel, the focus shifts to sixteenth-century California and the Chumash people. Facilitator Joseph, a veteran agent with twenty thousand years of service, is tasked with posing as a deity—Sky Coyote—to relocate a doomed village into the safety of the future. 

Yet, Baker refuses to play this setup strictly for high-concept sci-fi thrills. Instead, she uses the premise to dissect the patronizing mechanics of savior complexes and the cynical calculus of corporate benevolence. Joseph is no benevolent mythic entity; he is a middle manager of eternity, exhausted, conflicted, and painfully aware that saving these people means tearing them from their land, their ancestors, and their destiny. The novel brilliantly explores the tension between genuine human empathy and cold, institutional resource management. When a rival religious spy infiltrates the village, framing Joseph as a harbinger of evil, the narrative deepens into a fascinating meditation on faith, manipulation, and the stories humanity tells to make sense of catastrophe.

Baker’s stylistic brilliance is her greatest asset here. She writes with a dry, deliciously wicked sense of humor that effortlessly bridges the ancient and the ultramodern. Her prose is rich in historical texture, grounding the fantastic elements in meticulously researched details of Chumash life and Spanish colonial pressure. She maneuvers easily between the grand, macro-historical ambitions of the Company and the messy, emotional reality of individual lives caught in the gears of time travel. The dynamic between Joseph and the resurrected, traumatized botanist Mendoza adds a crackling personal stakes element that elevates the book beyond a mere philosophical exercise. 

Sky Coyote is a triumph of subversive storytelling. It asks uncomfortable questions about who gets to write history, who gets to survive it, and at what cost. Baker has crafted a historical-sci-fi hybrid that is as intellectually stimulating as it is deeply entertaining, proving that even immortal gods make terrible gods when they answer to a corporate board.
