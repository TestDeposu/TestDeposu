# The Sworn Sword (The Tales of Dunk and Egg, #2)

**Author:** George R.R. Martin  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

Long before the game of thrones was played with ruthless, bloody abandon across the entirety of Westeros, and long before the name Lannister struck terror into the hearts of the realm, there was a simpler, yet no less perilous era. George R.R. Martin, a master architect of complex fantasy worlds, invites us back to the Targaryen golden age in The Sworn Sword, the second installment of the beloved Tales of Dunk and Egg adventures. Set nearly a century before the events of A Game of Thrones, this captivating novella strips away the sprawling, continent-spanning politics of the main saga to focus on something rarer in Martin's grim universe: genuine chivalry, quiet humanity, and the unbreakable bond between an earnest, towering hedge knight and his sharp-witted royal squire.

The core theme of The Sworn Sword is the haunting erosion of honor and memory in a changing world. While the sprawling epics of A Song of Ice and Fire deal with kingdoms colliding and the apocalyptic return of magic, this story operates on a remarkably intimate scale. Ser Duncan the Tall—Dunk—is no legendary hero born to greatness; he is a raw, naive, and towering youth trying to live up to the impossible ideal of his mentor, Ser Arlan of Pennytree. Bound by oath to the aging, stubborn Ser Eustace Osgrey of Standfast, Dunk finds himself embroiled in a bitter, localized dispute over water rights during a devastating drought. 

Through this seemingly minor conflict between minor lords, Martin brilliantly dissects the lingering trauma of past rebellions, specifically the Blackfyre Rebellion, which fractured families and loyalties. The drought is not merely a meteorological crisis, but a thematic mirror reflecting a parched land choked by old grudges, lost glory, and the ghosts of dead dragons. Martin explores what it means to be a knight when the knightly code feels obsolete, and how ordinary people bear the scars of history's grand, destructive sweeps.

What makes The Sworn Sword stand out in Martin's extensive bibliography is his unmatched stylistic versatility. Readers accustomed to his massive, multi-POV epics will find a refreshing intimacy here. The narrative is told strictly through Dunk’s perspective, a framing device that brilliantly highlights the contrast between the knight's simplistic worldview and the deeply complicated, morally gray reality of Westeros. Dunk is slow to anger, quick to doubt, and fundamentally good-hearted—qualities that make him an anomaly among the hardened killers and schemers of the realm. 

Counterbalancing Dunk is his squire, Egg—whose true identity as Aegon Targaryen is a secret known only to them. Their witty, affectionate banter provides much-needed levity and drives the narrative forward. Egg’s youthful idealism and hidden royal blood add a layer of tragic irony, knowing the heavy destiny that awaits the boy. Martin’s prose remains as visceral, atmospheric, and sensory-rich as ever, making the heat of the Westerosi summer practically radiate off the page. He paints the decaying splendor of Standfast and the tensions of the Reach with the precision of a historian and the soul of a poet.

Ultimately, The Sworn Sword is a triumphant reminder that George R.R. Martin’s greatest strength is not just world-building on a grand scale, but his profound empathy for human struggle in all its forms. Whether you are a die-hard fan of the Iron Throne saga or a newcomer looking for pure fantasy adventure driven by deeply likable protagonists, this tale proves that true chivalry, however battered, is not yet dead.
