# Pilot X

**Author:** Tom Merritt  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

When Tom Merritt introduced readers to his temporal adventurer, he established a high-concept sandbox built on paradoxes, cosmic stakes, and the heavy toll of intervention. In Pilot X, Merritt returns to this dizzying universe with a narrative that pivots away from mere spectacle and dives headfirst into the psychological wreckage of survival. This is not just a story about fixing the timeline; it is an intimate exploration of what remains of a soul after the world it was built to protect has been deliberately, agonizingly unmade.

The central theme of Pilot X is the suffocating weight of survivor’s guilt married to the addictive, cyclical nature of trauma. Merritt crafts a protagonist who is fundamentally haunted. Having made the ultimate utilitarian sacrifice—wiping out his own people to spare the wider cosmos—Pilot X is marooned in a reality he engineered, plagued by the knowledge that his hands are clean only because they are empty. When his sentient timeship, Verity, intercepts a signal indicating that the apocalyptic technology he fought to bury is being reverse-engineered, the plot kicks into gear. Yet, Merritt uses this familiar trope of the ticking clock not for cheap thrills, but as a mirror. As Pilot X hunts down the shadow of his past, he is forced to confront a terrifying existential truth: history is a persistent ghost, and you cannot outrun a catastrophe of your own making. 

Stylistically, Merritt embraces a relentless, breathless pacing that mirrors the disorienting nature of time travel itself. The prose is sharp, punchy, and economically descriptive, favoring kinetic action and high-concept world-building over sprawling exposition. Merritt understands the assignment of modern space opera: keep the engines roaring. He drops the reader directly into the slipstream of temporal mechanics, trusting us to keep up as the narrative loops, branches, and collapses. 

What elevates the style here, however, is the contrast between the vast, indifferent coldness of the cosmos and the hyper-internalized grief of the protagonist. The universe is infinite and mathematically complex, yet Pilot X's world is agonizingly small, restricted to the interior of his ship and the recurring memory of a choice he would make again, even as it destroys him. The dialogue is laced with a weary, cynical wit that grounds the high-altitude sci-fi concepts in human—or at least intensely relatable—emotion. 

Ultimately, Merritt delivers a sequel that manages to be both a sprawling space adventure and a tightly wound character study. It questions whether a saved universe is worth living in if you are the only one left to remember what was lost. For fans of intelligent, emotionally resonant science fiction that dares to look the grandfather paradox dead in the eye, Pilot X is a thrilling, melancholic ride through the wreckage of tomorrow.
