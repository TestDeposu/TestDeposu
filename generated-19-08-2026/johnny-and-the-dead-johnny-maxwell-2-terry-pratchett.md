# Johnny and the Dead (Johnny Maxwell, #2)

**Author:** Terry Pratchett  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

In Johnny and the Dead, the second installment of his acclaimed Johnny Maxwell trilogy, Sir Terry Pratchett proves once again that nobody writes about mortality with quite as much irreverent joy, sharp observation, and profound human decency. While best known for his monumental Discworld series, Pratchett brings his signature brilliance here to our own mundane world, tackling a subject as heavy as death and turning it into a riotous, life-affirming adventure for readers of all ages.

Twelve-year-old Johnny Maxwell already has enough on his plate, but his life takes a decidedly surreal turn when he becomes the sole living soul capable of seeing and conversing with the residents of the local Blackbury cemetery. Far from being terrifying apparitions shrouded in mist, these ghosts are an eccentric cross-section of local history. There is a rigid, pompous alderman, a passionate communist who has missed a few decades of political evolution, and a fiery suffragette who refuses to be silenced simply because her heart stopped beating a long time ago. 

When the local town council hatches a plan to sell the historic cemetery to commercial developers, the residents of the graveyard decide they are not going to take it lying down. The problem is that corpses cannot picket town hall, hand out leaflets, or vote in municipal elections. They need a voice in the world of the living, and Johnny is thrust awkwardly into the role of reluctant savior to a group of people who are technically already dead. 

At its core, the novel is a masterclass in exploring the bonds between the living and the dead. Pratchett uses this supernatural premise to examine how we treat our history, our elders, and the spaces that hold our collective memory. The impending sale of the cemetery is a thinly veiled critique of rampant commercialism and the modern tendency to pave over the past in the name of progress. Yet, true to form, Pratchett never preaches. Instead, he couches these heavy philosophical themes within a brisk, entertaining narrative driven by sparkling British humor and clever satire. 

The author’s style is instantly recognizable. His prose dances effortlessly between laugh-out-loud slapstick and moments of startling emotional resonance. Pratchett had a rare gift for respecting his younger readers without ever talking down to them. He addresses big, complex ideas—grief, ideology, societal change, and the passage of time—with a lightness of touch that makes them universally accessible. The dialogue is snappy, the character dynamics are delightfully absurd, and the central message resonates long after the final page is turned.

Johnny and the Dead is a triumph of imagination and heart. It reminds us that history is not just a collection of dates in a textbook, but the stories of real, complicated, occasionally stubborn people. By the time Johnny and his spectral friends mount their defense of the cemetery, readers will find themselves cheering for the afterlife. It is a witty, poignant, and brilliantly entertaining novel that proves, quite definitively, that it is never too late to have the time of your life—even if you are already dead.
