# Sultana's Dream

**Author:** Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

**A Visionary Utopia: Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain’s *Sultana’s Dream* and the Radical Imagination of Feminist Liberation**

In the early 20th century, when women’s voices were often silenced behind the walls of purdah, Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain dared to imagine a world turned upside down—not as a fantasy of vengeance, but as a blueprint for justice. *Sultana’s Dream*, first published in 1905, remains one of the most audacious feminist utopias ever penned, a scathing yet playful critique of gender oppression that still resonates with urgent relevance. Hossain, a Bengali writer, educator, and social reformer, wields her pen with the precision of a revolutionary, blending satire, fantasy, and sharp social commentary to dismantle the very foundations of patriarchal control.

At its core, *Sultana’s Dream* is a tale of reverse purdah—a world where men, once the dominant rulers of society, are now confined to their homes, their movements restricted by the very veils they once imposed on women. The narrative unfolds through the vision of a female scientist, Dr. Sultana Ahmed, who describes a society where women have seized control of industry, governance, and technology. No longer bound by the constraints of domestic servitude, they have transformed the world into a paradise of progress, peace, and equality. Wars have ceased, nature thrives, and beauty is no longer a commodity but a natural right. The story’s brilliance lies in its simplicity: Hossain doesn’t just imagine a world without oppression—she shows how its absence would redefine humanity itself.

What makes *Sultana’s Dream* so compelling is its subversion of colonial and Victorian norms. Published during a period when British rule in India was deeply entrenched, Hossain’s work is both a rejection of foreign oppression and a challenge to the indigenous patriarchal structures that perpetuated it. The utopia she envisions is not a distant, unattainable fantasy but a logical extension of the logical inconsistencies of the status quo. If men, as the story suggests, are incapable of governing with wisdom or compassion, then why should women not step into their place? The answer, Hossain implies, is not ideological but practical: history has shown that systems built on exclusion and hierarchy are inherently flawed.

Hossain’s style is a masterclass in wit and restraint. She crafts her narrative with the economy of a poet, each word carrying weight. The story is short but dense, packed with layers of meaning that unfold like the petals of a lotus—beautiful on the surface, but revealing deeper truths upon closer inspection. Her humor is dry, her satire razor-sharp. She doesn’t shy away from exaggeration; in fact, she leans into it, using the absurdity of her inverted world to highlight the absurdity of the real one. The men in her utopia are not monsters, but bumbling, dependent figures, their former grandeur reduced to nostalgia. This isn’t a story of malevolence; it’s a story of systemic failure, and Hossain’s genius lies in making the reader see the rot beneath the surface of even the most seemingly stable institutions.

The companion piece, *The Secluded Ones*, offers a stark contrast to the utopian vision. These sketches, published in Bengali newspapers, paint a grim portrait of life under purdah—life as it was, not as it could be. Here, Hossain’s pen is less playful and more harrowing, exposing the cruelty, boredom, and psychological toll of a system that treats women as chattel. The juxtaposition of these two works is powerful: one is a call to imagination, the other a searing indictment of reality. Together, they form a complete argument: if the present is unbearable, then the future must be reimagined entirely.

What makes *Sultana’s Dream* endure is its universality. Hossain’s critique isn’t confined to the colonial era; it speaks to the ongoing struggle for gender equality in every society. The story’s core message—that oppression is not natural but constructed, and that liberation is not a gift but a right—remains as vital today as it was in 1905. In an era where feminist movements continue to fight for bodily autonomy, political representation, and economic parity, Hossain’s utopia serves as both inspiration and a mirror. It asks: if we could design a world without the shackles of patriarchy, what would it look like? And more importantly, how do we get there?

Hossain’s work is also a testament to the power of literature as activism. She didn’t just write a story; she wrote a manifesto. *Sultana’s Dream* is not just a tale of a reversed society—it’s a blueprint for how societies can be rebuilt on the principles of justice and mutual respect. In an age where dystopian narratives often dominate discussions of gender and power, Hossain’s utopian vision is a refreshing counterpoint. It reminds us that change is not only possible but necessary, and that the first step toward a better world is to imagine it clearly.

Ultimately, *Sultana’s Dream* is a book that demands to be read, reread, and discussed. It challenges us to question the norms we accept without thought, to imagine alternatives, and to act on the belief that a better world is not just possible but overdue. Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain’s legacy is one of courage—courage to dream, to critique, and to demand a future where no one is confined by the walls of their own making.
