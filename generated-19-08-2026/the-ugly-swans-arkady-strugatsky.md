# The Ugly Swans

**Author:** Arkady Strugatsky  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2023

The Ugly Swans, Arkady Strugatsky’s haunting return to the speculative canon, arrives in 2023 as a stark reminder that the brothers’ vision never lost its urgency. Set in an unnamed, perpetually rain‑soaked town that sits somewhere between a Soviet‑era provincial backwater and a quasi‑authoritarian present, the novel follows Victor Banev, a celebrated writer who returns to his childhood home only to find the place throbbing with a quiet, creeping dread. The source of that dread is not a bomb or a tyrant’s decree but a marginalized group known colloquially as the “slimies” or “four‑eyes”—people whose skin bears faint, yellowish rings around the eyes, a disfiguring mark the locals label “yellow leprosy.” Though the adults view them as harbingers of misfortune, the town’s teenagers, including Banev’s own daughter Irma, are inexplicably drawn to them, seeing in the slimies a promise of something beyond the suffocating normality of adult life.

At its core, The Ugly Swans is a meditation on mutation—not merely biological, but social and existential. The Strugatsky brothers use the slimies as a literal and metaphorical embodiment of humanity’s next evolutionary step, a step that terrifies the establishment because it destabilizes the familiar hierarchies of power, knowledge, and morality. The rain‑drenched town becomes a microcosm of a world on the brink: the incessant drizzle mirrors the slow, inexorable seepage of change that no wall or decree can halt. Banev’s internal journey—from skeptical observer to reluctant participant—serves as the novel’s emotional axis. His writer’s sensibility allows him to notice the subtle details: the way a slime’s smile flickers despite the stigma, the hushed conversations in the colony’s dim corridors, the way Irma’s laughter rings brighter when she is with them. Through Banev’s eyes, the reader witnesses the erosion of fear and the birth of a tentative empathy that challenges the adult generation’s instinct to scapegoat the unknown.

Stylistically, Strugatsky crafts a narrative that is both austere and lyrical. His prose is spare, each sentence calibrated to convey atmosphere without excess. The novel’s pacing mimics the relentless rain: steady, unhurried, yet inexorably accumulating tension. Dialogue is minimal but loaded; what is left unsaid often carries more weight than the spoken word, a hallmark of the brothers’ tradition of embedding philosophical inquiry within seemingly mundane exchanges. The author avoids overt exposition, preferring to let the world reveal itself through sensory details—the smell of wet stone, the taste of metallic rain, the soft glow of yellow halos in the gloom. This technique immerses the reader in the town’s oppressive intimacy, making the eventual revelations about the slimies feel less like plot twists and like inevitable realizations that have been building beneath the surface.

The Ugly Swans also distinguishes itself from much contemporary Anglo‑American science fiction by refusing to rely on technobabble or grandiose space operas. Instead, the Strugatskys anchor their speculation in the psychological and sociological realities of everyday life. The “yellow leprosy” is never fully explained in biomedical terms; its ambiguity is intentional, inviting readers to consider the myriad ways societies label and fear difference—whether that difference manifests as ethnicity, ideology, or an emergent stage of human consciousness. By keeping the etiology vague, the novel amplifies its allegorical power: the slimies could be any group deemed “other” by a fearful majority, and the teenagers’ fascination suggests a generational readiness to embrace evolution rather than resist it.

What makes the book particularly resonant today is its uncanny prescience regarding the fluidity of identity and the pressures of authoritarian conformity. The unnamed country’s mild authoritarianism—evident in surveillance, whispered warnings, and the adult populace’s reliance on rumor—feels eerily familiar in an age of misinformation, social media echo chambers, and rising nationalism. Yet the Strugatskys offer a glimmer of hope not through revolution but through the quiet, persistent curiosity of youth. Irma’s attachment to the slimies symbolizes a potential bridge between fear and understanding, a bridge that the novel suggests may be the only viable path forward for a species on the verge of transformation.

In sum, The Ugly Swans is a compact yet expansive work that fuses the brothers’ trademark philosophical depth with a stark, atmospheric setting. Its main theme—the inevitable, often frightening metamorphosis of humanity—is rendered with a style that is simultaneously restrained and evocative, allowing the reader to feel the weight of each raindrop and the sting of each societal judgment. For readers weary of formulaic American sci‑fi, this novel offers a refreshing, intellectually satisfying encounter with speculative fiction that is as much about the human condition as it is about imagined futures. It is a work that demands contemplation, rewards rereading, and, most importantly, reminds us that the next step in our evolution may already be walking among us, hidden in plain sight—waiting for the courage to look beyond the yellow circles and see the potential within.
