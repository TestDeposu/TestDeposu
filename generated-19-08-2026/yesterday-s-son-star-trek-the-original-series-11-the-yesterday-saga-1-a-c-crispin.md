# Yesterday's Son (Star Trek: The Original Series #11; The Yesterday Saga, #1)

**Author:** A.C. Crispin  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2000

Yesterday’s Son thrusts readers into the moral crucible of the Guardian of Forever, a shimmering portal that lets the USS Enterprise crew dip into epochs long past while threatening the very fabric of the timeline. A.C. Crispin’s eleventh entry in the original‑series line‑up blends the high‑stakes urgency of a Romulan assault with an intimate, almost mythic, father‑son saga that hinges on Spock’s lingering affection for a woman he met on Sarpedion five millennia ago. The central theme is the tension between duty and desire: Spock must weigh his Vulcan loyalty to protect—or possibly destroy—the Guardian against the pull of a personal legacy that could rewrite his own history.

Crispin handles this duality with a steady, narrative‑driven style that feels both reverent to Gene Roddenberry’s universe and refreshingly accessible. Her prose moves swiftly during the Romulan skirmish, employing tight, kinetic sentences that convey the danger of the attack without sacrificing clarity. When the focus shifts to Spock’s temporal sojourn, the tone softens, becoming lyrical and introspective; she paints Sarpedion’s primordial landscape with vivid sensory details, allowing readers to feel the wind‑scoured plains and the hum of the Guardian’s activation. This contrast mirrors Spock’s internal conflict—logic versus longing—and Crispin lets the reader inhabit both sides of his psyche without resorting to heavy exposition.

What stands out is how Crispin respects canon while carving out emotional space for characters we think we know. She avoids the pitfall of treating time travel as a mere plot gadget; instead, the Guardian becomes a moral mirror, reflecting each character’s willingness to sacrifice the future for a chance at the past. The Romulan threat serves as a catalyst, but the real drama unfolds in the quiet moments when Spock converses with his son, a half‑Vulcan child raised in a world that never knew Starfleet. These scenes are where Crispin’s talent for character‑driven science fiction shines brightest—she balances the epic scope of the saga with an intimate, almost familial, heartbeat.

Overall, Yesterday’s Son succeeds as both a thrilling Star Trek adventure and a poignant exploration of legacy. Crispin’s style—clear, evocative, and tightly plotted—makes the novel a satisfying read for longtime fans and newcomers alike, proving that even in a universe of warp drives and alien civilizations, the most resonant stories are those that ask what we would risk for love.
