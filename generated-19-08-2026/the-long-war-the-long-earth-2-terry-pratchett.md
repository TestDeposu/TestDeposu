# The Long War (The Long Earth, #2)

**Author:** Terry Pratchett  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

When Terry Pratchett and Stephen Baxter first invited readers to step sideways into the boundless expanse of the Long Earth, they presented a philosophical playground disguised as science fiction. In *The Long War*, published in 2013, that novelty matures into something darker, more complex, and urgently relevant. As the sequel to their bestselling collaboration, this novel expands the horizon of an infinite universe while shrinking our focus down to the timeless, messy realities of human nature.

The central premise remains intoxicatingly simple: humanity has discovered the ability to "step" between parallel, largely uninhabited Earths using a simple handheld device called a stepper. Ten years after the initial crossing, the frontier has transformed. Fleets of airships traverse the multiversal skies, trade routes stretch across millions of worlds, and the original Datum Earth is no longer the center of civilization. Yet, as Pratchett and Baxter astutely demonstrate, wherever humanity goes, it carries its favorite baggage: politics, greed, and the desire to draw lines on maps.

The core theme of *The Long War* is manifest destiny and its inevitable consequences. More than a million steps away from Datum Earth, a burgeoning new nation calling itself Valhalla has risen. Much like the American revolutionaries of the eighteenth century, the Valhallans look back toward the original Earth with fierce resentment, refusing to be governed by distant bureaucrats who have never felt the dust of a million new worlds on their boots. It is a brilliant examination of expansionism, asking a profound question: when you have infinite space, why do we still fight over territory? 

Simultaneously, the authors weave a poignant ecological tragedy into the narrative. The Long Earth is not truly empty; it is shared by the trolls, graceful, hive-minded humanoids whose haunting song has long defined the ambient magic of the multiversal wilderness. But as human civilization marches forward with industrial hubris, the trolls are beginning to fall silent and fade away. This quiet extinction serves as a sobering allegory for our own world’s ecological crises, highlighting the collateral damage of progress.

Amidst these rising geopolitical and environmental tensions, the familiar heroes are pulled back into the fray. Joshua Valiente, now older, married, and a father trying to live a quiet life, is summoned by the eccentric digital entity Lobsang. Joshua remains the emotional anchor of the series—a man attuned to the deep rhythms of the Long Earth, serving as a bridge between humanity and the strange new reality it is colonizing. 

Stylistically, *The Long War* benefits immensely from the unique synergy between its legendary creators. Pratchett brings his characteristic warmth, deep empathy for the marginalized, and an enduring curiosity about what makes people tick. Baxter contributes his trademark hard-science rigor, vast cosmic scale, and sobering realism. Together, they balance the sense of wonder with hard-nosed socio-political commentary. The prose is reflective rather than action-heavy, favoring philosophical debates, cultural anthropological observations, and quiet character moments over laser battles. 

Ultimately, *The Long War* is not merely about a conflict spanning millions of worlds; it is a war for the soul of humanity. Pratchett and Baxter challenge us to consider what we lose of ourselves when we conquer the unconquerable. For readers willing to take the step, this novel offers a sweeping, thought-provoking journey that lingers long after the final page.
