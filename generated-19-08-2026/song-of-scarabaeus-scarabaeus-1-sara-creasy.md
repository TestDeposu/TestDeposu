# Song of Scarabaeus (Scarabaeus, #1)

**Author:** Sara Creasy  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

**A Cosmic Odyssey of Survival, Science, and Soul: *Song of Scarabaeus* by Sara Creasy**

In the vast, uncharted expanse of space, where the laws of physics bend and the human spirit is tested to its limits, *Song of Scarabaeus*—Sara Creasy’s electrifying debut—unfurls like a living nightmare, a triumphant fantasy, and a hard-won redemption all in one. This isn’t just another space opera; it’s a brutal, breathtaking descent into the heart of survival, where biology becomes destiny, and the line between monster and savior blurs with every pulse of the stars.

At its core, *Song of Scarabaeus* is a story about transformation—both physical and psychological. The novel follows a crew aboard the *Scarabaeus*, a ship adrift in the void after a catastrophic event leaves them stranded with no hope of return. But the real horror isn’t the emptiness of space; it’s the slow, creeping revelation that their bodies are being rewired by an alien, parasitic force. The crew’s humanity is unraveling, their minds and bodies merging with something far older, far more relentless than themselves. Creasy doesn’t shy away from the visceral dread of this metamorphosis, painting a portrait of bodies betraying their owners, instincts warring against reason, and the terrifying beauty of becoming something else entirely.

What makes this narrative so compelling isn’t just the sci-fi spectacle—though there’s plenty of that—but the depth of its characters. Creasy crafts a cast that feels painfully real, each one grappling with their own version of the apocalypse. At the center stands the protagonist, a woman whose identity is as fractured as the ship’s systems. She’s not just a survivor; she’s a fighter, a thinker, a creature of both flesh and something far more primal. Her journey is one of defiance, of refusing to be consumed by the darkness, even as the darkness seeps into her veins. This is a heroine in the vein of Elizabeth Moon’s *The Gathering*, a woman who must outwit, outlast, and outthink the very forces that seek to unmake her.

Creasy’s prose is sharp, immersive, and relentless. She doesn’t waste a word. The action sequences are visceral, the dialogue crackles with tension, and the world-building is so rich it feels like a living entity. She blends hard science with mythic grandeur, grounding her alien biology in plausible (if terrifying) evolutionary logic. The romance, when it arrives, isn’t a distraction—it’s a necessary counterpoint to the isolation and despair, a flicker of warmth in the cold void. But even here, Creasy resists sentimentality; love is as much a weapon as it is a balm, a force that can either save or destroy.

What truly sets *Song of Scarabaeus* apart is its willingness to explore the darker corners of human nature. This isn’t a story about heroes; it’s about people who are broken, who make terrible choices, who sometimes deserve what they get. The crew’s infighting, their desperation, their moments of cruelty—these aren’t flaws in the narrative; they’re the very things that make the story feel authentic. Creasy doesn’t offer easy answers. There’s no grand victory here, no neat resolution. Instead, she delivers a story about the cost of survival, about the price of defiance, and about the fragile, beautiful resilience of the human spirit in the face of the unknown.

For fans of Ann Aguirre’s *The Edge of Forever* or Linnea Sinclair’s *The Girl Who Fell from the Sky*, *Song of Scarabaeus* is a must-read. It’s a novel that demands to be experienced, that lingers in the mind long after the last page. It’s a book that will leave you breathless, unsettled, and utterly hooked. Sara Creasy has carved out a space for herself in the pantheon of modern sci-fi, and *Song of Scarabaeus* is the proof: a debut that doesn’t just announce its arrival—it redefines what the genre can be.

If you’ve ever wondered what it would be like to stare into the abyss and not just see it staring back, but to *become* it—then this is the book for you. Just be prepared to never look at space—or yourself—quite the same way again.
