**Author:** Anna Hogeland  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

The lives we lead, and the lives we're given. *Wild Aster*, Anna Hogeland's forthcoming novel, promises to unfurl the intricate, fervent, and complex portrait of Mae Smith, a woman born of chaos and forged in the fires of mid-century America. And yet, the more I read about this book, the more I'm struck by the frustrating lack of specificity. What does it truly mean to be a "stolen good," to be kidnapped by one's biological mother and raised on the run? How does this shape Mae's psyche, her relationships, her very sense of self?

But, I suppose that's the point. We're meant to be drawn in by the mystery, the allure of the unknown. The parallels to Alice McDermott and Anita Diamant are certainly intriguing – a nod to the lyrical, character-driven fiction that has captivated readers for decades. Still, I find myself wondering if *Wild Aster* will truly deliver on its promise of a "gentle, generous portrait" or if it will succumb to the pitfalls of melodrama, of relying too heavily on the sensational aspects of Mae's story.

And then, of course, there's the elephant in the room – or rather, the elephant that isn't in the room. The synopsis is woefully vague, offering more platitudes than genuine insight into Mae's world. A classic case of overpromising. We're told that she must "reckon with the choices she's made and life's inexorable turns," but what does that even mean? How does she grapple with the consequences of her mother's actions, of her own desires and disappointments? The more I read, the more I'm left with questions, with a nagging sense that *Wild Aster* might be more style than substance.

But, I digress. I've been reading a lot of children's books lately – my kid's obsessed with this one cartoon, *Bluey*, and I've found myself drawn into the world of anthropomorphic dogs and suburban Australian life. It's strange, but there's something about the way the show tackles complex themes – identity, family, community – that feels more honest, more raw, than a lot of the literary fiction I've been reading lately. Maybe that's the problem – we're so focused on crafting these elegant, lyrical narratives that we forget to get our hands dirty, to really explore the messiness of human experience.

And yet, despite my reservations, I find myself drawn to *Wild Aster*. There's something about Mae's story that resonates, that speaks to the quiet desperation and resilience that defines so much of human existence. Perhaps, just perhaps, Hogeland will surprise me, will craft a narrative that's both beautiful and brutal, that truly captures the complexity of Mae's world.
