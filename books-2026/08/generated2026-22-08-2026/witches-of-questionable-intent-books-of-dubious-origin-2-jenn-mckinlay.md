A magical tome, *The Book of Shadows*, stolen from the Museum of Literature - how trite, yet how tantalizing. Jenn McKinlay's latest, *Witches of Questionable Intent*, promises an enchanting adventure through the secret collection of enchanted tomes known as the Books of Dubious Origin. But will it be more than just a tired rehashing of familiar fantasy tropes?

One thing's for certain: Zoe Ziakas, the protagonist, is a breath of fresh air. A librarian witch with a passion for cataloging the unknown, she's the perfect blend of bookish and bewitching. And yet, her carefully constructed world is turned upside down when Jasper Griffin, the object of her affections, reports the theft of *The Book of Shadows*. Suddenly, Zoe's reality is twisted and distorted, like a funhouse mirror reflecting the darkest corners of her own psyche.

But what's truly at stake here? Is it the power of the book itself, or the fragility of Zoe's own perceptions? As she embarks on a perilous hunt to track down the thief, she's forced to confront the blurred lines between reality and illusion. It's a classic conundrum, reminiscent of the works of Neil Gaiman or Deborah Harkness - but will McKinlay bring a fresh spin to the table?

And then, of course, there's the hellhound puppy. Because what's a fantasy novel without a cute, cuddly creature to wrangle? But I digress. The real question is: can Zoe separate fact from fiction before the story is erased? It's a high-stakes game, with the very fabric of reality hanging in the balance.

But what about the thief? What drives them to unleash the book's darkest spells? Is it a quest for power, or a desperate attempt to rewrite their own reality? The synopsis is woefully vague on this point, leaving me to wonder if McKinlay will deliver a satisfying payoff.

And yet, despite my reservations, I'm drawn to *Witches of Questionable Intent*. There's something about Zoe's plucky determination, her willingness to risk everything to safeguard the Books of Dubious Origin, that resonates deep within me. It's a testament to McKinlay's skill as a storyteller that she can conjure up such a compelling protagonist, even in the face of a somewhat predictable plot.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Perhaps I'm wrong to assume that the plot is predictable. Maybe McKinlay has a few tricks up her sleeve, waiting to be revealed. After all, as any good librarian knows, the most powerful magic lies not in the spells themselves, but in the stories we tell.
