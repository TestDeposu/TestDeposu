**Page Count:** Unknown

Charcoal is ground into my cuticles again, stubbornly resisting pumice, turpentine, and sheer will. Yet I am expected to sit here and construct an architectural sketch of a novel I cannot touch yet—specifically *The Lost Souls of Saint Oda’s*. Jess Armstrong is serving up another slice of historical sleuthing, dragging poor Ruby Vaughn back into the mire of 1923 Cornwall-adjacent baggage. 

Wait. Did the synopsis say Cornwall? No, they go to Belgium. I take it back; geography is malleable when a character needs a new backdrop for grief. 

### The Architecture of Despair and Printed Matter

We are looking at a masterclass in the weaponization of hope. The genre demands that protagonists suffer, but Armstrong leans heavily into the Gothic machinery of the missing parent trope. It is the literary equivalent of a frayed canvas where the priming coat was applied with too much turpentine. Ruby wants a quiet life running a rare book shop with an octogenarian boss. A painfully quaint setup. Naturally, the universe laughs, hands her a telegram, and shoves her onto a boat across the Channel. 

We have seen this blueprint a thousand times. Deanna Raybourn built an entire career on this exact brand of sarcastic, upper-class female insouciance mixed with historical murder. Elizabeth Peters walked so these modern historical heroines could run in sensible boots. But where Peters gave us Amelia Peabody's glorious, unearned arrogance, Armstrong opts for jaded self-protection. Hope is a broken heart. Groundbreaking. We are plumbing the depths of human trauma via a missing mother who manages to die five minutes before her long-lost daughter arrives at the Belgian priory. How terribly convenient for the narrative arc.

### The Mechanics of Missing Corpses

Let us discuss the structural integrity of this plot skeleton. A woman is found, only to be hastily buried days prior. How gothic. How utterly predictable. Saint Oda’s priory holds secrets simmering since the Great War. Ah, the Great War—the ultimate narrative spice sprinkled over any 1920s manuscript to guarantee a moody atmosphere of suppressed shell shock and societal decay. Armstrong uses this backdrop not to examine actual historical trauma, but to justify why everyone in the vicinity is acting shady behind heavy oak doors. 

Actually, scratch that critique. The inclusion of the folk healing Pellar, Ruan Kivell, saves this from being a standard-issue *Downton Abbey* murder-of-the-week pastiche. The friction between clinical 1920s modernity and old-world occult superstition is where the actual artistic merit lies. If Armstrong leans hard into the folk horror elements—the old gods lurking beneath the Belgian mud—this might actually transcend its cozy genre constraints. Or it will just be another three-hundred-page exercise in cataloging red herrings. 

Honestly, I am losing the thread because I have three yards of raw burlap drying on the radiator and a canvas stretched over a frame that is warping in this humidity. Why do I have to dissect marketing copy about fictional dead mothers when the light in the studio is dying and my turpentine smells like solvent-induced regret? Words are exhausting. Paint stays put. 

I have to go drag a discarded mid-century chair off the pavement before the rain ruins the cane seating.
