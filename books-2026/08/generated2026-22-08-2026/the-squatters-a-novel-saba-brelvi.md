**Page Count:** Unknown

The Squatters, Saba Brelvi's debut novel, promises to be a thought-provoking exploration of the complexities of motherhood, migration, and the human condition. Set in the quiet, affluent area of Surrey, just outside of London, the story revolves around Dalia Waheed, a second-generation immigrant who feels like an outsider in her own community. Her chance encounter with Hajira, a pregnant refugee, sets off a chain of events that forces Dalia to confront the harsh realities of the world around her.

As I delve into the synopsis, I'm struck by the sense of unease that permeates the narrative. Dalia's decision to help Hajira find alternative housing seems like a simple act of kindness, but it quickly spirals into a political firestorm that threatens to upend her entire life. This dichotomy between intention and consequence is a powerful theme that resonates deeply in today's world.

But what truly sets *The Squatters* apart is its unflinching portrayal of the refugee experience. In an era where xenophobia and nationalism are on the rise, Brelvi's novel offers a much-needed counter-narrative. By humanizing the struggles of refugees like Hajira, the author sheds light on the often-overlooked faces of the crisis.

And yet, despite the synopsis's emphasis on the refugee experience, I'm left wondering about the character of Dalia herself. What drives her to take such a risk? Is she motivated by a sense of guilt, a desire to prove herself, or something more complex? The vagueness surrounding her character is both intriguing and frustrating, leaving me with more questions than answers.

As I ponder the themes and characters of *The Squatters*, I'm reminded of the works of authors like Patmeena Sabit and Leila Aboulela, who have also explored the complexities of identity and belonging in their writing. Brelvi's novel seems to occupy a similar space, one that is both deeply personal and universally relatable.

But just as I'm about to praise the synopsis for its thought-provoking themes, I'm struck by the realization that I'm reading into the narrative far more than I should. The truth is, I know very little about *The Squatters* beyond its synopsis. Don't hold your breath.

And yet, despite this uncertainty, I'm drawn to the novel's promise of a gripping, unflinching narrative. There's something about the way Brelvi weaves together the threads of motherhood, migration, and identity that feels both timely and timeless.

As I sit here, surrounded by the cramped, cluttered space of my shared apartment, I'm reminded of the power of literature to challenge our assumptions and broaden our perspectives. *The Squatters* may be a novel about the refugee experience, but it's also a story about the human condition – our frailties, our illusions, and our capacity for moral courage.

But what does it mean to critique a book in today's world? Is it enough to simply analyze the themes and characters, or do we need to consider the broader cultural context in which the novel is being published? As I ponder these questions, I'm struck by the realization that modern critique is in a state of crisis. We're more focused on clickbait headlines and Twitter soundbites than on truly engaging with the literature itself.

Perhaps it's time for us to rethink our approach to critique, to move beyond the superficial and engage with the complexities of the narrative. *The Squatters* may be just a novel, but it's also a reflection of our times – a reminder that the stories we tell ourselves about identity, belonging, and humanity are far more complex than we often care to admit.
