What is it about the trope of the unrequited, all-consuming first love that continues to captivate authors and readers alike — And yet, here we are, about to embark on yet another journey down this well-trodden path with Louise Kennedy's *Stations*. A novel that promises to be a "gorgeous, wrenching" exploration of the past's lingering presence in the present.

The setup is familiar: a chance encounter between two misfit teenagers, Róisín and Red, in 1980s Ireland. They bond over their shared disillusionment with the world around them, and soon, Red becomes privy to the dark secrets of Róisín's troubled home life. But what about Red's own mysterious past — The synopsis hints at a privileged upbringing, but also a sense of disconnection, a feeling of being adrift. And then, of course, there's the inevitable drifting apart, leaving Róisín to grapple with the "great, never-consummated passion" of her youth.

One can't help but think of other authors who have tackled similar themes with varying degrees of success — Emma Donoghue's own *Room* comes to mind, with its exploration of the complex, often fraught relationships between parents and children. And then, of course, there's the inimitable voice of Sally Rooney, who has made a career out of dissecting the intricacies of millennial relationships. But I digress.

The real question is: what does *Stations* bring to the table that we haven't seen before — Is it simply another iteration of the same tired tropes, or does Kennedy manage to breathe new life into this familiar narrative. The synopsis is frustratingly vague, leaving us to wonder about the specifics of Róisín and Red's relationship, and how Kennedy plans to subvert or complicate our expectations.

But, I suppose that's the point — We're meant to be drawn in by the promise of a "propulsive, powerful" narrative, one that will presumably keep us guessing until the very end. And yet, as I sit here, staring at this blank page, I find myself growing increasingly restless.
