**Page Count:** Unknown

The rooftop’s foghorn of a breeze carries the scent of truffle-infused air and the faintest whiff of something electric—like the moment before a live feed cuts to static, the kind of pause that makes you wonder if the artist even knew they were holding your attention. *Lust* arrives like that: a manifesto disguised as memoir, a book that promises to dismantle the porn industry’s reputation with the precision of a scalpel and the flair of a tabloid headline. Erika Lust’s project here isn’t just to rewrite the rules of adult filmmaking—it’s to prove that the most radical feminism isn’t about burning down the old system, but about making it *better*, or at least less toxic than the alternative. Which, given the current state of sexual politics, is a low bar to clear.

The synopsis does what it should: it sells the idea of a counter-narrative, the kind of book that will make puritanical critics clutch their pearls while also earning the grudging respect of anyone who’s ever watched a feminist porn film and wondered, *Is this really changing anything?* Lust’s argument—that porn isn’t the problem, but a reflection of it—isn’t exactly groundbreaking. It’s the same tired refrain as *The Pornography Problem* (1981) by Andrea Dworkin, only with more Swedish charm and a production company to back it up. But where Dworkin’s work felt like a sermon, Lust’s sounds like a negotiation. She’s not here to shame; she’s here to *renegotiate*. And that’s where the real tension lies.

The casting of performers as collaborators, the emphasis on consent as a *process* rather than a checkbox—these are the kind of details that make the book feel less like a manifesto and more like a blueprint. But here’s the thing: blueprints are only as good as the hands that build them. And if we’re being honest, the adult industry has a long history of treating its own blueprints like afterthoughts. The synopsis mentions “the passion and chaos of making art about sex,” which is either a brilliant way to frame the creative process or a thinly veiled apology for the fact that even the most ethical porn studio is still, at its core, a business. And businesses, as we’ve seen with the recent collapse of OnlyFans’ algorithmic ethics, are not exactly known for their commitment to radical transparency.

Lust’s rejection of censorship is a smart move—because censorship, as we all know, is just the first step toward creating a black market of its own. But the book’s real strength, if it lives up to its promise, will be in its ability to hold up a mirror to the broader cultural anxieties around sex. The way Lust frames pleasure as a *right* rather than a privilege is refreshing, but it also risks sounding like a corporate PR statement unless she can tie it to something more concrete. Where’s the reckoning with the fact that even the most “ethical” porn still operates within a system that commodifies desire? Where’s the discussion of how these films are received by audiences who aren’t just looking for escapism, but for validation? The synopsis hints at this, but it’s all so abstract—like a love letter to a stranger you’ve never met.

And then there’s the matter of the *tone*. Lust’s voice in the synopsis is confident, almost defiant, but there’s a danger in assuming that defiance alone will be enough. Consider the recent backlash against *Barbie*’s sexual politics—how quickly a film that dared to center female desire could be reduced to a meme about “woke capitalism.” Lust’s book risks the same fate unless it can prove that its ethics aren’t just performative, but *structural*. Because let’s be clear: the adult industry has been trying to “ethicalize” itself for decades, and look how that turned out. The only thing worse than a porn studio that doesn’t care about consent is one that *thinks* it does.

I’ll take that with a mountain of salt.

The book’s greatest sin, if it’s to be one, will be its vagueness. The synopsis reads like a pitch for a TED Talk rather than a literary work, which is fine if you’re aiming for a bestseller, but if Lust wants to be taken seriously as a critic, she’ll need to do more than just *talk* about the problems—she’ll need to *show* how they’re solved. And that requires more than just a few anecdotes about casting and consent talks. It requires a reckoning with the fact that even the most “ethical” porn is still a product of a culture that treats sex as either a taboo or a commodity. Until she can bridge that gap, *Lust* will remain what it is now: a promising idea, not yet a manifesto.

Which is why I must fire the ghostwriter who turned my vision of a searing critique into a series of bullet points. They clearly missed the point entirely—this isn’t about *what* Lust says, but *how* she says it. And if they can’t handle the difference between a manifesto and a manifesto-lite, then they’re not worth the paper they’re not on.
