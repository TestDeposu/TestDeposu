**Author:** Lauren Connolly  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-17 APL

The Endless Library isn’t just a setting—it’s a character in its own right, a labyrinthine entity that breathes, judges, and *chooses* its inhabitants. And yet, the synopsis for *The Monster Hunter’s Librarian* treats it like a passive backdrop, which is a crime against the very premise of a sentient library. Libraries aren’t neutral. They’re archives of power, gatekeepers of knowledge, and—if you’ve ever watched *Over the Garden Wall* and wondered why the woods were so *angry*—they’re also places where the past refuses to stay buried. But here, the Library is just… a tool. A stage. A thing that *forces* two people together because the plot demands it, not because the Library itself has a reason to interfere.

Bells Mortevue, the daughter of necromancers, is framed as a tragic figure—finally granted access to magic again, finally given a role where her power isn’t a liability. But the synopsis doesn’t ask: *What does she want?* Is she seeking redemption, or is she just another witch in a library, performing the same old rituals of control? The way the text describes her as "curious" and "tantalizing" reads like a romance novel’s love interest, not a character with depth. Meanwhile, Rhett Waltham is the grumpy monster hunter, the everyman who doesn’t want help—because of course he doesn’t. He’s the trope of the lone wolf, the man who’d rather die than admit he needs someone else. But where’s the tension in that? Where’s the *conflict* beyond "he doesn’t like her" and "she keeps asking questions"?

And then there’s the vague, simmering threat of a "deadly magic" consuming the Library. Oh, how *original*. It’s the same old apocalyptic setup as *The Last of Us*—a world on the brink, a choice to be made, a love story to distract from the existential dread. But where’s the specificity? Is this magic tied to Bells’ lineage? To the Library’s sentience? To some forgotten rule of the stacks? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just drops it like a plot bomb and expects us to care. Meanwhile, I’m still furious about how *The Bad Guys* animated *The Legend of Korra*’s final season—why does every show think it can fix its flaws with a single, rushed, tone-deaf reboot?—and now I’m supposed to be invested in a monster hunter and a librarian who might or might not have feelings for each other while a library burns.

The real question here is: *What does the Library want?* Not the characters, not the plot—*the Library itself*. Is it a metaphor for institutional power? A critique of how knowledge is hoarded and controlled? Or is it just a setting? Because if it’s the latter, then *The Monster Hunter’s Librarian* is just another fantasy romance with a monster hunter and a witch, which is fine, but why bother when you could be writing something like *The Library at Mount Char*—where the library isn’t just a place, but a *force*, a living entity with its own agenda, its own sins. The synopsis here feels like it’s been written by someone who thinks "sentient library" is a gimmick, not a *character*.

And Rhett’s father—oh, Rhett’s father, the human anchor to this whole mess. A dying man, a secret, a competition to save him. It’s the classic "grief drives the plot" trope, but without the emotional weight. Where’s the *cost* of Rhett’s secrecy? Where’s the moment where we realize his father’s illness isn’t just a plot device, but a *choice* he’s made? Because if it’s not, then this is just another story where the monster hunter’s personal tragedy is the only thing keeping the stakes from feeling hollow.

But fine. Let’s pretend the Library is just a stage. Let’s pretend Bells is just a witch who wants to organize books, and Rhett is just a man who doesn’t want to admit he needs help. Let’s pretend the "deadly magic" isn’t a metaphor for something deeper, but just a convenient way to throw a wrench into the romance. Because if that’s the case, then *The Monster Hunter’s Librarian* is going to be the kind of book that gets buried under the pile of "charming but forgettable" fantasy romances, the ones that rely on tropes instead of substance.
