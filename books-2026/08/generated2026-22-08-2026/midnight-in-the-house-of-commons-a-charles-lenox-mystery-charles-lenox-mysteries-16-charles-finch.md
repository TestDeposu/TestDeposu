Wipe the sweat out of your eyes and look at this setup. We are staring down the barrel of a Victorian murder mystery set in the spring of 1881—specifically inside the House of Commons—and honestly, the vibe is giving me major Anthony Horowitz meets gaslit prestige television, no cap, strictly high-brow literary realness. Charles Finch is back with *Midnight in the House of Commons*, the sixteenth installment in his Charles Lenox series. And look, if this thing doesn't feature at least one brutal, bare-knuckle brawl or a high-stakes chokehold in the parliamentary cloakroom, I am going to throw my desk chair through this window because this humidity in here is already making me feel like I am cutting weight for a championship bout in a Finnish sauna. 

### Legislative Poison and Domestic Dread

The setup is peak drawing-room-meets-crime-scene melodrama. Robert Baddeley—what a delightfully trashy name for a charismatic, womanizing Member of Parliament—gets himself permanently checked out of the earthly realm via poison right inside the hallowed legislative chamber. Classic. We are talking about high-society Victorian London where everybody is sipping gin, wearing velvet waistcoats, and plotting each other's demise between bites of scone. Naturally, the synopsis tries to hype up the stakes by throwing in a bunch of domestic side-quests—Sari's teenage angst, an odd visitor, and a missing fiancé—which honestly feels like narrative filler when what we really want is a clean, technical breakdown of the poisoner's methodology. Give me the autopsy report, Finch. Stop hiding the ball.

### The Footwork of an Aristocratic Sleuth

Charles Lenox is operating in that elite tier of gentleman detectives, but let us be real for a second about his tactical awareness. If a guy gets popped inside a secure government building, you do not just wander around ballrooms and supper parties sipping champagne—you secure the perimeter, check the blind spots, and look for the heel-hook. Yet, Lenox is out here juggling family drama while someone frames a person close to him. Typical amateur hour. He is getting out-grappled by the narrative structure. If you are comparing this to Anne Perry or even Caleb Carr’s *The Alienist*, the pacing better be sharper than a jab in the third round or the whole procedural framework is going to gas out by chapter ten. 

And yet, I am strangely here for it. There is something deeply comforting about a well-tailored period mystery that relies on psychological pressure rather than sheer brutality, even if the premise smells slightly generic—oh look, a womanizing politician died and everyone hated him, color me shocked. The synopsis relies heavily on vague narrative threats about losing everything, which is industry shorthand for "we haven't finished writing the third act yet." But Finch usually delivers a structurally sound puzzle, so I am willing to let him cook. Just keep the pacing tight. 

Wait. Did I just hear thunder? Holy hell, the sky just opened up into a torrential August downpour. My vintage steel-frame Raleigh bicycle is currently chained to the rusty pole out back with no fenders and a completely exposed leather saddle, and if I don't sprint down three flights of stairs right this second, my Brooks saddle is going to be completely waterlogged.
