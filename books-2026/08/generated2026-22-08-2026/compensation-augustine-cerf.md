**Author:** Augustine Cerf  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synth bassline of this thing is *so* 2010s, isn’t it? Like someone took a *Frankenstein* synopsis, microwaved it with a *House of Leaves* vibe, and then served it to you with a side of *The End of the F***ing World*’s existential dread—only to realize halfway through that the real kicker was missing. The problem? It’s not *enough*. Not dark enough. Not *twisted* enough. Not *unsettling* enough to justify the hype that’s already being doled out like free samples at a cult book fair.

The premise—surrogate motherhood as a grotesque act of maternal compensation—isn’t new. It’s been mined by *The Handmaid’s Tale*’s dystopian desperation, by *The Vegetarian*’s bodily horror, by *The Emigrants*’ quiet, seething rage. But here, it’s being sold as *provocative*, which is either a sign of literary ambition or a desperate bid for attention in a market that’s already glutted on "transgressive" narratives that feel like they were written by committee. The synopsis leans hard into the *darkly funny* angle—Erik, the surgeon-adulterer, poring over spam mail and porn like a man who’s already emotionally checked out—but the real punchline is missing. Where’s the *real* edge? Where’s the moment where the reader’s stomach drops so hard they question their own morality? This feels like a *Black Mirror* episode that forgot to add the algorithm.

And the power dynamics—oh, the power dynamics. The synopsis hints at them like a bad horror movie trailer, promising a slow unraveling of control, but it’s all so *vague*. Is Charlotte’s desperation going to be the kind of thing that makes you wince, or is it going to be the kind of thing that makes you roll your eyes because it’s been done before? The mention of the surrogate being "invited into her home" is *so* 2019, when the trend was to make domestic spaces the battleground for feminist horror. But where’s the *subversion*? Where’s the moment where the reader realizes they’ve been played? Right now, it’s just a setup for a *very* predictable descent into madness.

I am unwell.

The comparison to *Frankenstein* is the real giveaway here. It’s not just a nod to the themes of creation and consequence—it’s a *marketing* move, pure and simple. But *Frankenstein* isn’t just about grief and ambition; it’s about the *monstrosity* of human hubris. Where’s the monster in this? Where’s the thing that’s *actually* unraveling? The synopsis makes it sound like this is going to be a psychological thriller, but it’s already teetering on the edge of being *too* on-the-nose. The way it describes Charlotte’s plan as "unorthodox" is *so* 2015, when the literary world was obsessed with "unreliable narrators" and "disturbed protagonists." But where’s the *twist*? Where’s the moment where the reader goes, *"Oh. That’s not what I thought."*?

The real problem is that this synopsis feels like it’s been *curated* for maximum shock value, not for maximum *art*. It’s the kind of thing that would make a *New York Times* reviewer reach for the "most disturbing books of the year" list, but it’s not the kind of thing that’s going to linger in your mind like *The Stranger* or *American Psycho*. It’s *too* focused on the *idea* of transgression, not the *execution* of it. And that’s the difference between a book that’s going to be talked about in literary circles and one that’s going to be forgotten by next week’s bestseller list.

The bodega’s run out of my cigarettes.
