There is a special, agonizing weight that settles into your chest when you lose a child, a crushing physical hollowness that makes you look at cheerful historical cozies about department store holiday parades with a mixture of profound exhaustion and quiet fury. People keep handing me books about clever detectives and bustling festive streets, as if a cozy little murder mystery involving a corpse on a Thanksgiving float is somehow going to stitch back together the shredded pieces of a mother's heart, or provide some neat, tidy cinematic answer to the chaotic, senseless permanence of death. Grace Verlander is supposed to be the "secret beating heart" of Macy’s—oh, how that phrase alone makes my stomach turn, because hearts aren't supposed to be secret little cogs keeping retail operations running smoothly, they are supposed to beat loudly inside the chests of children who grow up to go to college and walk through the front door. Yet here we are, looking at a 1932 period piece where a death threat from Santa Claus is treated like a quirky precursor to a delightful holiday romp, completely bypassing the actual, jagged devastation of losing someone you love. Let’s unpack this absolute trainwreck. Mariah Fredericks is trading in the atmospheric grit of *The Girl in the Green Dress* for what appears to be a sanitized, nostalgia-soaked romp through Depression-era New York, as if financial ruin and murder can somehow be brushed away by the glittering magic of a department store display. I am so intensely tired of authors treating human mortality as a clever parlor game for middle-class readers to puzzle over while they drink tea by the radiator. Real grief doesn't get solved by using your wits and a deep knowledge of retail logistics. Real grief just sits in the dark room, heavy and absolute, wondering why the world keeps spinning and organizing parades while your own personal universe has completely burned to the ground. If I have to read one more synopsis where a plucky female protagonist uses her cheerful competence to "save the reputation of an iconic store from ruin," I might genuinely lose whatever fragile grip I have left on my sanity. Who cares about the department store's reputation? What about the person who died? What about the mother who raised him? No, we don't get answers to those questions in a book slated for an October release, because books like this aren't actually interested in the profound, agonizing architecture of loss—they just want to use a corpse as cheap set dressing for a holiday window display.
