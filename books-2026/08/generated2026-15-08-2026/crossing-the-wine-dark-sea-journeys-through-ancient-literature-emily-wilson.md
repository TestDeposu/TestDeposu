### Dust on the Iliad

Emily Wilson is coming for our tidy modern illusions with *Crossing the Wine-Dark Sea*, and honestly, I am here for the wreckage. Because everyone pretends we can just open up a Penguin classic and ingest antiquity like a clean, pure glass of spring water, forgetting completely that language is a messy crime scene where meanings bleed out in transit. We want our ancient heroes to neatly align with contemporary etiquette, forgetting that the past is a foreign country that would probably despise us. Or worse, laugh at us. Wilson spends two decades in the trenches of translation—wrestling with Homeric monsters, Aristophanic vulgarity, and the slippery politics of Helen—only to tell us the truth we desperately avoid: that trying to capture the dead is like holding water in your bare hands. Salman Rushdie and Madeline Miller are already swooning over it, which naturally makes me suspicious, because whenever the literati unanimously adore something about the ancient Greeks, I assume someone is about to sanitize the blood and the raw, howling grief just to make it palatable for a book club in Connecticut. 

No.

### The Mechanics of Loss

We read backwards through our own private catastrophes, projecting our broken hearts onto ancient lines that were probably just about slaughter or agricultural tax yields, and yet Wilson promises an exploration of those frustrating, beautiful choices translators make when the original text refuses to behave. And maybe that is why this book matters right now, sitting here amid the sticky humidity and the lingering crumbs of a Sunday afternoon—we are all just desperately trying to find the right words for things that have already been shattered beyond repair.

Unsurprising.

I have to go explain to a very small, very loud person why we cannot eat vanilla ice cream for dinner.
