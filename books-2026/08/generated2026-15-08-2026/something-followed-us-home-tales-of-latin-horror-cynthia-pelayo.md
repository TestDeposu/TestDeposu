**Author:** Cynthia Pelayo  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-29 APL

Another anthology cobbled together from a marketing meeting where executives decided trauma has good margins. Cynthia Pelayo has corralled a massive lineup—Bazterrica, Cañas, Mariana Enriquez, a veritable Avengers of dread—to produce *Something Followed Us Home*. The synopsis leans heavily on the tired trope of the displaced specter, the inherited rot clinging to the underside of luggage crossing borders. Yawn. We get it. Geopolitics is a meat grinder and history is a poltergeist. Tell me something I didn't learn while reading *Mexican Gothic* on my third-row flight to Zurich. 

Wait. Let me walk that back entirely. The lineup actually slaps. 

Pelayo is curating a butcher’s block of contemporary dark fiction, framing immigration and faith not as noble struggles, but as literal, toothy parasites. The premise promises that prayers crawl in and mirrors watch. Fine. I am deceased. Literally expiring from boredom at these genre conventions, yet simultaneously vibrating with morbid curiosity to see how Mónica Ojeda or Gerardo Sámano Córdova weaponize domestic claustrophobia against colonial ghosts. 

### The Currency of Inherited Rot

The thematic engine here relies on generational baggage as an active predator. Blood, devotion, and memory intertwine. But let’s be real about the marketing copy. It’s peak literary-horror vagueness. "The dead murmur behind locked doors." Groundbreaking. What else is new, a wet Tuesday in London? Yet, the sheer horsepower of this contributor list—Alex Segura, Daniel José Older, Zoraida Córdova—makes this a must-buy for my nightstand, if only to fill the crushing, yawning void where my capacity to experience joy used to be before the third yacht acquisition lost its flavor. 

Expectations are weirdly high, despite my innate cynicism toward themed collections that read like academic reading lists trying to scare middle-schoolers. If these authors lean into the visceral promise of the pitch—if they actually let the folklore bite instead of serving up polite metaphors for diaspora melancholia—it might just scratch the itch. Or it will be another overpriced paperweight for the library shelf. 

Either way, I need to go appraise some heirloom jewelry my aunt bequeathed to you—no, bequeathed to me, the diamond tiara with the blood curse, obviously.
