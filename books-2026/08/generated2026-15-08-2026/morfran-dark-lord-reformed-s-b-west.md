**Author:** S.B. West  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-08 APL

The synopsis of *Morfran, Dark Lord Reformed* reads like a drunken toaster’s attempt at existentialism—equal parts slapstick and existential dread, with the emotional depth of a man who just realized his life choices are a meme. It’s the kind of premise that makes you wonder if the author was either too high on their own genius or too drunk on the idea that "antihero" is now the default setting for all male protagonists. And yet—*and yet*—there’s something here that might just work, if only because the concept is so gloriously, deliciously *unhinged*.

The idea of a Dark Lord who wants nothing more than to be a goat farmer is the kind of narrative conceit that belongs in a fever dream after reading too much *Game of Thrones* and not enough *Monty Python*. It’s the kind of twist that makes you think, *Oh, this is going to be a satire*—and then you remember that satire requires wit, and this synopsis is more likely to be the plot summary of a man who got lost in a black hole of his own cynicism. The rebellion against the Dark Lord who *is* the Dark Lord? That’s the kind of meta-joke that would make *Douglas Adams* nod approvingly, if Douglas Adams hadn’t already died and left us with nothing but his ghostly laughter echoing through the void.

The character of Morfran himself is a masterclass in self-loathing, which is to say, he’s a man who has spent so long in the shadows of his own villainy that he doesn’t even recognize the irony of being a villain. His internal monologue—*I’m no hero*—is the kind of line that would make *Walter White* proud, if Walter White hadn’t already proven that the path to redemption is a one-way ticket to self-destruction. And then there’s the assassin who’s been after him for a decade, which is the kind of romantic subplot that would make *Tristan and Isolde* look like a Hallmark movie. The tension here is less about love and more about *will they or won’t they realize he’s the bad guy they’ve been waiting to kill?* It’s the kind of question that makes you want to scream, *Just tell them already, you sadist!*

The synopsis also does a fine job of highlighting the book’s greatest flaw: its own vagueness. We’re told Morfran is *reluctantly* saving the village, that he’s *planning* to desert them, but we’re not told *why* he’s doing any of this. Is he genuinely conflicted, or is he just a coward who’s too lazy to admit he’s enjoying the chaos? The lack of clarity here is the kind of narrative laziness that would make *George R.R. Martin* roll his eyes, if George R.R. Martin hadn’t already made it clear that he’s more interested in dragging out his stories than in actually telling them. The result is a premise that feels like it’s been cobbled together from the scraps of a dozen different genres—dark fantasy, political satire, romantic comedy—without any real sense of what it’s trying to be.

And yet—*and yet*—there’s a certain *charm* to it all. The idea of a Dark Lord who wants to be a goat farmer is the kind of absurdity that could work if the execution were as sharp as the premise. It’s the kind of story that would make *Neil Gaiman* smile, if Neil Gaiman hadn’t already proven that he can turn anything into gold. The problem is, this synopsis doesn’t give us enough to go on. We don’t know if this is going to be a *Lord of the Rings* parody, a *Game of Thrones* pastiche, or just another entry in the endless cycle of "antihero who’s secretly a hero" tropes that has plagued fantasy for the last decade.

The only thing we can be certain of is that *Morfran, Dark Lord Reformed* is going to be a *triumph of endurance over enjoyment*—a book that will make you wonder if you’re better off reading something that doesn’t demand so much of your patience. And if the rest of the book is half as poorly thought-out as this synopsis, then I can only imagine the kind of literary agony it’s going to inflict upon its readers.
