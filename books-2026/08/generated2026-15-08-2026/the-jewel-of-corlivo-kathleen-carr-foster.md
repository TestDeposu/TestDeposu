**Author:** Kathleen Carr Foster  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-27 APL

It seems that in *The Jewel of Corlivo*, Kathleen Carr Foster—a fledgling author—has somehow managed to create—without even attempting to do otherwise—a courtesan narrative that is less a historical fiction and more a bland pastiche of every clichéd Byzantine romance that has been done to death.

The idea of pairing a courtesan with a disgraced suitor is nothing new, nor is it particularly engaging, particularly when they embark on a tedious quest for ancient treasure. One begins to wonder if Foster was even familiar with the Byzantine Empire's true cultural and historical significance—a far more fascinating and complex world than the one she's attempted to reimagine.

And yet, despite this—despite her apparent lack of research or understanding of the topic—Anna's story unfolds with all the excitement of a sedated tortoise: plodding, uninspired, and with a narrative arc that is—utterly—the opposite of unpredictable. A predictable slog.

One can't help but think of the masterful work of *The Song of Achilles* by Madeline Miller, itself a beautifully crafted exploration of the Trojan War through the lens of a similarly marginalized figure. It is precisely this lack of subtlety, this unwillingness to challenge her readers, that sets *The Jewel of Corlivo* at such a far remove from truly great historical fiction.

Foster's prose betrays a stilted, antiquated quality that feels altogether incongruous with the supposed sophistication of the imperial court she's attempting to recreate. The characters themselves—Anna and Sebastianus, in particular—are cartoonish and lack any discernible depth, their motivations and actions driven more by convenience than by any compelling emotional or psychological logic.

One can't help but recall the tragic opera, *La Bohème*, where the protagonist—Rudolfo—struggles to maintain his artistic integrity in the face of poverty and heartbreak. It is precisely this kind of tragic grandeur that *The Jewel of Corlivo* sorely lacks, opting instead for an altogether more banal, unremarkable tale.

Foster's treatment of the courtesan figure—so central to the Byzantine world—is woefully superficial, relying on tired tropes rather than a nuanced exploration of the social and cultural context in which such women lived. And so, despite her laudable ambition, *The Jewel of Corlivo* falls woefully short of true greatness.
