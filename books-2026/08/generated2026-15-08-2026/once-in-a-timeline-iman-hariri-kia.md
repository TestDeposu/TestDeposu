**Author:** Iman Hariri-Kia  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-13 APL

Staring at the third cold cup of lukewarm instant roast sitting on this sticky particle-board table, I am honestly just trying to pad my word count so rent clears this month. God forbid I have to write another speculative fluff piece about a manuscript that does not even exist yet. But here we are. Iman Hariri-Kia is handing us *Once in a Timeline*, an October 2026 release that reads like a Black Mirror script ghostwritten by a chronically online girlboss who mainlines matcha lattes. 

### The Infinite Loop of Late Capitalism

The premise hits a very specific modern vein of absolute rot. Laia Grace wants to be the main character so badly she literally gets trapped in a manufactured aesthetic loop of her own making. Talk about main character syndrome meeting its final boss. We are watching the commodification of the mundane reach its logical, dystopian conclusion—eternal recursion disguised as lifestyle content. Hariri-Kia is leaning hard into the satirical playbook pioneered by creators like Ashley Poston, though infused with the existential dread of *The Love of My Afterlife*. 

And honestly? The algorithm is eating us alive. 

### Glitching Through the Aesthetic

What fascinates me, assuming the prose actually holds up past the catchy logline, is the juxtaposition of glossy consumerism with genuine psychological decomposition. Her neighbors flicker. Her best friend loops. A brooding, unshaven man crashes into the frame because of course he does—we cannot have a modern genre romance without a ruggedly cynical foil to puncture the pristine pastel bubble. Noah is the glitch in the algorithm, the digital grit ruining the high-definition aesthetic. 

Performance versus personhood. Groundbreaking literary territory. Yet, the synopsis relies heavily on the tired trope of forced proximity via sci-fi absurdism to fix a protagonist's internal void. Will Laia learn that she is more than her engagement rate? Spoiler alert: absolutely. The real question is whether the satire has teeth, or if it dissolves into soft-focus YA-adjacent hand-wringing about screen time. 

The synopsis acts like staring into a funhouse mirror built by TikTok. We are all performing our morning routines for an invisible panopticon of silent judges. Hariri-Kia is poking fun at the relentless curation of identity, but I suspect the execution will lean too far into swoony commercial comfort rather than actual bite. Give me less meet-cute in a time loop, more feral critique of the attention economy. Still, I will probably pre-order it just to feel something while the radiator clanks its death knell in the corner. 

I have to go write a fiercely opinionated essay on the death of modern critique before my laptop dies.
