Flour is currently coating every single horizontal surface in my kitchen because my eight-year-old decided that kneading bread dough requires the kinetic energy of a small tectonic event. Why anyone thought it was a brilliant idea to bake a sourdough starter from scratch while simultaneously analyzing a domestic comedy about generational trauma is a question for the gods. Yet here we are. 

### The Inheritance of Quiet Suffering

*I'm Sorry You Feel That Way* arrives with a heavy burden on its shoulders. Publishers love dropping the *Sorrow and Bliss* comparison like a golden ticket, but honestly, does every messy family drama involving sharp-tongued women now have to wear that exact same t-shirt? I am exhausted by the marketing department's obsession with pegging every new release to Meg Mason or Sally Rooney as if readers cannot comprehend a book on its own terms. Give the author some credit. Rebecca Wait is working with familiar ingredients—estranged sisters, funerals as narrative bookends, mothers who leave psychological shrapnel behind them—yet the synopsis hints at a particular brand of British mordant wit that might just save it from the standard trauma-porn pile. 

### Sibling Dynamics and Flour-Dust Dystopias

And speaking of trauma, why is every fictional set of siblings split neatly down the middle into saint and sinner archetypes? It is so tiresomely binary. Alice and Hanna, the twins, supposedly mirror their mother Celia and their aunt Katy. This cyclical doom feels terribly neat, almost too tidy for actual human dysfunction. Real families do not operate like clockwork tragedies; they are messy, repetitive, boringly cruel, and rarely care about symmetrical symmetry. Give me a sibling rivalry born out of who got the bigger bedroom or a petty disagreement over a childhood pet over these grand, predestined generational curses any day of the week. Still, the promise of a brother who is eternally disapproving offers a glimmer of hope. We all know that brother. He is currently standing by the oven judging my baking technique and sighing loudly at the mess on the floor. 

The synopsis leans heavily on denial and despair as the comic nexus of the human condition. I can respect that. If you cannot laugh at the absolute absurdity of burying relatives while harboring decades of unspoken resentment, what is the point of reading domestic fiction at all? Wait clearly understands that families curdle like unpasteurized milk left out in the August sun. Whether the prose can sustain that wryly beating heart without descending into a trite parade of mental health tropes remains to be seen. I am crossing my flour-dusted fingers. 

My teenage son just hollered from the driveway that his parallel parking practice session is scheduled for precisely two minutes ago, and my nerves are already shot.
