It's one of those nostalgic days that finds me reminiscing about forgotten summers and the eerie silence that descends over the rural landscape when the wind picks up. The kind that makes me yearn for a good scare, one that lingers long after the credits roll or the lights flicker back on.

*It Looks Like You in the Dark*, the latest addition to the Kobuk Valley Series by Mathilda Zeller—I'm reminded of the works of Carmen Maria Machado and her unsettling ability to subvert genre conventions— promises to be a propulsive Indigenous horror debut. The synopsis reads like a carefully crafted folkloric warning, as if the author is passing down a cautionary tale to those willing to listen. It's a promise of terrors both ancient and new, and it's one that I've become increasingly accustomed to expecting from the current crop of horror writers.

Tapeesa, the protagonist, lives in a remote Alaskan village, surrounded by white men and their extractive endeavors. She knows the legend of the kushtuka—a creature that assumes the form of a loved one, intent on luring its prey into the abyss. I'm taken back to the works of Thomas Pynchon and his intricate explorations of the American psyche, though Zeller's approach feels more intimate, more vulnerable.

But what's truly striking about this setup is the notion that the lines between myth and reality are blissfully blurred. Tapeesa's sister Esther goes missing, and she must delve headfirst into the dark, where her perceptions are constantly warped by the looming presence of the supernatural. It's reminiscent of the way authors like Jeff VanderMeer and Ann Quin expertly weave the rational and the irrational into an unsettling tapestry.

And yet, I find myself questioning the notion of "delve" headfirst into the darkness. Does Tapeesa, or the author for that matter, really know what they're getting themselves into? Or is this a metaphor for something more profound, a commentary on the hubris that drives us to uncover secrets better left unspoken? It's an interpretation that I'd be remiss to ignore, much like the way an archaeologist carefully excavates artifacts from the earth.

But then there's the notion that Tapeesa's "bad blood" might be more than just a metaphor. Does this imply a deeper connection to the mythological creatures that inhabit this realm? Perhaps it's a symbol of the village's troubled legacy, a reminder of the violence that lingers just beneath the surface. Zeller has a keen sense of how to wield these motifs, often blurring the lines between the sacred and the profane.

The *Kobuk River Valley*, where the white men come to dig up the rare earths, is a symbol of the destruction that awaits when we turn our backs on the land. It's a stark reminder of the fragile connection between humans and the environment. But does this make the author guilty of environmentalist sloganeering? Or is it merely a clever subtext for a tale that's ostensibly about monsters in the dark? I'd argue it's a little bit of both.

The more I ponder the premise of *It Looks Like You in the Dark*, the more I find myself convinced that Mathilda Zeller is onto something special. She's crafted a narrative that feels both personal and universally relatable, one that acknowledges the terrors we're capable of unleashing upon ourselves and the world around us. It's an unsettling message, to be sure, but also one that I believe merits our attention.

I'm left with more questions than answers, wondering if Tapeesa will ultimately find her sister or succumb to the void that's been waiting for her. But one thing's certain: I'll be keeping an ear out for any whispers from the *Kobuk Valley*, and Zeller's latest offering will undoubtedly be at the top of my priority list.
