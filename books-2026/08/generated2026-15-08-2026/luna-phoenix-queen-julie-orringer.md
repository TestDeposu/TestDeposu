**Author:** Julie Orringer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-13 APL

Another midwestern faculty lounge drama about academic infidelity and stolen manuscripts. Haven't we read this exact book thirty times already? Yet Julie Orringer is dusting off the old "husband steals his cognitively declining wife's brilliant novel" trope as if it hasn't been done to death by every MFA graduate since the Clinton administration. At least nobody is building a theoretical faster-than-light drive with impossible physics in this one, which is refreshing for once. Give me actual hard science any day over endless prose about ivory tower neuroses. 

### The Stolen Prose Industrial Complex

The synopsis lays out the whole mechanical contraption right there in the open. Dava writes a book while losing her grip to early-onset Alzheimer's. Barr reads it, seethes with typical mediocre-male academic jealousy, and publishes it under his own name. Groundbreaking. Riveting. Let me just check my watch while I sigh deeply at the predictable architecture of literary theft. Actually, wait. I take that back. The chorus of voices structure—incorporating a traumatized shelter dog named Luna alongside the human cast—might actually save this from being standard literary fiction drudgery. Or it might be entirely unhinged. Dogs shouldn't get point-of-view chapters unless they are barking at lasers or questioning the laws of thermodynamics. 

### Memory, Theft, and Other Clichés

Orringer attempts to construct a sprawling narrative spanning four decades and thousands of miles, tackling the weaponization of memory and the fragility of identity. But let us be honest about the mechanics here. I am sitting in this freezing campus basement staring at a glowing screen, just trying to hit the mandatory word count for this preview so I can stop thinking about academic plagiarism and go home. Why do we romanticize the destruction of female intellect for the sake of a man's guilt-ridden redemption arc? James makes a startling discovery after his father dies. Groundbreaking stuff. 

Expect comparisons to Ian McEwan or Donna Tartt because critics are allergic to originality. If you liked *The Goldfinch*, you will allegedly love this. But this smells like the kind of heavy-handed prose that wins prizes precisely because it lacks any joy, preferring to wallow in the quiet devastation of marital misery. 

Look, I cannot dwell on the thematic implications of stolen authorship any longer. I have an exclusive wine tasting event at the country club in an hour and I still need to find my cardigan.
