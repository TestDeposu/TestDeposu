**Author:** Kimberly Garza  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

The synopsis for *Between You a River* reads like a family tree that’s been set on fire—charred edges, smoke still curling from the pages, and the promise of something *visceral* beneath the ashes. But here’s the problem: it’s too neat. Too *saga-ready*. The 1960s Philippines, the immigrant dream, the ancestral land slipping through fingers—it’s all there, but where’s the *mess*? Where’s the moment when the reader realizes this isn’t just a love letter to diaspora, but a *wound*? Because the best family sagas—think *The House of the Spirits* or *The Namesake*—don’t just tell you the family is broken; they make you *feel* the way the cracks bleed.

And yet, the synopsis sells it like a postcard. "A lush family saga"—lush? Really? Lush implies something *grown*, something *intentional*, but what if the lushness is just the way the family’s secrets fester? What if the "push and pull of home" isn’t a theme but a *physical force*, like gravity pulling you toward something you can’t name? The synopsis hints at it—Corazon’s choices, the fire, the daughters returning—but it doesn’t *show* the weight of those choices. Is she a martyr? A coward? A woman who made the only decision she could, and now her daughters are left to clean up the mess? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just *promises*.

But let’s talk about the *real* crime here: the way diaspora narratives often get reduced to *origin stories*. Like that time I tried to explain to a room full of people why *Avatar: The Last Airbender* isn’t just about "cultural exchange" but about *erasure*—how the show’s "diversity" was just a backdrop for white saviorism, and now I’m stuck comparing *Between You a River* to it, which is unfair because this isn’t a cartoon, but still, where’s the *subversion*? Where’s the moment when the family’s history isn’t just a pretty backdrop but a *landmine*? The synopsis mentions "long-buried secrets," but secrets are only interesting if they’re *dangerous*. If they don’t cut. If they don’t make you question whether the mother was a hero or a villain.

And don’t even get me started on the way the synopsis frames the sisters’ return as some kind of *redemptive arc*. "Together for the first time in years"—oh, please. Family reunions in literature are either *miraculous* or *disastrous*. They’re not just "together." They’re *clashing*, *betraying*, *gaslighting*. The synopsis makes it sound like they’re uniting to save the land, but what if they’re uniting to *fight over it*? What if the land isn’t the issue—what if the issue is that they *never really knew each other*? That the mother’s sacrifices weren’t just about money or legacy, but about *erasing* parts of themselves to make room for their daughters? The synopsis doesn’t go there. It just *assumes* the reader will care about the land, as if that’s the only thing that matters.

But here’s the thing—I *do* care about the land. I care about the way diaspora narratives often get reduced to *property*. Like that time I tried to explain to a landlord why "cultural heritage" isn’t just a pretty story but a *legal* and *emotional* battle, and now I’m stuck thinking about how *Between You a River* could’ve been so much sharper if it didn’t just *tell* us about the land but *showed* us the way it *owns* the family. The way it *judges* them. The way it’s not just a place but a *character*—one that’s been waiting for decades to be reckoned with.

And yet, the synopsis is too clean. Too *precious*. It’s like reading a menu where every dish is described as "delicious" but you can’t tell if it’s spicy or sweet or just *expensive*. The fire that splits the family? Was it an accident? A crime? A metaphor? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just *hints*. And hints are for people who don’t want to be surprised. They’re for people who want to *feel safe*.

But the best family sagas don’t make you feel safe. They make you *uncomfortable*. They make you *angry*. They make you *question*. And right now, *Between You a River* feels like it’s trying too hard to be *accessible*. Like it’s afraid of being *too much*. But the best stories aren’t about being *understood*—they’re about being *haunted*.

And now I have to translate a particularly stubborn piece of French existential poetry because apparently, that’s what happens when you spend too long thinking about family sagas that don’t dare to be *messy*.
