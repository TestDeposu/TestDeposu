**Author:** Siân Hughes  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-10 APL

Rain keeps drumming against the windowpane with the steady, heavy persistence of an unresolved argument from ten years ago. Siân Hughes is dropping *No Such Thing as Monday* upon us soon, and the publishing houses are already whispering about redemption arcs like they can be neatly packaged and sold at duty-free shops. 

### The Architecture of Bruises

Look at the premise. A dry cleaner. An impoverished father. The classic golden child holding the hand of a monster while the scapegoat runs for the horizon. It is a heavy pitch. But wait—actually, let me walk that back entirely. The synopsis leans so hard into the working-class misery trope that it borders on a caricature of British grit. 

We have seen this exact scaffolding before. Douglas Stuart builds these monuments to suffering with terrifying precision. *Shuggie Bain* left us gasping for air because the love in the filth felt earned. Does Hughes manage that same claustrophobic magic? The synopsis gives almost nothing away about the actual mechanics of the prose, hiding behind industry buzzwords like "scrappy" and "sardonic." 

And speaking of abrasive children—why must modern media insist on making every single adolescent character talk like a disillusioned thirty-year-old philosophy major who just discovered gin? My youngest has been watching some blindingly neon cartoon where a magical dog fixes every emotional rupture in twenty-two minutes flat with a catchy jingle and an apology. It makes me want to drive my car directly into the nearest hardware store. Real life doesn't resolve in a brightly colored montage. It staggers forward in the rain, smelling faintly of old bleach and cheap cigarettes, exactly where Steffie stands over her dead father. 

### The Geography of Absence

The real engine of this novel won't be the death of the old man. No. It will be the silence he left behind. Caroline fled, and Steffie stayed. That asymmetry is the only part of the synopsis that actually hurts. We carry the weights we choose, or the weights our parents glued to our spines before we had the vocabulary to resist. 

Hughes wants us to believe in a quest for forgiveness. Yet forgiveness is a lazy literary destination. True survival is just waking up on a Tuesday and deciding not to throw the iron through the front window. If the book leans too heavily into the comedy of working-class despair, it risks turning real trauma into a quirky indie film script. 

I am going to read a battered copy of Kerouac until the sun comes up.
