**Author:** Natalie Baszile  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

As an ardent fan of contemporary drama, I've been anxiously waiting for *Friends and Family*, the latest offering from *Queen Sugar* author Natalie Baszile. But what I found in the official synopsis is more a tease than a tantalizing prospect, rather like a half-baked cake that refuses to fulfill its promise. I'm not buying it, folks. 

Torn between respecting Nora's boundaries and embodying the values so essential to Babs's family, we're told, Babs must make a choice that will have far-reaching consequences for these two friends and their children. Yet, I still have no idea what that choice is or why it's supposed to change the course of their relationships. It's a bit like a recipe book without actual recipes, full of tantalizing flavor descriptions but devoid of substance. What kind of narrative do we get to inhabit, where friends, privilege, and identity intersect, but none of these terms is interrogated critically?

In this tight-knit enclave in the Bay Area, the very real problem of racial tensions and systemic injustices is reduced to a series of vague pronouncements about 'allyship'—an ill-defined concept that only serves to obfuscate deeper structural issues. It's a bit like saying that a novel is about character development without delving into the complexities of motivation, backstory, or psychological nuance. 

The plot's vagueness is disconcerting, to say the least. It feels like a set of disconnected plot threads—police encounters, traumatic events, the fraught dynamics between adoptive mothers and their children—lacking any clear narrative thread to tie them all together. One would expect a more incisive, thoughtful portrayal of this precarious world, particularly from someone as well-regarded as Baszile. It feels like an opportunity squandered, and I'm starting to worry about the trajectory of this series.

Yet, I still remain optimistic, though my enthusiasm is tempered by the lukewarm prospect of *Friends and Family*. It's a bit like being promised a delicious meal at a Michelin-starred restaurant only to receive a mediocre, hastily thrown-together burger instead. Perhaps Baszile has some trick up her sleeve, but based on this synopsis, I'm just not convinced.

The themes of privilege, community, and identity are timely and crucial, and I applaud the author for tackling these issues, even if they are handled in a somewhat clumsy manner. Baszile's work is not without its parallels to the likes of *The Mothers* by Brit Bennett, a book with which she shares some stylistic and thematic affinities.

But in the end, the suspense is killing me, because I just want to get on with it. I'm tired of half-promises and half-measured portrayals of what it means to truly be an ally, a friend, and a parent in this divided world.

Literally.

Hard pass on this one, folks.
