Somebody just spilled lukewarm cider on my boots while the bass player screams lyrics that sound like garbage disposal blades hitting silverware. Perfect. I am trying to parse Anna Rasche’s upcoming *The Cursed Diamond* against the deafening roar of thirty thousand screaming anarchists and a guy with a mohawk who keeps elbowing my ribs. Give me a break. Or better yet, give me a book that doesn't rely on the exact same cursed-object trope we have been dragging through the mud since Wilkie Collins crawled out of his grave. 

### The Century-Spanning Ghost Train

Dual timelines. Oh, joy. Because what modern thrillers really need is a rhythmic bounce between a terrified runner in the 2010 Diamond District and a disgraced heiress on a 1910 ocean liner. And look, I usually love a good period piece wrapped in modern paranoia—give me Ruth Ware or a gritty dens-of-iniquity vibe any day of the week—but this synopsis is screaming clichés louder than the amps behind my head. A cryptic note reading "Not Safe"? Groundbreaking. Truly, literary history trembles. 

Wait. Let me back up for a second. Maybe the dual-timeline gimmick actually works here because the physical object—this blue rock of doom—acts as the anchor. Or maybe it's just an excuse to pad the word count until November 2026 without giving away whether there's an actual supernatural element or just a bunch of greedy people killing each other over carbon. Actually, scratch that. Supernatural curses are lazy writing. Give me human greed or give me nothing. 

### Diamonds Are a Detective's Worst Friend

Look at the psychological trap Rasche is setting. Ambition as a vector for ruin. Ground covered by everyone from *The Great Gatsby* to every dusty noir paperback rotting in a subway station trash can. Marcella is smuggling the gem into New York after a London scandal—classic—while Jennie stumbles over a dead friend's final breadcrumb. And yes, the blue diamond trope is as old as the Hope Diamond itself, which means the author is fighting an uphill battle against historical predictability. But if the pacing moves fast enough to outrun the sheer weight of its own derivative setup, I might actually forgive the recycled plot beats. 

The bass just rattled my teeth right out of my skull. And honestly? The book's premise is aging worse than milk left out in this godforsaken park. No wait—that's unfair. A tight, claustrophobic chase through modern Manhattan paired with Gilded Age maritime scandal could actually snap like a whip if the prose has enough teeth. Or it will sink faster than the Titanic. We will find out in November. 

Right now I need to go home and patch a hole in my favorite, impossibly worn tweed jacket before the mosh pit completely rips the left sleeve off.
