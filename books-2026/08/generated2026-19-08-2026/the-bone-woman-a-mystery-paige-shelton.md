**Page Count:** Unknown

Something about the Arizona desert always smells like sun-baked copper and rot. Paige Shelton knows this, presumably—or at least she’s banking on the visceral, skeletal aesthetics of *The Bone Woman* to carry the weight of a premise that feels like it fell out of a dusty trunk in the back of a second-hand bookstore. We have a protagonist named Hazel, because of course we do, and the tired trope of the forensic expert with a Daddy Issue that could swallow a small planet. 

### Osteological Trauma and Other Banal Terrors

The conceit—using bones as a structural medium for taxidermy-like sculptures—is undeniably grotesque, yet I find myself rolling my eyes at the surgical precision mentioned in the blurb. It reeks of that tedious, pseudo-intellectual slasher aesthetic that tries way too hard to be Thomas Harris without the actual literary merit. Is it meant to be profound? Or just shock value for the Sunday morning brunch crowd? Hazel is the quintessential damaged professional—a woman who talks to skeletons because people are too messy, I assume. It’s a total mood, honestly, but it’s been done to death by every crime novelist who watched too many episodes of *Bones* in the mid-aughts. Yet, there is a certain bleak potential here. If Shelton leans into the desiccated, sun-bleached nihilism of the desert rather than settling for a standard procedural, this could actually stop being basic and start being something genuinely disturbing.

### The Problem with Disappearing Patriarchs

Let’s be real. The missing father figure is the lowest hanging fruit in the thriller garden. It’s a narrative crutch for characters who lack a personality outside of their trauma. Does every female investigator need to solve a crime to heal her own internal void? Apparently. If I have to read one more scene where a protagonist holds a trinket—a finger bone, in this case—and experiences a spiritual epiphany, I’m going to lose my mind. It’s melodramatic trash, but the kind of trash that keeps me awake at 3 AM wondering if I’m just as broken as the archetypes I critique. The tension between the professional obsession with death and the personal intimacy of family legacy is a massive pendulum. If she swings it too far toward sentimental closure, the book is dead on arrival. It needs to stay in the dirt. It needs to remain uncomfortable. 

I’m betting this ends with a predictable reveal that the father isn't a victim but the architect of the chaos. It’s so cliché it might just be brilliant in its sheer audacity. It reminds me of the vibe in *The Lost Village* by Camilla Sten, only with less hiking gear and more existential dread. I’m giving it a fifty-fifty chance of being a cult classic or a bargain bin inhabitant by November. 

The machine is hissing and sputtering again, which is probably a metaphor for my current state of mind. I’m going to pull a shot so acidic it’ll strip the enamel off my teeth and stare at a blank screen until the sun decides to quit.
