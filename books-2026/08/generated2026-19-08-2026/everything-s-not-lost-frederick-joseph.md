**Author:** Frederick Joseph  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-20 APL

Hold onto the overhead strap. Someone just elbowed my ribs because they missed their stop at 34th Street. 

Frederick Joseph is dropping *Everything's Not Lost* in late 2026, and I am already squinting at my screen, trying to read a publishing blurb over the shoulder of the person squished next to me. Let us be entirely real for a second. I agreed to type out this anticipation piece because my coffee was lukewarm and I desperately needed to distract myself from the guy playing TikTok videos without headphones three inches from my face. 

Wait. Let me back up. I just said I was excited for this release, but honestly? YA grief lit is a minefield. Half the time it reads like a pamphlet handed out by an anxious guidance counselor who has never actually met a teenager. 

Yet. Joseph usually avoids that sanitized trap. 

### The Art of the Crush and the Collapse

The synopsis drops us straight into the skull of a sixteen-year-old protagonist named Ella. Bipolar disorder. A deceased sister. A schoolyard bully gunning for her mental stability. It is heavy. It is loud. It hits with the subtlety of a freight train barreling down the express track. 

And I hate it. Or maybe I love it. Make up your mind, brain. 

We are looking at a narrative framework that relies heavily on internal friction. You have the external antagonist—some anonymous tormentor at school—acting as a catalyst for the internal war. Mental illness meets adolescent cruelty. It is a classic literary pressure cooker. Think Angie Thomas meets John Green, but cranked up several notches into the psychological thriller adjacent zone. Except this isn't a thriller. The blurb promises a "vividly hopeful" trajectory. 

Hope. Right. Because teenagers navigating clinical diagnoses and sudden familial loss always find their answers neatly wrapped in artistic expression and new friendships right before the final bell rings. 

### Punching the Clock

Why am I even writing this? Honestly, I am just padding the word count because my editor demanded a deep dive into themes I can only guess at from four paragraphs of publisher-approved marketing copy. We are flying blind here. The synopsis gives us art, grief, memory, and a vague threat. It is structural scaffolding pretending to be a plot. 

And yet. The endorsement from Jacqueline Woodson carries weight. Real weight. Not just blurb-quote fluff designed to push units on pre-order. 

Ella’s relationship with her own mind is the actual antagonist here. The school bully is just noise. The real dread comes from the fog. If Joseph can capture the erratic, jagged cadence of a manic episode colliding with profound mourning without turning it into an after-school special, this book might actually survive the cynical gauntlet of modern young adult fiction. 

Hold on. The train is lurching. Someone dropped a briefcase on my sneaker. 

If *Everything's Not Lost* leans into the jagged edges instead of smoothing them over with tidy cinematic resolutions, it could work. If it relies on cheap emotional manipulation? Well. I will be throwing it directly against the subway car door. 

My stop is in three minutes. I am going to collapse into my sagging recliner the second I unlock my front door and aggressively ignore every single chore waiting for me in the kitchen.
