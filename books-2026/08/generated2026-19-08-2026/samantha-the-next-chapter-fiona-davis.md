**Author:** Fiona Davis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-13 APL

The radiator down the hall has been clanking out a rhythmic, mocking metal song for three hours, and my tea went cold ages ago. I stare at the water stains mapping imaginary continents across the ceiling while contemplating Fiona Davis stepping into the sacred archives of childhood nostalgia. *Samantha: The Next Chapter* arrives later this year, dragging a beloved doll-turned-literary-pioneer kicking and screaming into the brutal reality of 1920. Or does it? The synopsis leans heavily on the kind of historical fiction tropes that keep mass-market paperbacks spinning off airport kiosk racks. Yet, there is a distinct, sharp edge to the premise of a Gilded Age orphan losing her entire fortune overnight and being forced to punch a clock. 

### From Silk to Cheap Soap

The class migration trope is well-worn, almost threadbare by now. Give me Edith Wharton over a sanitized historical costume drama any day. Wait, scratch that—whining about Wharton is pretentious when we are talking about American Girl fan service disguised as adult historical drama. Actually, Davis usually avoids the lowest common denominator, treating her architectural backdrops with genuine reverence. Mount Bedford stripped away? A boarding house bedroom barely larger than a twin mattress? I know that claustrophobia intimately. The plaster crumbles here just like it surely did in the Lower East Side tenements. 

### Suffrage and Stolen Bonds

The intersection of the 19th Amendment and sudden destitution creates a fascinating thematic friction. Samantha is fighting for a ballot while losing her literal bed. But let us be entirely honest with ourselves for a breathless second. The synopsis plays out like a mad-lib designed for historical fiction addicts who consume books like potato chips. Infiltrating a criminal underworld? Please. Next thing we know she will be solving murders with a snappy newsboy and drinking bootleg gin at the Plaza. It feels a bit derivative of Kate Quinn or Paula McLain, chasing that elusive dual-timeline market magic even when sticking to a single, rigid year. 

The psychological descent from privileged socialite to struggling shop girl demands grit, not gloss. If Davis trades genuine grit for romanticized grit—the kind where everyone still has immaculate hair despite the soot—the book will crumble under its own weight. I desperately want Samantha to fail at undercover sleuthing. Let her make terrible financial choices out of sheer desperation. Let the ink on those suffrage pamphlets smudge with grease from her new, low-wage job. 

My tarot reading this morning involved the Three of Swords followed swiftly by the Five of Pentacles, which basically guaranteed an evening of profound annoyance.
