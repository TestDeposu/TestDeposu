**Author:** Katie Chandler  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-08 APL

This steamy sports romance, *Triple Threat*, seems to be a thrilling tale of love, tennis, and self-discovery. The story revolves around Octavia Ricci, a disgraced tennis star, her new coach Ariel de Cordova, and her soulmate Leo Mantovani, who, in a twist, is also Ariel's soulmate. As they navigate the complexities of their relationships, the tennis world watches their every move, and they must decide if love is worth risking their careers, reputations, and hearts.

—And I'm already sensing the tension. The author, Katie Chandler, is known for her exquisite storytelling in *Backhanded Compliments*, and I'm excited to see how she weaves this complex web of relationships. But I have to admit, the synopsis feels a bit...vague. I mean, what exactly is the nature of their relationships? How does Leo's soulmate status with both Octavia and Ariel affect their dynamics?

One thing that stands out is the exploration of the tennis world and its cutthroat nature. The pressure to perform, the constant scrutiny, and the toll it takes on the players' mental health are all themes that I'm eager to see explored. And with the added complexity of a polyamorous relationship, I'm curious to see how Chandler navigates these sensitive topics.

But what really caught my attention was the mention of Octavia's controlling father and her public meltdown at the Australian Open. This sounds like a classic case of a high-pressure environment taking a toll on a young athlete's mental health. I'm reminded of *Challengers* and *Carrie Soto Is Back*, both of which explored similar themes of tennis, fame, and the psychological effects of competition.

A bold claim for a Tuesday, but I think Chandler might be onto something here. The combination of sports romance, polyamory, and mental health themes has the potential to create a compelling narrative that explores the complexities of human relationships and the pressures of the sports world.

Yet, I'm also worried that the book might fall into the trap of relying too heavily on romance tropes. The "secret relationship" plot device, while intriguing, feels a bit overused. And what about the power dynamics between Octavia, Ariel, and Leo? How will Chandler navigate the complexities of their relationships without resorting to clichés?

As I continue to ponder these questions, I'm reminded that I still have a lot of work to do. And, honestly, I'd rather be repairing my broken French press than writing this review. Ugh, the things I do for a good story.
