The descendants of people who actually owned things are currently forced to watch the hoi polloi fumble with glowing rectangles like chimpanzees discovering fire for the first time. August Lamm has penned *You Don't Need a Smartphone*, an upcoming manifesto set for publication in October 2026, which promises to salvage what remains of human cognitive capacity from the jaws of late-stage dopamine harvesting. Or perhaps it is merely another marketable lifestyle accessory for burnt-out influencers. One grows terribly fatigued keeping track of the bourgeoisie rediscovering basic analog autonomy and attempting to monetize it as a radical spiritual awakening. 

Lamm spent her formative years glued to a glowing pane of glass—a thoroughly pedestrian affliction—before abruptly deciding she preferred the quiet dignity of a "dumb phone." Yet, calling the abolition of digital leashes a revolutionary geopolitical stance feels remarkably dramatic. But who am I to scoff at the profound realization that checking dual-factor authentication without a pocket supercomputer requires minor logistical inconvenience? 

### The Aristocracy of Boredom

The central motif here relies on the ancient, forgotten art of looking out a train window rather than staring blankly at algorithmic sludge. Lamm suggests that parting ways with one's digital appendage restores curiosity and attention spans. Naturally, the modern literati will clutch their pearls at the sheer Luddite audacity of navigating a city without real-time satellite mapping. Let them weep. True lineage demands an internal compass, not an orbiting constellation of government hardware telling you where to purchase overpriced artisanal coffee. 

Wait. Dismissing the logistical hurdles of modern bureaucracy as mere inconveniences is terribly myopic of me. Try paying property taxes or boarding a transatlantic flight without a QR code and watch how quickly romantic analog theories shatter against the cold, unyielding wall of administrative digitization. Lamm apparently tackles these exact modern bottlenecks, though one suspects her practical suggestions amount to little more than telling the peasantry to simply touch grass and stop being parasocially obsessed with TikTok.

### The Grift of Going Offline

Catherine Price blurbed the volume, drawing an immediate parallel to *How to Break Up With Your Phone*. We are drowning in a veritable sea of self-help screeds telling adults how to put down their toys like misbehaving toddlers. Yet, Lamm’s approach leans heavily into biographical redemption. She wasted her youth on the internet, which is the modern equivalent of squandering a pristine country estate at the baccarat table, and now she wants to charge us for the architectural blueprint of her escape. 

The stylistic architecture of the book remains entirely opaque from the marketing copy, relying entirely on emotional resonance rather than structural heft. Is it a rigorous sociological critique of surveillance capitalism, or just a glorified diary entry written by someone who couldn't handle the algorithm? Both, most likely. It gives the readers maximum vibey satisfaction without demanding any actual intellectual heavy lifting.
