Sasha Sinclair carries the crushing weight of ancestral ambition on her shoulders, which is a tired enough trope to make me sigh into my cold espresso. Yet, Ashley Herring Blake wants us to believe that losing documentary-filmmaking parents three years ago translates directly into a life of casual hookups and geographic drift. Because apparently, women in contemporary romance cannot simply exist without trauma dictating their odometer. *Take a Chance, Sasha Sinclair* drops us squarely into Clover Lake territory, betting heavily on the tired dynamic of the brooding drifter colliding with the grounded local protector. 

Let us talk about Margot Bishop. She is not a witch—except the marketing apparatus of her family’s struggling amusement park, Ghostlight, relies entirely on the century-old myth of the Rose Witch to keep the lights on. Capitalism demands that women weaponize their own folklore, and Margot is the designated point person tasked with babysitting an enthusiastic interloper. I am already exhausted by the mandatory friction. 

### The Commodification of Eccentric Ancestresses

Blake is trading in the exact same cozy, hyper-romanticized quirkiness that populated *Delilah Green Doesn't Care*. But wait—did I just praise the author's previous work? Let me correct that immediately. I actually found those forced proximity dynamics aggressively trite, so expecting structural subversion here is likely a fool's errand. We are promised an ice-melting romance built on historical digging into Alice Bishop's 1901 arrival at Thornrose Apothecary. 

Why must cinematic storytelling always be the catalyst for emotional awakening? Must creative passion be a proxy for sexual availability? ### The Mechanics of Manufactured Friction

The synopsis leans hard on the presumed antagonism between familial duty and outsider intrusion. Margot wants to protect her family's legacy. Sasha wants to validate her existence through her deceased parents' unfinished dream project. Groundbreaking psychology. Groundbreaking conflict. 

And yet, romance audiences eat this up. They crave the inevitable slow-burn pivot where professional annoyance dissolves into heart-fluttering breathless yearning inside a haunted wood. Will the prose elevate the cliché? Blake usually delivers snappy banter, though I am profoundly tired of heroines whose primary personality traits involve being peppy counterpoints to surly locals. Give me a protagonist who actively chooses destruction over charm. Give me a narrative that lets women be difficult without a dead-parent excuse attached to their resumes. 

If this town truly suspected a woman of witchcraft back in 1901, Alice Bishop probably just wanted everyone to leave her alone in the apothecary. A mood I deeply respect. Instead, modern descendants turn her sanctuary into a theme park snack bar while contemporary love interests interrogate the archives just to catch feelings. 

I have to go untangle the dog's leash from the dining table.
