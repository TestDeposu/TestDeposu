**Author:** Heather Fawcett  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Emily Wilde’s History of Dark Faerie* reads like a scholar’s footnote left in the margins of a book that’s already been torn out of its binding—just enough context to make you want to burn the library. The Black Forest, of course, is never just a forest in these kinds of stories. It’s a character in its own right, a place where the trees whisper in languages that defy translation, where the air hums with the residual static of something ancient and hungry. And yet, the synopsis doesn’t tell us what the Folk are *actually* afraid of—only that they are. That’s the kind of vague menace that makes you suspect the book will lean into the kind of gothic dread that’s been overused since *The Witch* (2015) proved that slow-burn dread sells better than CGI demons. But then again, Heather Fawcett has a knack for balancing academic rigor with something that feels like it was plucked straight from a Victorian parlor game of "What If the Faeries Were Just a Little Too Real?"

The real intrigue here is the shift in Emily Wilde’s dynamic with Wendell Bambleby. She’s not just a scholar anymore—she’s a woman who’s been married for two years, which means she’s either grown into her role or is now the kind of person who forgets to take out the trash while chasing eldritch mysteries. The synopsis hints at a tension that isn’t just about the portal or the missing friend, but about the cost of her obsession. And Ariadne? Oh, she’s the wild card. A niece who wants to follow in her aunt’s footsteps—because nothing says "family legacy" like being groomed to become the next Emily Wilde, complete with all the emotional baggage that comes with it. It’s the kind of setup that makes you wonder if Fawcett is channeling *The Dark Tower*’s Roland Deschain, where every generation thinks they’re the one to finally solve the mystery, only to realize they’re just another cog in the machine.

But here’s where the synopsis fails—it doesn’t tell us what Dark Faerie *is*. Is it a place? A concept? A metaphor for something Emily Wilde has been avoiding? The way the Folk fear it suggests it’s not just another faerie kingdom, but something *wrong*, something that exists outside the usual rules of magic and folklore. And yet, the synopsis treats it like a puzzle box, promising answers without ever revealing the mechanism. It’s the same problem with *The Name of the Wind*—you’re left wanting more, but not because the story is compelling, because the marketing is. This is why we can’t have nice things.

Speaking of nice things, I can’t help but think about how *Avatar: The Last Airbender* handled its own "dark faerie" equivalent—Zuko’s redemption arc, which was built on the idea that even the most broken people can find light. But *Emily Wilde’s History of Dark Faerie* isn’t about redemption. It’s about the cost of knowledge, the way curiosity can eat you alive, and the fact that some doors should never be opened. The synopsis doesn’t even hint at whether Emily Wilde will become the villain of her own story, but I have a feeling she will. She’s already shown she’s capable of ruthlessness—remember when she helped Wendell reclaim his throne? That wasn’t just diplomacy; that was a calculated move, and Emily Wilde doesn’t do calculated moves out of the kindness of her heart.

And then there’s the Black Forest itself, which is treated like a character in a horror movie, but without the payoff. It’s the kind of setting that should be dripping with atmosphere, with the kind of dread that makes you want to turn off the lights and check under your bed. But the synopsis doesn’t give us any of that. It’s just a place where a friend goes missing, which is the same thing that happens in every YA thriller since *Goosebumps*. The real question is whether Fawcett can make the Forest feel like more than just a backdrop. Can she make it feel like a living, breathing entity that’s been waiting for someone to disturb it? Or is this just another book where the setting is a gimmick, and the real action happens in the portal?

I don’t know. But I do know that the synopsis is doing a disservice to the book by not giving us more. It’s like reading a menu that only lists the appetizers—you know there’s a main course, but you have no idea what it’ll be. And that’s frustrating, because *Emily Wilde’s History of Dark Faerie* has the potential to be something special. It’s got the academic rigor of *The Once and Future Witches*, the gothic dread of *The Night Circus*, and the family drama of *Good Omens*—but without the charm. And that’s a problem, because charm is what makes these kinds of stories stick.

But let’s be honest—this is the kind of book that’s going to get lost in the shuffle. It’s not *The Locked Tomb*, it’s not *The House in the Cerulean Sea*, it’s just another entry in a series that’s already been outpaced by its own success. And that’s a shame, because Fawcett has the chops to do something truly original. She just needs to stop treating her readers like they’re children who need to be hand-held through every plot twist.
