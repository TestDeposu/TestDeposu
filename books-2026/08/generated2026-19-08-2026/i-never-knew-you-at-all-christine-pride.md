**Author:** Christine Pride  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-13 APL

The complexities of marriage, the weight of family secrets, and the blurred lines between loyalty and truth. *I Never Knew You at All*, the latest novel from Christine Pride, promises to deliver a gripping narrative that challenges the very foundations of relationships. Or so it seems.

As I pour over the synopsis, I find myself drawn to the character of Simone Hartwell – a Black woman who has made the difficult choice of giving up her promising career as a journalist to support her husband, Alex, and their son, Langston. The echoes of compromise and sacrifice resonate deeply, and I am reminded of the works of authors like Tayari Jones and Celeste Ng, who have so masterfully explored the intricacies of marriage and identity.

But as I delve deeper into the synopsis, I begin to feel a sense of unease. The plot seems to rely heavily on convenient coincidences – Kwame, Simone's college ex and first love, resurfacing in her life, investigating a decades-old murder tied to Alex's family. It's a contrived setup, one that feels like a lazy attempt to inject drama into the narrative. I'm literally begging for a new plot.

And yet, despite my reservations, I am drawn to the themes that Pride seems to be exploring. The tension between Simone's desire for a comfortable life and her lingering passion for journalism is a compelling one, and I am reminded of the works of authors like Jennifer Weiner and Emily Giffin, who have so skillfully explored the complexities of women's lives.

But what truly sets my teeth on edge is the way the synopsis glosses over the issues of racism and privilege that are so deeply ingrained in the narrative. The fact that Alex's family has attended the elite Tate School for generations, while Kwame is investigating a murder tied to that very same institution, raises so many questions about power and privilege. And yet, the synopsis barely scratches the surface of these issues.

As I continue to read, I find myself growing increasingly frustrated with the lack of depth and nuance in the synopsis. It's a surface-level exploration of complex themes, one that fails to truly engage with the issues at hand. I am reminded of the works of authors like Jesmyn Ward and Angela Flournoy, who have so masterfully explored the complexities of racism and identity in their writing.

And so, I am left with a sense of disappointment and frustration. *I Never Knew You at All* promises to deliver a gripping narrative, but the synopsis falls flat. It's a shallow exploration of complex themes, one that fails to truly engage with the issues at hand.

But I suppose that's what makes me so intrigued. Despite my reservations, I am drawn to the possibilities that this novel presents. Will Pride be able to deliver a nuanced and thoughtful exploration of the themes she has set out to tackle? Or will the narrative fall flat, relying on convenient coincidences and shallow character development?
