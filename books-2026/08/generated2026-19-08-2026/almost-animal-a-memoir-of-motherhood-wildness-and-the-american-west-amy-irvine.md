Stubbornness is a dying art. Everyone these days wants to fold the second the wind changes direction, adjusting their sails to chase whatever cultural consensus is currently drifting across the horizon like industrial smog. I refuse. Look at the early notices for *Almost Animal*. Amy Irvine is heading straight back into the arid, unforgiving theater of the American West, dragging a mirror behind her. Good. We need fewer polite memoirs about finding oneself over a matcha latte in Brooklyn, and far more harrowing accounts of women wrestling with the ghosts of manifest destiny while isolated on a remote Colorado mesa. 

My grandfather used to tell me about the old gods—the ones who didn't care about your comfort, the ones who demanded you look them dead in the eye when the snow started piling three feet high against the cabin door. People forget that nature is not a wellness retreat. It is an indifferent machine. When Irvine compares her postpartum descent to zoochosis—the literal madness of a caged beast—she is bypassing the sanitized language of modern psychological hand-wringing. She is describing a primal cage. 

And speaking of cages, why is every television show made for children nowadays just flashing neon colors and screaming at my grandkids for twenty-two unbroken minutes? There is zero silence. Zero quiet dread. You hand a kid a tablet and suddenly they are lobotomized consumers of hyperactive nonsense, completely divorced from the dirt beneath their fingernails or the terrifying vastness of a empty landscape. We are raising a generation of indoor houseplants who would literally perish if they had to face a mountain lion or stare down a black widow spider with anything other than a plastic flyswatter. 

Bring on the wildness. 

### Blood, Bone, and Bitter Sage

The synopsis leans heavily on the mythology of pioneer forebears, and I expect nothing less than structural arson from these pages. Irvine knows the blueprint of patriarchy because she spent her youth trying to tear it down with red-rock climbing hooks and wildfire axes. Yet, motherhood has a way of short-circuiting rebellion. It traps you in the exact loop you swore you escaped. 

Expect comparisons to *The Empathy Exams* or Cheryl Strayed, but Irvine possesses a sharper, more feral edge. She isn't looking for redemption in a gift shop at the end of a long trail. She is looking at the sheer, unadulterated loneliness of the frontier, asking why we keep romanticizing the very landscapes that nearly swallow us whole. 

The prose will likely burn. It better. 

I cannot sit here all afternoon waxing poetic about the psychological weight of isolation when the damn maple tree out back has dropped another ten pounds of wet debris directly into the drainage pipes.
