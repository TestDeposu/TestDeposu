**Author:** Nicolina Torres  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

The neon glow of a mall’s parking lot—empty now, but once humming with the kind of youthful chaos that only the ‘90s could manufacture—lingers in the synopsis like a half-buried corpse. *Night of the Mallrats* isn’t just a time-travel thriller; it’s a *Jaws* for nostalgia addicts, where the real horror isn’t the monster but the realization that the past was never as shiny as you remembered. Nicolina Torres seems to have swallowed a whole *True Detective* season and a *Stranger Things* binge in one gulp, then spat out something slick and unhinged.

The premise is *so* 2024—five friends, a condemned mall, a missing girl from their past—yet it’s also *so* 1994, because the synopsis drips with the kind of deliberate anachronistic charm that only a writer who’s never actually been to a mall in the ‘90s could pull off. The Beechnut Mall isn’t just a setting; it’s a character, a *Gothic* villain in its own right, its abandoned corridors whispering secrets like a *Rebecca* protagonist who’s been dead for thirty years. But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t *show* us the mall. It *tells* us it’s haunted, which is the literary equivalent of handing someone a Ouija board and saying, “Go on, ask it about the murder.” Where’s the *sensory* dread? Where’s the *physical* weight of the fluorescent lights buzzing like a swarm of angry bees? The description of the mall’s decay is more *atmospheric* than *visceral*—like reading a *Twilight* fanfiction where the vampires are just *really* moody.

And then there’s the time-travel bit, which is either a gimmick or a missed opportunity. The synopsis frames it as a *lost souls* scenario—these friends are dragged back to the night Beth Packard vanished, forced to confront their own complicity in her disappearance. But where’s the *mechanism*? Is this a *Groundhog Day* loop? A *Dark* universe where time is a prison? Or is it just a lazy way to say, “Oh, and also, they’re stuck in the past”? The synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at the rules of the time jump, which is *sloppy*—like serving a five-course meal and forgetting to include the main dish. Compare this to *The Time Traveler’s Wife*, where the mechanics are *obsessive*, or *Annihilation*, where the rules are *poetic* and *terrifying*. Here, it feels like Torres is too busy *waxing nostalgic* about Beanie Babies to bother with the logistics of time itself.

The real gold in this synopsis is the *psychological* angle—the idea that the friends are drawn back not just to solve a mystery, but to *unravel each other*. That’s *promising*, if vague. It’s the kind of setup that could either be *brilliant* (think *Gone Girl* meets *The Stepford Wives*) or *painfully* predictable (think *The Breakfast Club* but with more stabbing). The synopsis doesn’t give us enough to judge, but it *does* tease the kind of *betrayal* that could make this book *unputdownable*—if Torres has the guts to follow through. Right now, it reads like a *Halloween* movie script that’s been *softened* for a YA audience: the stakes are high, but the execution feels *safe*.

And yet—there’s something *deliciously* campy about the whole thing. The title alone is a *delightful* bit of self-aware schlock, like if *Scream* and *Clueless* had a love child. The synopsis leans into the *irony* of the ‘90s nostalgia—Glamour Shots, Lisa Frank, the *hollow* promise of mall culture—while also promising something *darker*, something *vengeful*. It’s the kind of book that could either be a *masterclass* in *nostalgic horror* (like *The Last Time I Lied* meets *Ready Player One*) or a *disappointing* mess of *unearned* dread. The synopsis doesn’t *convince* me one way or the other, which is *frustrating*—but then again, that’s the point of a *good* synopsis, isn’t it? To leave you *itching* for more.
