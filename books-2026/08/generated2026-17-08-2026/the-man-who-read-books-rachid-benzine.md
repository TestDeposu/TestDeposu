**Author:** Rachid Benzine  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-10 APL

The Man Who Read Books – a title that already feels like a quiet defiance in the face of chaos – promises to be a poignant exploration of the human condition, set against the backdrop of a war-torn Palestine. Rachid Benzine's latest offering is a tale of an elderly bookseller, a man who finds solace in the written word, and shares his story with a young French photographer.

But what does it mean, really, to find refuge in literature? Is it a form of escapism, a way to temporarily forget the harsh realities of one's surroundings? Or is it something more profound, a way to connect with the world beyond the confines of one's own experience? The bookseller's story, we're told, is one that encompasses exile, imprisonment, activism, and love – all tied to the books that helped him make sense of it all. Milan Kundera, Frantz Fanon, Umberto Eco – the names are dropped like breadcrumbs, leading us deeper into the heart of the narrative.

And yet, despite the promise of this setup, the synopsis feels curiously vague. We're given glimpses of the bookseller's life, but the specifics are sketchy at best. What does his story really entail? How do the books he reads shape his understanding of the world? The answers, it seems, lie within the pages of the book itself.

But I'm willing to take a chance on Benzine's vision. There's something about this setup that feels reminiscent of Azouz Begag's Shantytown Kid, or even the works of Assia Djebar – a sense of resilience in the face of adversity, a determination to hold on to hope, no matter what. And the writing, judging from the synopsis, seems to be infused with a sense of lyricism, a poetic sensibility that's hard to ignore.

Still, I have my doubts. Will the narrative feel overly sentimental, relying too heavily on the power of literature to transform lives? Or will it be something more nuanced, a exploration of the complexities of human experience? The truth is, I don't know – and that's what makes me want to read this book.

The Man Who Read Books feels like a love letter to literature, a testament to the power of words to sustain us in the darkest of times. It's a theme that's been explored before, of course – but there's something about Benzine's approach that feels fresh, innovative.

And so, I'll wait for the book to be released, my curiosity piqued. I'll read it, and I'll see if it lives up to the promise of its synopsis. Until then, I'll just have to stare at this blank canvas, waiting for inspiration to strike.
