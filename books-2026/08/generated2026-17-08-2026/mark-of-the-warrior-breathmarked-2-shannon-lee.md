I'll get straight to the point on *Mark of the Warrior* by Shannon Lee – the second installment in her ostensibly epic fantasy series, *Breathmarked*. From the get-go, I'm already getting whiplash from the breathless marketing blurb: "The explosive finale to the instant New York Times bestseller *Breath of the Dragon*..."

And what a finale it promises to be, with Li Jun, our brooding young warrior, tasked with uniting the realm – because, of course, it's not just about him, but also his family and... well, his family. Again. It's a tired trope – the Chosen One, born from bloodlines, destined for greatness – and I'm not buying into the whole 'fate' business.

But let's not get ahead of ourselves. We have two breathmarked women – those mystifying harbingers of doom — answering to the all-powerful Council of Virtue, an entity so opaque it could easily be a plot device. And then there's the enigmatic Yin Yue, Jun's longtime rival, joining forces with the aforementioned 'breathmarked' duo (because, naturally, it's a dynamic duo), to uncover the secrets hidden within the Scrolls of Heaven and Earth. Sounds straightforward enough, yet the Council's motivations are as murky as a swamp.

And did I mention the Sun Pagoda? No? Oh, that's right; we have to deal with a twin brother as High Keeper – the High Keeper! Protecting the Scroll of Earth from, among others, his very own sibling. Talk about an existential conundrum. Can we say 'brother complex'? Yet, according to the synopsis, this internal turmoil will 'test their skills, their loyalties, and everything they thought they knew about themselves and the nature of destiny'. 

I'm reminded of the works of Patrick Rothfuss, where character development takes precedence over plot machinations. I'm also thinking of *The Fifth Season* by N.K. Jemisin, which masterfully juggles complex themes and moral ambiguity without sacrificing character agency. But, let's face it, Lee's series still relies on familiar fantasy tropes, with its predictable plot beats and melodramatic family struggles.

I'm genuinely curious to see how Lee will subvert or, at the very least, challenge these expectations in *Mark of the Warrior*, considering her penchant for atmospheric world-building and morally dubious characters.
