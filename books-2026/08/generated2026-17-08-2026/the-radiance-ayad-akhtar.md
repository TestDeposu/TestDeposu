Sunlight is pooling across the eighteenth-century parquet in a manner that demands an immediate glass of Sancerre, provided the staff remembers where the good vintage is kept. Meanwhile, the literary world is hyperventilating over the forthcoming arrival of *The Radiance*. Ayad Akhtar has decided to grace us plebeians with what the blurbs promise is a masterpiece. God save us all from Pulitzer winners who discover God on rural asphalt. 

### The Geometry of a Crack-Up

Consider the premise. A routine hit-and-run precipitates an existential awakening. How dreadfully suburban. Yet, if anyone can elevate mid-life metaphysical whiplash into high art, it is Akhtar. We are promised an intoxicating descent—or perhaps a profoundly pretentious psychological unravelling—where madness and divine revelation blur into the exact same shade of grey. And quite frankly, I am entirely here for the ensuing dumpster fire of academic scandal. 

The synopsis breathlessly outlines an illicit liaison with a campus colleague, because nothing accelerates spiritual transcendence quite like tenure-track infidelity. We have seen this exact flavor of intellectual self-flagellation before. It leans heavily into the Don DeLillo or Philip Roth playbook of aging men grappling with their own irrelevance while the cultural architecture crumbles around them. But let us be entirely real for a second: the man is cooking with gas, and the prose is bound to slap harder than a disgruntled curator at an exclusive biennale. 

### Geographies of the Ineffable

The canvas here stretches across rural America and Europe, dragging Islam and Christianity into the ring for a heavyweight bout on the nature of the fractured modern soul. One prays the dialectical sparring avoids the heavy-handed earnestness that usually plagues contemporary novels attempting to map the cosmos in three hundred pages. Give me cynicism wrapped in silk. Give me prose that tastes like expensive iodine. 

Mary Gaitskill blurbs it with a breathless mention of the ineffable. Naturally. Critics love throwing around mysticism when a book refuses to explain its own symbolism. Yet, the tension between the domestic debris—a hidden family secret—and the cosmic radio static of the narrator's brain-damaged epiphany holds immense promise. Or it will completely implode under the weight of its own ambition. Either way, the cocktail party chatter will be insufferable. I am buying extra champagne just to survive the launch parties. 

Now, if you will excuse me, my wealth manager is calling on the private line because some hedge-fund barbarian is aggressively outbidding me for an obscure seventeenth-century Flemish oil painting at Christie's, and I simply must go ruin someone's financial life before luncheon.
