Elbows pinned to my ribs, someone’s backpack wedged directly against my spine while the L-train rattles violently toward Bedford Avenue. Perfect conditions to dissect a historical fiction synopsis about World War II Paris. Because nothing screams literary focus quite like a screeching hydraulic brake and a stranger coughing unmasked into my ear. 

Fiona Schneider wants us to care about *The Perfumer’s Secret: A WWII Novel*. Or maybe she wants me to just hit a strict word count so I can close my laptop before the next stop. Let us be entirely honest about what we are looking at here. It is another dual-timeline historical puzzle box. We get Stella in the present—or a slightly adjacent present where corporate or family perfumery failures keep people awake at night—and Iris in 1939, dodging fascist thugs and boarding the *Orient Express* with five magical scents. Five scents. Like Infinity Stones for the cosmetics industry. 

### The Olfactory Escape Hatch

Memory tied to scent is an old trick. Proust did it with a madeleine. Schneider does it with a missing journal containing pages torn out by someone who clearly understood the dramatic pacing requirements of mid-list commercial fiction. Yet I am contradicting myself instantly. Earlier I thought the dual-timeline trope was completely dead, but watching how the synopsis forces these two eras to collide through a literal notebook makes me oddly nostalgic. Or maybe that is just the exhaust fumes leaking into the subway car. 

The synopsis leans heavily on convenience. Iris just happens to be a perfumer who can travel across a war-torn continent under the guise of meeting an apprentice named Alessandro to brew concoctions while the Nazis roll through France. No one questions it. The Gestapo apparently took a smoke break while the *Orient Express* rolled past. But who reads historical war romance for gritty documentary realism? You read it to feel transported while a teenager plays trap music on a bluetooth speaker two inches from your skull. 

### Smelling Salts for the Soul

The thematic machinery here runs on predictable tracks. War destroys, but perfume preserves. Loss strips everything away, except for top notes and base notes. Iris has to save her mentor's family from Mussolini's thugs, which sounds less like historical tragedy and more like an action-adventure pitch greenlit by an algorithm that just watched *All the Light We Cannot See* get adapted. Think Kristin Hannah meets a high-end department store counter during the holiday rush. 

And yet. 

There is an undeniable pull to the premise. The contrast between the City of Light plunged into actual darkness and a modern descendant staring down bankruptcy via dead relatives' notes gives readers the exact emotional whiplash they buy these books for. It is safe peril. It is danger you can close and slide into a tote bag when your stop finally arrives. 

I am going to go smoke a pipe and ponder the futility of modern existence.
