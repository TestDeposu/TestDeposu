Nathaniel Philbrick's forthcoming *The Rush: California Gold, the Civil War, and the Making of the Modern World* reads like a promise, a siren's call to the historians who have long been enthralled by the California Gold Rush's mythos. But what lies within the covers of this National Book Award winner's latest magnum opus? A story of American exceptionalism, perhaps, or a cautionary tale of the perils of unchecked capitalism? I suspect, however, that *The Rush* will instead be another instance of the author's fondness for romanticizing the American past, that old trope of the rugged individualist triumphing over adversity.

Philbrick's prose often veers towards the grand, a sweeping panorama of American history that threatens to engulf the reader in its sheer velocity. But what about the nuances, the messy contradictions that make for compelling historical fiction? Does Philbrick's vision of California gold, with its promises of instant wealth and boundless possibility, also acknowledge the systemic inequalities that fueled this very rush—Native American dispossession, Chinese immigrant labor, the racial tensions that simmered just beneath the surface of the mining camps? I, for one, wouldn't be surprised if Philbrick chose to sidestep these complexities, opting instead for a more crowd-pleasing narrative that revels in the romance of America's Wild West.

But what about the character of Samuel Brannan, the merchant-showman who finds himself at the center of vigilantism in San Francisco? Is he more than just a cipher, a straw man to be pitted against the Southern power broker William Gwin? Philbrick mentions Brannan's "drawn into the center of early vigilantism," but what does this mean, exactly? That he's a passive observer, swept up in the tumultuous politics of California? Or is he perhaps more integral to the story, a symbol of the rough-and-tumble capitalism that defined this era?

—In a move that should give even the most hardened literary critics pause, Philbrick is rumored to be collaborating with a prominent film director to adapt *The Rush* into a feature-length drama. This would be a cinematic version of *The Exorcist*'s infamous 'head-spinning' scene, a mess of conflicting ideas and competing interests that threatens to blow the very lid off the American narrative.

The Rush's supposed focus on the intersection of California and the Civil War feels like a bold, innovative move—especially given the relative dearth of literature on this period of American history. Yet, I remain skeptical, my mind wandering instead to the more sensationalized accounts of this era, the pulp fiction versions that reduced complex historical events to simple tales of good vs. evil.

### A Question of Timing

Is it merely a coincidence that *The Rush* is set to release in the midst of a national reckoning with our country's history of racism and inequality? Or is this a deliberate attempt by Philbrick to tap into the zeitgeist, to offer a timely reminder of the American experience's more unsavory aspects? Either way, I imagine *The Rush* will be a commercial success, its grand narrative and larger-than-life characters resonating with readers eager to revisit a more storied, more heroic past.

—And don't even get me started on the problematic implications of Philbrick's historical narrative, a thinly veiled defense of Manifest Destiny that threatens to whitewash the brutal realities of American expansionism. Can anyone say 'Wendy Doniger's 'The Hindus'*?

Philbrick's writing does possess a certain gravitas, a sense of authority that commands attention. But can we trust that authority, given the author's propensity for mythologizing historical events? Is he merely offering a fresh spin on familiar tales, or is there genuine insight to be gleaned from *The Rush*? Only time (and a thorough reading of the book itself) will tell.

I, for one, expect *The Rush* to leave me feeling unsatisfied, like a dinner guest whose appetites have been cruelly tantalized but never quite satiated. But what about the reader who craves a panoramic epic, a sweeping narrative that redefines the contours of American history? Will they find *The Rush* to be a revelation, or simply another disappointment in a long line of grand, but ultimately hollow, historical narratives?

For now, I'll continue to await the release of *The Rush*, my skepticism tempered by the knowledge that even the most questionable historians can sometimes produce something unexpected, something truly remarkable.

And now, I need to appraise my antique watch collection for insurance purposes.
