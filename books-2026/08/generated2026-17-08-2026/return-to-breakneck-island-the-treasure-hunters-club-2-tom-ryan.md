The art of constructing an entire novel around a single, isolated setting — in this case, Breakneck Island, a foreboding outcrop off the coast of Maple Bay. Tom Ryan's *Return to Breakneck Island* promises an alluring mystery, replete with long-buried secrets and the requisite small-town charm. One can almost smell the saltwater and decay emanating from the crumbling lighthouse that stands sentinel over the rocky shores.

But let's get real for a second — I'm stuck writing this review, and I'm already feeling the weight of my impending word count. The synopsis is a masterclass in vagueness, leaving me to fill in the blanks with my own fevered imagination. I mean, what's the deal with the MacLeary family? They just... disappeared? Without a trace? It's a bit too convenient, if you ask me. And don't even get me started on the "ruthless storm" that apparently precipitated their vanishing act. It's a bit too on-the-nose, a bit too... obvious.

And yet, despite my reservations, I find myself drawn to the disparate cast of characters that Ryan has assembled. There's Peter Barnett, the brooding transplant trying to piece his life back together; Dandy Feltzen, the wistful teenager searching for new beginnings; and Cass Jones, the successful author with a cryptic tip for a haunting new story. It's a veritable melting pot of angst and creative energy, all swirling together in a maelstrom of mystery and suspense.

But what's the real story here? Is it the MacLearys' disappearance, or is it something more sinister? Something that's been lurking just beneath the surface of Maple Bay's idyllic facade? I'm reminded of the works of Laura Lippman, who's also mastered the art of crafting intricate, small-town mysteries that are equal parts character study and whodunit. And let's not forget the obligatory nod to Agatha Christie, whose influence is evident in the isolated setting and the gathering storm of secrets and lies.

### The Island as a Character

Breakneck Island itself is a character, a brooding presence that looms large over the proceedings. It's a place of rugged beauty, where the rocky shores meet the unforgiving sea. And yet, it's also a place of darkness and foreboding, where the past and present collide in unexpected ways. I'm intrigued by the way Ryan has woven the island's history into the fabric of the narrative, hinting at a deeper, more sinister truth that's waiting to be uncovered.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. I still have no idea what the actual plot of this book entails, beyond the vague hints and teasers that Ryan has provided. And honestly, I'm not sure I care. Sometimes, it's the journey that matters, not the destination. Sometimes, it's the atmosphere and the characters that make a book worth reading, rather than the plot itself.
