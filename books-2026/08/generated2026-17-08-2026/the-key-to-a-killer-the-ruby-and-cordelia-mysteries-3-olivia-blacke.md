**Author:** Olivia Blacke  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-20 APL

The synopsis for *The Key to a Killer* is so deliberately opaque it feels like a ghost itself—hauntingly vague, just enough to tease but not enough to satisfy. It’s the literary equivalent of a dimly lit hallway where you can *almost* see the shape of a body, but the details are swallowed by the shadows. And yet—this is exactly the kind of ambiguity that makes the Ruby and Cordelia Mysteries so addictive. Because what’s the fun in a ghost who remembers everything? The real tension lies in the gaps, in the way Cordelia’s fragmented memories become a puzzle that Ruby can’t ignore. It’s not just about solving a murder—it’s about confronting the idea that some truths are so painful they’re buried under layers of denial, even in death.

But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t tell us *why* Cordelia’s memories are coming back now. Is it trauma? Is it Ruby’s persistence? Is it something darker, something that suggests the ghost isn’t just a witness but a victim still bound to the scene? The way it’s phrased—*"what led to Cordelia’s death?"*—implies a slow unraveling, a case where the killer’s motives are personal, intimate. That’s the kind of psychological horror that makes *The Silent Patient* or *Sharp Objects* so gripping, but with a supernatural twist. And if Blacke’s pulling it off, it’ll feel like a love letter to the gothic, where the past isn’t just a ghost—it’s a living, breathing entity that refuses to stay buried.

Yet—*and this is where the synopsis fails*—there’s no sense of the *mechanics* of the investigation. Is Ruby’s skepticism about ghosts a real barrier, or is she fully invested in the supernatural? Does Cordelia’s ghostly state limit what she can reveal, or is she just as resourceful as she was in life? The Ruby and Cordelia Mysteries have thrived on this balance between the mundane and the uncanny, but here, the synopsis leans so hard into the mystery that it forgets to ground us in the *how*. It’s like watching a horror movie where the scares are great, but the logic of how the killer operates is nonexistent. And that’s a problem, because without that, the tension feels like a house of cards waiting to collapse.

Speaking of logic—*why does this remind me of the time I tried to explain to my nephew why *Bluey* isn’t actually a good show?* He kept asking, *"But why do the dogs just *do* things?"* And I’d say, *"Because it’s a cartoon, you idiot!"* But *The Key to a Killer* isn’t a cartoon—it’s supposed to be a mystery, and mysteries need rules. The synopsis gives us the *what* (a murder, a ghost, a lingering question), but it doesn’t give us the *how*. And that’s where the real work happens. That’s where the reader’s brain gets to do its thing, filling in the blanks with their own theories, their own fears. But if the book doesn’t deliver on that promise, it’ll feel like a cheat code in a video game—too easy, too convenient.

The thematic potential here is *huge*. Cordelia’s death isn’t just a crime—it’s a wound that’s been left open, festering. And Ruby’s refusal to let it go isn’t just about justice; it’s about facing the fact that some people don’t get to choose how they die, and some relationships don’t get to choose how they end. That’s the kind of emotional core that makes *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo* or *Gone Girl* so compelling, but with a supernatural edge. If Blacke can make Cordelia’s ghost feel like a character who’s still fighting for her own story, even in death, then this book could be something special. But if she just treats it like another murder mystery with a ghostly twist, it’ll feel like a gimmick.

And that’s the real question: *is this a ghost story, or is it a mystery?* Because if it’s the former, it’ll lean into the eerie, the unsettling, the way *The Haunting of Hill House* or *The Turn of the Screw* make us question what’s real. But if it’s the latter, it’ll be a traditional whodunit with a supernatural layer, like *The Woman in the Window* or *The Last House on Needless Street*. The synopsis doesn’t tell us which it is, and that’s frustrating. Because if it’s the former, the killer’s identity might not even matter—what matters is the *why*. And if it’s the latter, then the ghost is just a red herring, and we’re left with a story that’s more about Ruby’s personal demons than anything else.

But here’s the thing I *know*: Olivia Blacke doesn’t write books that are just about solving crimes. She writes books about the people who solve them, about the scars they carry, about the way trauma shapes them. So if *The Key to a Killer* is anything like the first two books, it’s not just about finding a killer—it’s about Ruby and Cordelia facing the fact that some wounds never fully heal, even when one of them is already dead. And that’s the kind of story that sticks with you, the kind that makes you sit up at night and wonder, *what if I were the one left behind?*
