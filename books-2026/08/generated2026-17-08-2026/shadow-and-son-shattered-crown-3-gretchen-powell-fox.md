**Author:** Gretchen Powell Fox  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-08 APL

The saccharine glow of newlywed bliss, forever tainted by the dark allure of power and the crushing weight of demonic possession. Kazimir and Arabella, the star-crossed lovers of Gretchen Powell Fox's *Shadow and Son*, are about to embark on a honeymoon that promises to be as treacherous as it is tantalizing. 

It's a little disconcerting that the synopsis doesn't quite commit to the darker aspects of Kazimir's past, instead choosing to dance around them with an air of mystery. One can't help but wonder if this is a deliberate choice, meant to preserve the air of intrigue that surrounds these two villainous lovebirds, or if it's simply a case of the author not wanting to show her hand too early. Whatever the reason, it's a move that leaves this critic feeling more than a little skeptical.

And yet, despite this vagueness, there's something undeniably alluring about the world that Fox has created. A world where the boundaries between good and evil are blissfully blurred, and the only constant is the pursuit of power. It's a world that feels almost... familiar, like a twisted echo of the *Assistant to the Villain* series, or perhaps the deliciously wicked *Dreadful*. One can almost imagine the sardonic wit of Kazimir and Arabella's banter, the way they dance around each other with a mixture of affection and animosity. 

But what truly sets *Shadow and Son* apart is its unapologetic embracing of the 'villainous' trope. These are characters who are unashamedly, unapologetically evil, and yet... they're also somehow likable. It's a delicate balancing act, one that requires a deft hand and a deep understanding of the complexities of human nature. And if the synopsis is any indication, Fox seems more than up to the task.

Or does she? The more one reads the synopsis, the more one begins to wonder if Fox is simply relying on the same old tropes, rather than truly subverting them. Is Kazimir's demonic past merely a convenient plot device, or is it something more? And what of Arabella's stolen memories - are they a genuine source of tension, or simply a contrived way to create conflict? 

But I suppose that's the thing about *Shadow and Son* - it's a book that promises to be as much about the journey as the destination. A journey that's fraught with peril, and yet somehow, impossibly, romantic. It's a journey that I, for one, am more than a little eager to embark on.
