**Author:** Keith Rosson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-22 APL

People still want neat little bows on everything. They crave tidy resolutions where the missing child walks through the front door, homework in hand, and the parents weep tears of sweet redemption under a porch light that actually works. It is pathetic. Life does not hand out clean closes, and yet we shovel money at authors who promise that trauma can be neatly metabolized in three hundred pages. 

Eli Lamp is an ex-detective, ex-addict, and a father whose daughter vanished a decade ago. He lives in a rotting trailer, drowning in debt to a local drug gang called the Crooked Wheel, preserving a shrine to a child who is likely dust. Naturally, a convenient murder drops in his lap. Solve it, and the debt vanishes. It is the oldest, dustiest trope in the noir playbook. 

My ten-year-old has been obsessed with this animated series about a magical blue dog for months, and I am losing my mind. Every single episode wraps up with a gentle life lesson, soft acoustic strumming, and a warm parental hug that solves systemic emotional ruin in seven minutes flat. Why do we feed children this absolute poison? Life is a slow bleed of unreturned text messages and accumulating debt, not an afternoon cartoon where daddy learns how to communicate his feelings. If only real grief could be resolved by playing pretend in the backyard. 

Keith Rosson—fresh off *Coffin Moon* and *Fever House*—seems poised to drag us through the mud again. Clay McLeod Chapman calls him the reigning champ of horror noir, which usually means a lot of gratuitous violence masking a hollow character arc. Yet there is a distinct grit here that appeals to my current cynicism. 

### Rotting Trailers and Broken Pledges

The synopsis leans heavily on familiar genre machinery. An investigation spanning decades. A shady conspiracy linking current bloodshed to past sorrows. And, of course, the obligatory boogeyman in the woods. Is it a literal monster, or a manifestation of shared guilt? The synopsis refuses to commit, dangling the w-word—witch—like cheap bait. Add it to your TBR, or don't. It will likely sell a million copies to people who think a happy ending is just a tragedy you stopped reading five minutes early. 

I refuse to buy into the hype of miraculous healing. The woods do not give back what they take. They just wait for the next broken person to wander off the asphalt and into the dark. 

I have to go untangle the dog's leash from the dining table.
