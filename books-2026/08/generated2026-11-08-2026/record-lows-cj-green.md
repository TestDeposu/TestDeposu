**Page Count:** Unknown

Winter in Oneira, New York, 1977. A meteorological disaster wrapped in a missing-persons bulletin. One reads the premise of *Record Lows* and immediately reaches for an umbrella, or perhaps a cyanide capsule, given the sheer exhaustion promised by domestic fiction that insists on unearthing skeletons while the weather acts as a heavy-handed metaphor for emotional repression. We are being promised an intimate and epic family drama. We are always promised epics. Usually, we get a glorified tax audit with emotional baggage. 

### The Currency of Secret Ruin

Harmon Pond is drowning in debt. His daughter Teresa wants a Pulitzer. His wife Pauline is guarding Italian American Catholic trauma like a dragon atop a pile of tarnished silver. And beneath all this suburban decay lies the classic trope of the convenient disappearance—a creditor vanishes, and everyone breathes a sigh of relief until the snow falls and the secrets begin to rot in the frost. It is a narrative dynamic as predictable as the Andrea Doria hitting the bottom of the Atlantic. 

Wait. Let us rephrase that entirely. Predictable is perhaps too harsh an indictment for a debut. It might just be aggressively familiar. 

### The Geography of Freezing to Death

CJ Green’s staging relies heavily on the Buffalo blizzard of ’77, a historic whiteout that forces people indoors to confront the misery they spend fifty weeks a year avoiding. Yet, the children—middle-child Fran sneaking out, youngest-child Lonnie courting hypothermia—are classic literary scaffolding. They exist solely to wander into danger while the adults are too self-absorbed to notice. It is the JonBenét playbook filtered through mid-century nostalgia. 

And why do we constantly romanticize the mid-century family unit as a ticking time bomb? Every single debut novel arriving this season seems contractually obligated to feature a patriarch on the verge of bankruptcy and a matriarch hiding behind a rosary and a pastry crust. Give us ambition. Give us a crime that does not involve a mortgage payment or an errant uncle. 

The praise from Rumaan Alam and Claire Lombardo tells you precisely what demographic this book targets: readers who demand their misery wrapped in elegant prose and served with a side of mild suburban panic. I suspect the ending will offer a tidy little epiphany right as the snow melts. Happy endings are just delayed tragedies anyway.
