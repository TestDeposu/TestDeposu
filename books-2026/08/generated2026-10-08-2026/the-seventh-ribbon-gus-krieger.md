**Author:** Gus Krieger  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-27 APL

The synopsis of *The Seventh Ribbon* is a glittering, if deliberately opaque, invitation into a genre that’s been quietly thriving in the literary margins—what I call *the grieving genre*, where loss isn’t just a plot device but the atmospheric pressure that warps everything else. And yet—*and yet*—this isn’t just another *Me Before You* wannabe. The typewriter ribbons? That’s the kind of specificity that makes me sit up. Because magic in literature isn’t about grand gestures; it’s about the *friction* of the mundane. Think of *The Night Circus*’s ribbon-like tent threads, but here, the magic is the *act* of writing itself, the way ink bleeds into reality like a bad metaphor. Or like the way grief bleeds into your coffee cup at 3 AM.

The synopsis leans hard into the *what if* framework—Caroline’s attic discovery isn’t just a MacGuffin; it’s a *structural* choice. The typewriter ribbons force us to interrogate the very mechanics of storytelling. But here’s the rub: the synopsis doesn’t *tell* us how the magic works. Is it a literal spell? A psychological projection? A metaphor for the way grief rewrites memory? The vagueness is deliberate—it’s the same trick *The Invisible Life of Addie LaRue* pulls with its time-loop ambiguity. And yet, unlike *Addie*, which leans into existential dread, *The Seventh Ribbon* seems to want to be *funny*. The synopsis mentions "riveting, funny and heartbreaking by turns"—which is a bold claim. Because the grieving genre has a reputation for being either maudlin or nihilistic. To pull off humor here, Krieger would need to channel the dry wit of *The Unhoneymooners* or the absurdist levity of *The House in the Cerulean Sea*, but with the emotional core of *Where’d You Go, Bernadette*. That’s a tightrope.

The character of Caroline Ora is already a fascinating study in *liminal grief*—that space between mourning and acceptance where you’re neither fully dead to the world nor fully alive in it. The snowbound cottage? Classic gothic isolation, but the oceanfront detail adds a layer of *restlessness*, like the sea is pulling her under even as she’s trying to hold onto the shore. And Forest’s death—hit-and-run, violent, sudden—is the kind of tragedy that *demands* a rewrite. But here’s where the synopsis stumbles. It doesn’t tell us *why* Caroline is a poet. Was she a romantic? A cynic? A woman who believed in love as a verb, not just a noun? That detail would ground her entire arc. Right now, she’s just a blank slate, which is fine for a debut, but it also means the book risks feeling like a *projection* of the reader’s own grief rather than a distinct narrative.

The thematic tension here is *control vs. chaos*—Caroline’s attempt to rewrite reality is a classic *feminist fairy tale* trope, but with a twist. Most stories about women rewriting fate (*The Time Traveler’s Wife*, *The Night Circus*) end in tragedy because the system is rigged. But the synopsis hints at something more *subversive*. The ribbons aren’t just tools; they’re *limits*. Every wish has a cost, every miracle a reckoning—this sounds like *The Perks of Being a Wallflower* meets *Practical Magic*, but with the *mechanical* metaphor of typewriter ribbons. And that’s where the book could get interesting: if the magic isn’t just about love, but about *language itself*. Can you rewrite a life? Or are you just rewriting the story you tell yourself?

The comparison to *In Five Years* is telling—both books play with *temporal grief*, but *The Seventh Ribbon* feels like it wants to be more *playful*. The synopsis’s mention of "whimsical" is either a promise or a red flag. Whimsy in grief stories often feels like a cop-out, like the author’s way of saying, "It’s okay to laugh, just don’t look too closely." But if Krieger pulls it off—if the humor isn’t just surface-level but *structural*—this could be the next big thing in *emotional sci-fi*, the kind of book that makes you question whether magic is just another word for *hope*.

I predict this will be a *cult favorite*, not a blockbuster. It’s too specific, too *personal* to go mainstream. But in the right hands—like the hands of a reader who’s lost someone and needs to believe, just for a moment, that the universe might bend—it could be *devastating*. And that’s the mark of great art: it doesn’t just entertain. It *haunts*.

Now, if you’ll excuse me, I have to remember to thaw the chicken. Again.
