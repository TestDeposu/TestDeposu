**Author:** Cassandra Khaw  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

August in Manhattan is a sticky, miserable affair, yet here I am, sweating through my bespoke linen blazer while trying to figure out why Cassandra Khaw’s latest offering, *Find Me Where It Ends*, insists on dragging its feet before page one even opens. As an executive who has rejected a thousand bloated manuscripts this quarter alone, I care about one thing above all else: momentum. Does this premise move, or does it idle? 

Let us dissect the anatomy of this blurb. Khaw gives us a woman, a doorstep, and a mythic hound. Standard gothic boilerplate. Yet—and this is where my interest is begrudgingly piqued—the pacing outlined in this synopsis is dangerously slow. We have a protagonist, Antigone, inviting Death's spectral puppy inside for tea and introspection. 

### The Slow Burn Trap

How long can a narrative sustain itself on mere self-reflection before the reader tosses the volume across a cramped Brooklyn apartment? Khaw’s bibliography—think *The Salt Grows Heavy*—relies on visceral, atmospheric shock. But interiority is a notoriously tricky beast. If Antigone spends the first hundred pages simply pondering her burnout and the pieces of herself she sacrificed for ungrateful relations, the structural integrity of the novella will collapse under its own melancholic weight. 

Pacing is not merely about velocity. It is about tension. And frankly, this synopsis lacks teeth. 

### The Economics of Exhaustion

Look at the thematic framing here. Burnout. The hollowing out of the self. It is the literary zeitgeist of our exhausted late-capitalist moment, echoing the bleak, atmospheric exhaustion found in Ottessa Moshfegh’s work or the folkloric dread of Mariana Enríquez. Yet, art requires propulsion. A good death coming at a price is a compelling hook—truly, what is the toll?—but the blurb stops dead right when the stakes should skyrocket. 

### The Verdict

Will it sell? Absolutely. Khaw’s cult following guarantees a robust pre-order window. But as an editor whose desk is currently buried under three competing drafts of grief-drenched horror, I worry about the middle act. Will the narrative drag like a humid August afternoon? Or will Khaw deliver the jagged, razor-sharp prose we expect? I suspect we are in for a lingering, atmospheric slog. Brilliant prose, perhaps. But narrative stagnation is a luxury few readers tolerate in our current hyper-accelerated attention economy. 

Somebody get my assistant. The caviar I ordered from room service is far too salty, and I refuse to tolerate culinary incompetence on top of sluggish editorial pacing.
