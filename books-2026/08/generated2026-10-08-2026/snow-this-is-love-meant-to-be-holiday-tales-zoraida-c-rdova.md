Sweat is currently dripping off my nose into the prep-sink, and the dinner rush hasn't even hit its peak, but my mind is currently stuck on the upcoming release of *Snow This Is Love*. It’s a strange juxtaposition—this collection of holiday-themed Disney reimagining’s sitting next to my station, amidst the chaos of searing scallops and barking line cooks. 

### The Heavyweight Division of Rom-Coms

These authors aren't lightweight fighters. They are the heavy hitters. You have Zoraida Córdova, Jasmine Guillory, and Christina Lauren—the pound-for-pound champions of modern romance. When I look at a lineup like this, I’m not looking for the technical perfection of a defensive fighter. I’m looking for the knockout blow. I want the emotion that hits like a perfectly timed counter-hook in the twelfth round.

And yet, looking at this synopsis, I have to ask: where is the friction? 

The setup for "Siren in the Snow" suggests a lost luggage incident as the catalyst for conflict. Listen, I’ve seen middleweights lose a title because their corner forgot their mouthguard. That is real stakes. But can a lost suitcase really sustain the emotional weight required for a satisfying romance? I’m skeptical. Then you have the "dream baking competition" in *Beauty and the Baking Battle*. It sounds lovely. It sounds sweet. But does it have the intensity of a high-stakes championship bout? I hope Guillory pushes the stakes higher than a collapsed soufflé, or else the reader is going to feel as cheated as a crowd watching a fixed fight.

### Footwork and Storytelling

The thematic core here is the reimagining of classic Disney lore. It’s a trope—and a tired one, if I’m being honest—that relies heavily on the audience's preexisting affection for the source material. It’s the literary equivalent of a fighter leaning on the ropes to catch their breath. It works, but it’s not graceful. Still, Julie Murphy in "Deck the Heels" is tackling the stepmother narrative—a classic heel turn—and that gives me hope. If there’s one thing we know about professional combat, it’s that a good villain makes for the most memorable night of your life. 

I predict that Jesse Q. Sutanto’s entry, "Dreaming of a Perfect Lunar New Year," will be the dark horse of this anthology. The mention of a "possible curse" is the kind of aggressive storytelling I appreciate. It adds a layer of uncertainty that the other stories, based on the synopsis, seem to lack. It’s the difference between a technical sparring match and a brawl in a dive bar. Give me the brawl. Give me the stakes that feel like they could actually break something.

The vagueness of the synopsis is a bit frustrating—a lot of "dreaming" and "magic" and not enough grit. But hey, I’m just a cook in a kitchen with aching feet. I’ll probably buy it anyway. My laptop battery is at 1% and I can't find the charger.
