**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-17 APL

The air in my study is thick with the scent of aged leather and the faintest hint of ozone—summer’s last stubborn heat clinging to the windows like a reluctant guest. One can almost taste the irony of it all: the gods are plotting ruin, yet here I am, lounging in a chair that cost more than most people’s mortgages, sipping a martini that’s been chilling since noon, while the world of *Soul of Shadow* burns with the kind of romantic tension that makes one wonder if Emma Noyes has ever actually read *The Great Gatsby*—or if she’s just channeling it through a particularly sultry filter.

*Dreams of Ruin* arrives like a thunderstorm after a prolonged drought—dramatic, inevitable, and with the potential to leave everything in its wake. The synopsis is a masterclass in controlled ambiguity, the kind that makes one’s fingers twitch with anticipation. Charlie Hudson, our reluctant heroine, wakes from her Trial to find herself transformed into the very monster she despises: a nightmare. And not just any nightmare—one with the weight of divine lineage pressing down on her like the crushing heat of a London summer afternoon. The imagery here is *deliciously* cinematic, the kind that makes one imagine a slow-motion shot of Charlie’s shadow stretching unnaturally long, her reflection in a blackened mirror warping into something inhuman. It’s the kind of visual poetry that would make *Caitlin Starling*—bless her—nod in approval.

But let’s talk about the *real* tension here: the love story. Or rather, the *lack* of it. Because what makes this synopsis so tantalizing isn’t just the magic or the gods—it’s the slow-burning, simmering, *will-they-won’t-they* dynamic between Charlie and Elias Everhart. The synopsis drops the bomb that he’s her "once-sworn enemy" who has somehow become her "closest ally." And then—oh, then—it delivers the kicker: *"being near Elias didn’t make the darkness within Charlie grow bigger and more all-consuming by the day."* That’s not just romantic tension; that’s *literary alchemy*. It’s the kind of writing that makes one think of *Sarah J. Maas* at her most melodramatic, but with the psychological depth of *Tessa Bailey*—if Tessa had ever written a book where the love interest was also the source of the protagonist’s greatest fear.

Yet—*yet*—there’s a vagueness here that borders on the lazy. The synopsis teases the idea of Charlie’s magic "malfunctioning," but it doesn’t *show* us what that looks like. Is it a physical manifestation? A psychological unraveling? Does it manifest as literal shadows bleeding into the real world, or is it something more insidious, like the way love can twist into obsession? The lack of specificity is frustrating, like being handed a menu where the descriptions are all "delicious" and "unforgettable" but the actual dishes remain a mystery. One can almost hear the groans of the *Twilight* fanbase, who will no doubt arrive with their pitchforks and torches, demanding more clarity—or at least the promise of a werewolf equivalent to Jacob and Edward’s dynamic.

The thematic weight here is undeniable. Charlie’s struggle isn’t just about mastering her newfound powers; it’s about reconciling the war within her. And what better metaphor for that than love? The synopsis doesn’t shy away from the dark romance trope—*"the only thing worse than becoming a nightmare, is loving one"*—but it doesn’t wallow in it either. It’s a balance, a tightrope walk between the poetic and the perilous. It’s the kind of writing that makes one think of *Katherine Arden*, where the stakes are high, the emotions are raw, and the world feels like it’s hanging by a thread.

But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t *tell* us enough. It doesn’t *show* us the training montages, the whispered confessions, the moments where Charlie’s shadow magic fails her in the most critical of ways. It doesn’t give us the visceral, the tactile, the *sensory* details that would make this feel like more than just another installment in the "chosen one" saga. And that’s a shame, because the potential is *so* there.

I can already see the cover: a silhouette of Charlie, her shadow stretching like a living thing, Elias looming in the background, his expression unreadable. The title in bold, jagged letters, as if carved into stone. It’s the kind of cover that would make one’s pulse quicken, that would make one *need* to know what happens next. But will it deliver? Only time—and the patience of a wealthy literary sociologist—will tell.

For now, I’ll leave you with this: if Noyes can pull off the psychological depth of *Heart of Mischief* while adding the romantic tension of *A Court of Thorns and Roses*, then *Dreams of Ruin* will be a triumph. But if she leans too heavily into the "chosen one" tropes, if she doesn’t give us enough to *feel* the stakes, then it might just be another entry in the crowded pantheon of YA fantasy that promises more than it delivers.

And speaking of promises—did I mention the country club’s new membership application? It’s a *disgrace*. The wait for drinks is longer than the wait for the next *Soul of Shadow* book, and the Wi-Fi cuts out every time I try to order a copy of *The Bell Jar* for my summer reading list.
