**Author:** Jennifer E. Smith  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-15 APL

Thirty-nine is a hell of an age to decide you’re going to force destiny to blink. Jennifer E. Smith’s *Rabbit Rabbit* gives us Billie, a film editor facing down the ticking biological clock with the kind of mid-life urgency that usually results in either a new puppy or a very expensive mistake. The premise? A New Year’s Eve meet-cute in Florence that is, frankly, teetering on the edge of a Hallmark script. She wants a baby; he’s a Scottish conservationist who thinks children are noise pollution. 

### The Calculus of Compromise

The conflict here is as old as the rom-com genre itself. Can you mold a person into the supporting actor your life script requires, or will you just end up resenting the rewrite? Smith is positioning this as a study of "paths not taken," but let’s be honest—it’s a study in how we justify our own stubbornness. Billie is choosing between two distinct versions of the future. One involves a fertility clinic, the other involves a man who represents the "wrong time" trope. This binary choice feels manufactured, a classic case of plot-driven agony meant to keep the reader guessing until the final page. 

It’s giving me major vibes of Rebecca Serle’s *In Five Years*, but with significantly less emotional carnage and more European travel aesthetics. Will Smith lean into the grit of the fertility journey, or will she gloss over the reality of it to keep the vibe breezy? My bet? It’s going to be a sugary, predictable romp through beautiful cities that serves as a backdrop for a very tidy epiphany.

### The Problem with Perfect Men

Arthur sounds insufferable. A 44-year-old bachelor who travels the world and hasn't settled down? Please. We have seen this archetype in every airport-kiosk bestseller since the late nineties. He is the human equivalent of a glass of lukewarm white wine—pleasant, unremarkable, and ultimately disappointing. Yet, the friction between his child-free existence and Billie’s yearning for motherhood is where the actual narrative tension lives. If Smith chooses to resolve this through some miraculous change of heart from Arthur, I’m going to throw the ARC across the room. If it stays grounded in the uncomfortable reality that love rarely fixes structural incompatibility, she might actually have a book worth the paper it’s printed on. 

But I’m skeptical. The synopsis is painfully vague about how they reconcile these two irreconcilable goals. Maybe they don’t? Perhaps the "life-changing year" is actually about Billie realizing that her dream of motherhood doesn't require a partner, or that her dream of partnership doesn't require a nursery. Or maybe it’s just filler until December 2026. Either way, it’s a narrative experiment in the sunk-cost fallacy. 

I have to go make a Spotify playlist based on this terrible book.
