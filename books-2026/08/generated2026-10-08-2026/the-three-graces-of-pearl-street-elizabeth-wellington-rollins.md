**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-01 APL

With the upcoming release of Elizabeth Wellington Rollins's *The Three Graces of Pearl Street*, one cannot help but ponder the revival of a most intriguing narrative tradition: the intergenerational family drama. Like our dear friend Ann Weinstock's *In the Middle of Somewhere*, *The Three Graces* promises to weave an intricate exploration of family dynamics—yet, unlike Weinstock's work,.Rollins' focus appears to be on the feminine, as embodied by three generations of women sharing the same first name.

—And what a stroke of genius this is! By employing the Roman myth of the Three Graces, Rollins sets the stage for a rich thematic examination of femininity, tradition, and identity. One cannot help but think of the inimitable Anne Tyler's ability to imbue her characters with an effortless, if occasionally frustrating, humanity. Will Rollins's Three Graces meet a similar fate?

Graziella, a ninety-six-year-old former nun, is the matriarch of this family drama, running the parish's food kitchen with a fierce dedication that borders on the obsessive. Her daughter, Grace, a respected psychology professor, appears accomplished and composed, yet finds herself unmoored as her personal life falters. Ella, Graziella's granddaughter and Grace's daughter, is a vegan, anti-capitalistic free spirit who longs for a place to belong. But—Hold your horses,—the question remains whether Rollins will opt for a more straightforward portrayal of her protagonists or instead craft a more complex, if occasionally maddening, exploration of the human experience.

The Three Graces' exploration of the delicate balance between tradition and modernity is nothing new in literature, yet *The Three Graces* appears poised to offer a fresh perspective on this tired trope. Rollins's focus on the ways in which women reconcile their individuality with their familial responsibilities is both timely and timeless. And what of the titular Graces themselves— will they be depicted as one-dimensional symbols of femininity, or will they exhibit the depth and nuance that we've come to expect from contemporary fiction?

Ultimately, one cannot help but wonder if Rollins will be able to avoid the pitfalls of the more sentimental family dramas that populate the contemporary literary scene. Will her exploration of the Three Graces' lives be as nuanced and insightful as it is moving? Only time will tell, but one thing is certain: this reviewer is eager to see how Rollins's vision plays out on the page.

I do, however, have some reservations about the synopsis's rather...vague portrayal of the novel's themes. While I understand the desire to pique the reader's interest, a bit more specificity would be greatly appreciated—specifically regarding Rollins's treatment of the Three Graces' relationships with one another. 

Still, I suppose only a literary Luddite would quibble with the finer details at this point.

In any case, I shall be eagerly awaiting the release of *The Three Graces of Pearl Street*, hoping that it will deliver on its promise of a "truly captivating" reading experience.
