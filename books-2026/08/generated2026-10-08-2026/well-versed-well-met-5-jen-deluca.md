**Author:** Jen DeLuca  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-22 APL

As I gaze out at the sweltering summer air, the smell of sweat and sunscreen wafting through the air, I find myself drawn to the synopsis of *Well Versed*, the latest offering from USA Today bestselling author Jen DeLuca. It's a Romeo and Juliet-esque tale of second chances, set against the whimsical backdrop of the Renaissance Faire. But, as we all know, there's more to a story than meets the eye — especially when it comes to female agency and the constraints placed upon it.

The protagonist, Caitlin Parker, finds herself back at the Faire after a heartbreak-fueled hiatus. We're told she's twenty-four, unemployed, and stuck in a dead-end position as a substitute queen, yet her passion for the Faire remains unwavering. This is where things get interesting: Caitlin's not just a damsel in distress, waiting to be rescued by a dashing hero. No, she's a complex, multifaceted character, with a past that's informed her present. Her relationship with Alex Wright, the sword-fighting, Shakespeare-spouting young man who broke her heart, will no doubt be a focal point of the story.

But, let's be real, we've seen this trope before. We've seen the "second chance at love" narrative, the "rediscovery of oneself" arc. It's a familiar narrative thread, one that's been woven into the fabric of women's fiction for decades. Think Sally Field in *Steel Magnolias*, or Jennifer Weiner's *Who Do You Love* — these stories often feature women navigating love, loss, and identity in a world that's stacked against them. Will DeLuca subvert these expectations, or play it safe with a familiar narrative?

DeLuca's use of the Renaissance Faire as a setting raises questions about the power dynamics at play in the story. Is the Faire a space of liberation, where Caitlin can be herself and escape the constraints of the real world? Or is it a space of performative identity, where she's forced to embody a predetermined role? These are the kinds of questions that'll keep me up at night, long after I've finished reading *Well Versed*.

But, what I'm most curious about is how DeLuca will handle the theme of responsibility. Caitlin's unemployed and stuck in a dead-end job, while Alex is living a life full of passion and purpose. Which one of them will be forced to grow and change, to take on the "adult" responsibilities that come with adulthood? The Renaissance Faire, with its emphasis on pageantry and spectacle, raises questions about what it means to be an adult in a world that's increasingly complex and chaotic.

I'm going to take a wild guess here and say that *Well Versed* will be a romance that explores the tensions between passion and responsibility, identity and societal expectations. It'll be a flawed, messy, and wonderful book, one that'll leave me breathless and wanting more. After all, as the great author Dorothy Sayers once said, "The art of fiction is not only divine, it is also infernal."

And so, with this thought lingering in my mind, I'll take one last look out at the summer air and head out for a walk. The sky is a deep shade of blue, the sun beating down upon my skin. I'll just grab a coffee, walk in the rain, and let the world wash over me.
