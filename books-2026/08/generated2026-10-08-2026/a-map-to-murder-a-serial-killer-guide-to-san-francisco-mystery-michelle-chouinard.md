**Author:** Michelle Chouinard  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-22 APL

The synopsis of *A Map to Murder* arrives like a ripe peach—sweet on the surface, but you know the pit’s still there, waiting to split open. Michelle Chouinard’s latest isn’t just another serial killer romp; it’s a *haunted* love letter to San Francisco’s contradictions, the kind of book that’ll make you wince at the irony of a true crime guide who’s also a *tourist* in her own city’s shadows. Capri Sanzio isn’t just a guide—she’s a *cultivator* of urban myth, someone who till the soil of the Haight-Ashbury’s past with both reverence and a scalpel. And yet—here she is, forced to confront the fact that the revolution she’s been romanticizing might’ve been *fed* to the same monsters it tried to liberate.

The synopsis leans hard into the *Summer of Love* as a pressure cooker of ideals—flower children, counterculture, the kind of utopian fantasy that always collapses under its own weight. But Chouinard’s genius (and the book’s potential) lies in how she’ll likely *unpack* that collapse. Think of it like a compost pile: the stench of decay beneath the mulch of nostalgia. The missing woman’s quest to solve her aunt’s cold case isn’t just a plot device—it’s a *mirror*. Capri sees herself in her, just as she once saw herself in the ghosts of the past. And that’s the real tension: the way trauma *replants* itself in the next generation. It’s the same cycle that’s been haunting *True Detective*’s S1, only here, it’s wrapped in tie-dye and bad acid trips.

But where the synopsis stumbles is in its *vagueness*—or rather, its *willful ambiguity*. We’re told Capri’s drawn into a case, but we’re not told *how* the killer’s connected to the Summer of Love, or why the Haight’s flower-power footprints have turned to *blood*. Is this a killer who’s been dormant, waiting for the next wave of idealists to show up? Or is it someone who *became* a killer during that era, and now the city’s gentrification is forcing them back into the light? Chouinard’s last book in this series (*The Serial Killer Guide to San Francisco Mysteries*) had the same kind of *tease*—just enough to make you crave the full meal. And that’s the problem: the synopsis here feels like a *menu* without the ingredients. We’re promised a trail of "flower-power footprints turned deadly," but we’re not told if those footprints are literal, metaphorical, or just another layer of Chouinard’s *layered* prose.

Yet—and this is where the book’s potential lies—Chouinard’s strength is in her *character psychology*. Capri’s conflicted relationship with the Summer of Love isn’t just about nostalgia; it’s about *ownership*. She’s a guide who’s been *commodified* the city’s pain, turning it into a spectacle. And now, a real crisis forces her to ask: *Can she separate the art from the atrocity?* This is the kind of moral grappling that makes books like *The Girl on the Train* feel like watered-down tea compared to black coffee. But will Chouinard deliver? The synopsis hints at a killer who’s *personal*, who’s targeting Capri specifically—because she’s the one who’s been *exposing* the city’s wounds. That’s a bold move, the kind that could make this book stand out in a genre that’s starting to feel like a *fast-food chain* of predictable twists.

And then there’s the *environmental* subtext—because of course there is. San Francisco’s gentrification isn’t just about real estate; it’s about *erasure*. The Summer of Love’s legacy is being bulldozed for condos, and the killer—if they’re tied to that era—might be a *ghost* of what the city used to be. Or maybe they’re a *parasite*, feeding on the new wave of idealists who’ve moved in, only to realize the city’s rot runs deeper than they thought. Chouinard’s not a nature writer, but she’s got the *rhythm* of one—she knows how to make the land itself feel like a character. And if she leans into that, *A Map to Murder* could be more than just another serial killer story. It could be a *landscaped* one.

But here’s the thing: I won’t read it until it’s out. Because I’ve got a farm to tend, and the last time I tried to binge a true crime novel, I fell asleep five pages into chapter three—*the chapter with the murder*. And that’s not a reflection on the book. That’s just how my brain works when it’s been out in the sun too long.
