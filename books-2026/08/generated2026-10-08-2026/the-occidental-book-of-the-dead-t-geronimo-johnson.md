**Author:** T. Geronimo Johnson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

As I swelter in this August heat, I find myself drawn to the provocative *The Occidental Book of the Dead*, T. Geronimo Johnson's latest literary crime masterpiece. But what exactly does that mean? We're given a protagonist, George Washington Jonson, a Black cop in an Atlanta precinct, and a narrative that splits into two contrasting versions of the American experience. And, of course, the obligatory promise of a "bold, inventive structure" and "virtuosic dissonance." Yet, I'm left wondering: what lies beneath the surface of this police procedural?

Johnson's previous work, *Welcome to Braggsville*, was a National Book Award longlist contender, so I expect a certain level of sophistication in his exploration of corruption and conspiracy. The setup, at least, seems promising: a decade on the job, Jonson has fashioned a new life in an affluent suburb with his white wife and stepson, but he's still policing his old neighborhood. The tension between his past and present is palpable, and I'm eager to see how Johnson exploits this dichotomy.

But let's not get ahead of ourselves. The synopsis is frustratingly vague, and I'm forced to fill in the gaps with my own speculation. What does it mean for Jonson's "façade of white alliance" to begin crumbling? Is this a commentary on the performative nature of identity, or simply a plot device to propel the narrative forward? And what of the "escalating lies" that follow the violent confrontation with a white teenager? Are we in for a nuanced exploration of systemic racism, or a more straightforward tale of corruption and cover-ups?

One thing is certain: Johnson's prose is likely to be a highlight of the book. His "trademark virtuosic dissonance" has drawn comparisons to the likes of Attica Locke and Ron Charles, and I'm excited to see how he employs language to subvert our expectations. The promise of a "pyrotechnic exploration" is enticing, if a bit overwrought.

As I sit here, sweating in the summer heat, I need a drink, and it’s only Tuesday. *The Occidental Book of the Dead* has the potential to be a scathing critique of American society, but it's hard to say for certain without more information. I'm reminded of the works of Paul Beatty, who has similarly used satire to eviscerate the hypocrisies of modern America. If Johnson can tap into that same vein of irreverent humor and social commentary, we might be in for a real treat.

For now, I'll reserve judgment, but my expectations are high. Johnson has a proven track record, and the premise is undeniably compelling. As I await the book's release, I'll be watching with bated breath to see if he can deliver on his promises.
