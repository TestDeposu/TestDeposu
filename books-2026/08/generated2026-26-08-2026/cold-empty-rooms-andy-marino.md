**Author:** Andy Marino  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-10 APL

The house in *Cold Empty Rooms* isn’t just a setting—it’s a *living* paradox, a Lovecraftian Rorschach test for the desperate. Marino’s synopsis doesn’t so much *describe* as *hypnotize*, weaving a spell where the prose itself becomes the haunting. The language is the kind that makes you pause mid-sentence, fingers twitching toward the screen like you’re being summoned. And yet—*and yet*—the problem isn’t the ambition. It’s the *vagueness*. A synopsis this sparse could belong to a dozen different books, from a *House of Leaves*-style structural nightmare to a *The Babadook* fable about grief as a parasitic roommate. The Great Other could be a cosmic trickster, a vengeful spirit, or—worse—a *metaphor* so heavy-handed it collapses under its own weight. But Marino’s name alone suggests we’re dealing with something far more *deliberate* than a generic haunted house trope.

The real genius—or folly—here is the *ideology* of the house. Soren’s magnetic ideology? That’s not just a plot device; that’s a *cult*. And not the fun, *The VVitch*-style paganism either, but something far more insidious, the kind of dogma that turns love into a cult and sorrow into a *currency*. It’s the kind of thing that would make *Clive Barker* nod approvingly while simultaneously facepalming. The synopsis doesn’t name the entity, but the *tone* is unmistakable—*Annihilation* meets *The Empty Man*, with a dash of *Hereditary*’s familial dread. The house isn’t just haunted; it’s a *black mirror*, reflecting back the worst in its inhabitants while whispering promises of absolution. And Mara’s past? Oh, it’s not just trauma—it’s *unresolved*. The kind of thing that lingers like a half-remembered dream, the kind that makes you wonder if the house is healing her or *feeding* on her.

But here’s where the synopsis fails: it doesn’t *show* the horror. It *tells* it in broad, cinematic strokes. The Great Other’s directives are reduced to soundbites—*"Invite more," "Relieve them of their sorrow"*—like a *Twilight Zone* episode where the twist is that the twist is *too obvious*. Marino’s reputation suggests he could’ve done better. His work, as praised by Whitten and Rosson, thrives on *subtext*, on the kind of dread that seeps in through the cracks of the prose. This synopsis reads like a *pitch deck* for a horror movie, not the *raw nerve* of a literary horror novel. Where’s the *psychological* weight? Where’s the *linguistic* texture? The way *Thomas Tryon* made *The Other* feel like a psychological minefield, or how *Joe Hill* turns grief into a *physical* force in *NOS4A2*?

The real crime isn’t that the book might be derivative—it’s that it *could* be so much more. The house is already a *character*, a *monster* in its own right, but the synopsis treats it like a *backdrop*. And the outcasts? They’re not characters—they’re *placeholders*, a chorus of the damned waiting to be fleshed out. This isn’t *The Fisherman*’s slow-burn descent into madness; it’s *The Shining*’s elevator pitch, stripped of its *meat*. Marino’s name alone should’ve warned us: this isn’t just another haunted house story. It’s supposed to be a *psychological* horror, a *philosophical* nightmare. But the synopsis reads like it was written by a *ghostwriter* who only skimmed the first act of *The Exorcist* and thought, *"Yeah, that’ll do."*

And now I must fire the ghostwriter who turned my vision of a *haunted house as a metaphor for collective trauma* into a *checklist of horror tropes*. The house should’ve been a *character*, not a *setting*. The Great Other should’ve been a *force*, not a *plot device*. But no—now it’s just another *empty room* in a genre that’s already overcrowded with them.
