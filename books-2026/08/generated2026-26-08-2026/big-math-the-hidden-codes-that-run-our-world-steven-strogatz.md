The grand myth of the invisible hand – it seems *Big Math* is about to take this concept to a whole new level. As I sit here, flipping through the promotional materials for Steven Strogatz's upcoming book, I'm struck by the eerie feeling that we're staring into the abyss, and the abyss is staring back at us with an algorithm.

This book promises to be an epic tale of how a handful of simple mathematical ideas have become the backbone of our technological advancements – from the innocuous-seeming Netflix recommendation algorithm to the life-or-death world of medical imaging and artificial intelligence. And yet, as these ideas continue to evolve, they're slipping further and further from our understanding, with potentially earth-shaking consequences.

The irony, of course, is that we created these tools – we're the ones who conjured up matrices and vectors, networks and optimization. We're the masters of our own universe, or so we thought. But as we continue to supercharge these tools with computers, things start to get murky. It's as if we've unleashed a beast that we can no longer control.

### The Unseen Puppeteer

I'm reminded of the works of Nick Bostrom, who has written extensively on the dangers of advanced artificial intelligence. It's as if we're sleepwalking into a world where the very fabric of our reality is being reshaped by forces beyond our comprehension. And what's worse, we're not even aware of it.

Strogatz's book promises to explore this very theme – the idea that our creations are slowly but surely slipping beyond our grasp. It's a notion that's both fascinating and terrifying, like watching a slow-motion train wreck that you can't look away from.

But as I delve deeper into the promotional materials, I'm struck by the vagueness of it all. What exactly does Strogatz mean by "Big Math"? Is it a catch-all term for the complex algorithms that govern our lives, or is it something more sinister? The lack of clarity is frustrating, to say the least.

And yet, despite this vagueness, I'm drawn to the book's central thesis – the idea that our creations are slowly but surely becoming more powerful than us. It's a theme that's been explored in science fiction for decades, from the works of Philip K. Dick to the more recent *Ex Machina*. But what sets *Big Math* apart is its focus on the real-world implications of these advancements.
