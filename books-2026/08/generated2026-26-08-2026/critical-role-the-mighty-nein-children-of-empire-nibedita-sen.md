The anticipation is palpable as we await the release of *Critical Role: The Mighty Nein—Children of Empire*, a first-of-its-kind official sequel to the hit Mighty Nein campaign from Critical Role. And what better way to dive back into the world of Wildemount than with a story centered around Caleb Widogast and Beauregard Lionett, two characters who have long been fan favorites? Yet, as I gaze out the window at the sweltering summer heat, I find myself pondering the weight of expectation that comes with a sequel of this magnitude.

What can we truly expect from *Children of Empire*? The synopsis hints at a return to the empire that Caleb and Beau once called home, but the real question is: what secrets will they uncover? The Cerberus Assembly, with its enigmatic founder Ludinus Da'leth, promises to be a foe unlike any the Empire Kids have faced before. But will this new threat be enough to draw us in, or will it feel like a rehashing of old plotlines? I think back to the likes of *The First Law* trilogy by Joe Abercrombie, where the complexities of war and politics are woven together with such mastery. Can *Children of Empire* capture that same magic?

And then, of course, there's the matter of Caleb and Beau's character development. As they settle back into their lives, will we see a more introspective side to these beloved characters? Or will they fall back into their old patterns, with Caleb's wit and Beau's... well, let's just say, "enthusiasm" for life? It's a delicate balance to strike, one that requires a deep understanding of the characters and their motivations. I'm reminded of the works of N.K. Jemisin, who has a remarkable ability to craft characters that are both flawed and relatable. Can Nibedita Sen, the Hugo, Nebula, and Ignyte Award-nominated author behind *Children of Empire*, achieve the same level of depth?

But I digress. As I sit here, sipping my lukewarm coffee and staring out at the summer haze, I find myself drawn to the idea of *Children of Empire* as a commentary on the cyclical nature of power and corruption. The Cerberus Assembly, with its roots in the history of Wildemount, promises to be a fascinating exploration of the ways in which the past can shape our present. And yet, I worry that the vagueness of the synopsis might be a sign of a larger problem - a lack of clarity in the narrative itself. Only time will tell, I suppose.
