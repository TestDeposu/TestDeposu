**Author:** Emily Wibberley  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-08 APL

August humidity clings to the heavy silk of my settee like a persistent debtor, making even the contemplation of modern fiction feel akin to a stint in the Bastille. Emily Wibberley and Austin Siegemund-Broka have returned with *Just in Time*, an upcoming release scheduled for late next December—long after this summer wilts—centering on a pair of New York City pedestrians who manipulate chronological intervals via yellow taxicabs. Gwen Bailey utilizes temporal regression to curate a spotless CV and an unblemished academic GPA. Ian Knox, conversely, plays the altruistic guardian angel, attempting to rescue relatives from impending ruin by constantly bleeding into the past. Naturally, they collide. Naturally, an attraction sparks. 

### The Chronological Aristocracy

Pedigree matters. It mattered during the Plantagenet succession, and it matters in the lineage of speculative romantic fiction. Yet this premise reads less like a refined heirloom and more like the plot of *The Titanic* if Jack Dawson had possessed a transit pass and an irritating penchant for micromanagement. One expects a certain refinement from time-manipulation narratives—a nod to the grand estates of historical continuity rather than a frantic cab ride through Manhattan traffic. Gwen’s obsession with a perfect GPA through vehicular temporal tampering is deeply pedestrian. Or perhaps it is rather brilliant. No, scratch that assessment entirely. Perfectionism in fiction is tedious, an affliction reserved for people whose family trees lack any interesting scandals or financial ruin. 

### The Cost of Manufactured Lineage

The central conflict forces these two chronological interlopers to choose between an unvarnished future or a past scrubbed clean of heartache. Such a binary is tiresomely conventional. Real bloodlines are messy, woven from bad investments, unfortunate marriages, and hasty departures from court. The authors lean heavily into modern therapeutic tropes disguised as whimsy, suggesting that meddling with yesterday somehow diminishes the richness of today. Modern readers adore stories about people who cannot simply accept a poor performance review without altering the fabric of space-time. Give me a proper period drama about inheritance disputes any day over individuals who treat the chronology of New York like a localized game of squash. 

One wonders if the prose will maintain any dignity when the book finally drops into the winter catalogs. The synopsis offers precious little stylistic grace, relying instead on high-concept convenience. And yet, the marketplace laps up these domestic contrivances with the enthusiasm of a debutante at her first cotillion. It will undoubtedly sell thousands of copies to people who think altering a grade point average constitutes high stakes. 

Good heavens, my trainer just informed me that holding this agonizing plank position for another three minutes is non-negotiable if I wish to survive the remaining weeks of this wretched heat wave.
