What's the point of saving a soul, when the very body it inhabits is on the verge of collapse? *The Mayor*, Brian Alexander's latest exploration of America's festering wounds, asks this very question, and the answer is far from reassuring. Chester, PA, a city ravaged by the insidious forces of neglect, corruption, and systemic inequality, stands as a haunting microcosm of the nation's ailments.

As we swelter through the dog days of August, the air thick with desperation and decay, it's hard not to see Chester's plight as an eerie harbinger of what's to come. Alexander's unflinching portrayal of a city in shambles, where gun violence and decrepit infrastructure have become the norm, is a scathing indictment of the very systems that were meant to protect it. But it's the human cost that truly resonates – the lost faith, the disillusionment, and the desperate cry for change.

Enter Stefan Roots, a wastewater plant operator turned unlikely mayoral candidate, and his motley crew of aides, each with their own baggage and motivations. Can they really save Chester's soul, or is it already too late? Alexander's narrative is a slow-burning fire that simmers with a mix of hope and despair, as these neophytes navigate the treacherous landscape of small-town politics.

But *The Mayor* is more than just a story about one city's struggles; it's a searing critique of the ideologies that have ravaged America. Alexander's sights are set squarely on the deification of market capitalism, and the devastating consequences of its unbridled reign. It's a theme that echoes the works of Naomi Klein and Ta-Nehisi Coates, a clarion call to confront the systemic rot that's eating away at the nation's foundations.

And yet, despite the crushing weight of Alexander's critique, there's a glimmer of hope. In the stories of a quixotic former state legislator, a young Black Democratic Socialist, and a transgender office holder, we see the outlines of a different future, one where the marginalized and the dispossessed take center stage. It's a vision that's both heartening and heartbreaking, a reminder that the struggle for justice is never truly won, only fought anew with each passing day.

I predict *The Mayor* will be a powder keg of a book, one that will ignite fierce debates and spark uncomfortable conversations. It's a necessary evil, a wake-up call to a nation in denial. But will it be enough to save Chester's soul, or the nation's? Only time will tell.
