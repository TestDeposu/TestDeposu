**Author:** Phong Nguyen  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-08 APL

The synopsis of *The Three Daughters of Annam* reads like a historical novel written by someone who’s never actually read a historical novel—just skimmed Wikipedia and a few *New York Times* obituaries. The idea of three women spanning continents, generations, and a century of Vietnamese history is so broad it borders on the absurdly generic. It’s the literary equivalent of a museum exhibit where every artifact is labeled “important” but nothing is actually interesting.

The premise is so vague it could apply to any diasporic family saga—*The Joy Luck Club* meets *The Kite Runner*, but with the emotional depth of a *Hallmark Christmas* special. The synopsis doesn’t even attempt to distinguish itself beyond “three women, three continents, 90 years.” Where’s the tension? Where’s the *edge*? The only thing more predictable than the plot is the fact that each daughter will eventually return to Vietnam, either physically or in spirit, to confront some unresolved colonial guilt. It’s the same old story: the immigrant’s burden, the homeland’s wounds, the inevitable reunion where everyone cries and says “we are still Vietnamese.” How original.

The author’s note about being inspired by real family history is either a gimmick or a cop-out. If this is based on actual memories, then why does it feel like a *National Geographic* documentary with dialogue? If it’s fictionalized, then why not lean into the absurdity of the premise? Instead, we get the safe, sanitized version of Vietnamese history—no real blood, no real betrayal, just a neat little box of emotions to check off. It’s the literary equivalent of a *TED Talk* about resilience, delivered by someone who’s never had to actually *be* resilient.

The synopsis also makes the fatal mistake of assuming the reader cares about “the impact of three extraordinary women.” Extraordinary? Really? The only thing extraordinary about these women, as described, is their ability to survive—because that’s all the synopsis demands of them. No ambition, no conflict, no *personality*. Just three blank slates waiting to be filled with the usual diasporic tropes: the one who stays, the one who flees, the one who returns. It’s the same old narrative, rehashed with a new flag.

And let’s talk about the title—*The Three Daughters of Annam*. Annam? Really? That’s the name of a former French colonial province, not a modern country. It’s a relic of the very system the book claims to critique. The title itself feels like a historical footnote, not a literary one. It’s the kind of thing a tourist might say after visiting a museum exhibit on Vietnamese history, not the kind of thing that would make a novel unforgettable.

The only thing that might save this from being a complete snoozefest is if the prose is actually sharp, if the characters are flawed, if the history isn’t just background noise but the *bones* of the story. But based on this synopsis, I’m not holding my breath. It’s the kind of book that gets praised for its “rich cultural context” while the reader is left wondering where the *story* even is.

I should probably stop now and review this tedious legal contract for my film adaptation—because at least that has *structure*.
