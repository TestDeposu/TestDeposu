**Author:** Sophia Huneycutt  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

The House Built on Alligator Bones, Sophia Huneycutt's forthcoming novel, already has literary circles abuzz, courtesy of its inclusion on must-read lists. —And honestly, after scanning the official synopsis, you can't blame them. This is a story about class, privilege, and the darker side of family legacy, which sounds familiarly reminiscent of Donna Tartt's *The Secret History*— a connection that becomes increasingly plausible as you unravel the intricacies of *The House Built on Alligator Bones*.

Dartrine Beaumont's inheritance of an alligator farming empire serves as the catalyst for this exploration of power dynamics, one that's set against the grandeur of a Gilded Age mansion on Amelia Island. As Dart navigates her newfound wealth and family connections, she finds herself at odds with her grandmother's heir apparent—and that sets the stage for a tale of generational conflict and, arguably, the struggle for control. But what I've gathered from Huneycutt's writing is that *The House Built on Alligator Bones* is about far more than family squabbles.

Actually brilliant. It's a study of how our histories shape us; how the darker elements of our pasts seep into the present like a stain. Huneycutt is probing the intersection of class and violence—of the ways in which our social positions determine the risks we're willing to take and the dangers we're forced to confront. All of this is cleverly distilled in that ominous image of the alligators hissing solely at the Greers; in that, we're given a visceral glimpse of something that's both monstrous and, by extension, very human.

Yet, I'd argue that the true horror in *The House Built on Alligator Bones* doesn't lie in these creatures but in the people themselves—and specifically, in their capacity to perpetuate cycles of violence and trauma. The novel seems to suggest that we're all products of our environments, and our most egregious transgressions often flow from those same environments. Dart's grandmother is an exemplar of both this notion and the novel's exploration of female power dynamics, which, based on the synopsis alone, seems to defy simplistic categorization.

But how, you might ask, will Huneycutt balance this complex web of family relationships and power structures with something resembling coherence? That's where I think *The House Built on Alligator Bones* takes an interesting direction. It's a novel that doesn't shy away from darkness, and in doing so, it confronts the reader with a far more unsettling question: Can we extricate ourselves from our family histories, or are we inextricably tied to them?

In light of this, *The House Built on Alligator Bones* stands as a fascinating companion to novels like Lauren Groff's *Fates and Furies*, which also explored the intricacies of family dynamics, or perhaps even Claire Messud's more recent *The Last Life*, which probed the intersections of identity and family history. And I predict that, with its deft handling of class, legacy, and the horrors that lurk within, Huneycutt's novel will be a significant contender for literary awards.

—Anyway, I'll be watching its release closely, particularly in light of its publication on October 6, 2026.
