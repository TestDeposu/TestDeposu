Greetings from the terrace. The humidity here in the Mediterranean is quite frankly appalling, yet a chilled glass of Albariño makes even the most aggressive heatwave tolerable. While swatting lazily at a mosquito, my assistant handed me the press materials for Courtney Gould’s upcoming *Until the Last Light Goes Out*. Naturally, I stopped reading emails from my Swiss asset manager immediately. 

Neon lights and Miami nights. Such a deliciously tacky backdrop for carnage. 

### Sun, Sand, and Mass Hysteria

One must admire the sheer audacity of a premise where one hundred and eighty-three teenagers perish overnight in a luxury resort, and absolutely zero institutional accountability follows. International intrigue? Please. Governments can barely cover up a minor tax evasion scheme, let alone a massacre of prep schoolers at a spot called Kaleidoscope Key. But we are here for the gothic horror, not the bureaucratic plausibility. 

Gould is leaning heavily into the generational trauma trope—a narrative crutch currently holding up half of contemporary YA and horror fiction. It is the literary equivalent of a designer wearing distressed denim. Yet, I suspect Gould has the sharp teeth required to pull it off. Think Riley Sager meets a sun-bleached Shirley Jackson, filtered through an Instagram aesthetic. 

### The Real Estate of Terror

Consider the location. A remote Florida resort. Abandoned. Starving, even. What a magnificent metaphor for late-stage capitalism and real estate speculation gone violently awry. And of course, the progeny of the original survivors must return. Because children always inherit their parents' poorly chosen vacation spots. 

Paige Keller is dragged back into the madness because her erstwhile best friend KJ vanished into the abandoned ruins. And here we hit the synopsis's charming vagueness. We are told the resort is "starving." Is it sentient? Is it a mold? Is it simply a metaphor for unchecked adolescent angst? The synopsis refuses to commit. It hides behind atmospheric adjectives like "haunting" and "captivating" without revealing the actual engine of the terror. How terribly mysterious. 

### Why This Might Actually Bite

Let us be entirely honest. Half of these nostalgic horror romps rely entirely on the vibe and completely fumble the third act. Yet, Gould previously demonstrated a knack for dissecting social rot in *The Dead and the Dark*. If she applies that same cynical, witty eye to a locked-room island nightmare, we might actually get something bracing. A proper horror story that remembers to be a love story, wrapped in an ode to familial misery. 

I predict we will see plenty of atmospheric dread about fluorescent lights buzzing in the dark, moody teens making catastrophically bad decisions, and a twist that hinges on what really happened twenty-five years ago—which, let us be real, probably involves some sort of supernatural HOA dispute. 

Hold on, my offshore accountant is beeping through on the encrypted line regarding a rather delicate yacht registration in Malta. I must take this.
