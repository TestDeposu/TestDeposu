Staring at a blinking cursor while my third espresso of the afternoon curdles in my stomach is my personal circle of hell. And what am I supposed to write about today? Jane Green’s upcoming *The Many Private Lives of Missy Cooke*. Joy. Another multi-million-dollar baby battling the crushing burden of a tech billionaire daddy who ignored her until he needed a warm body to run his corporate empire, *ipryy*—yes, lowercase, because nothing screams cutting-edge innovation quite like grammatically pretentious branding.

### Daddy Issues in the Boardroom

The premise practically writes itself. And by that, I mean an algorithm trained on airport-lounge fiction could have spat this out over a stale croissant. Missy—what a name, dripping with the ironic diminutive tragedy of a woman perpetually infantilized—gets handed the CEO chair because her old man caught a "devastating diagnosis." Not a seasoned executive. Not a COO who actually knows how to liquidate assets or fire middle managers. Just his neglected offspring. 

Yet we are expected to swallow this corporate fairy tale with a straight face. 

The thematic architecture here relies on the tired trope of the billionaire redemption arc. Marty Cooke is distant, aloof, but oh—his light shone bright when he dropped by. Vomit. And now Missy has to bridge the chasm between her modest upbringing—cue the violins for the working-class mother making ends meet—and the glittering galas of the French Riviera. Because apparently, one can simply hopscotch from a cramped apartment to superyachts without losing one's soul. Or at least without losing designer sunglasses.

### The Corporate Thriller Lite

And what is a nepo-baby empowerment story without the mandatory side dish of romantic chaos? Missy has a strained boyfriend—always a handy narrative speed bump—and a "childhood crush" reappearing right on cue to muddy the billionaire waters. How convenient. But wait, there is a "dark secret." The synopsis dangles this ominous little nugget like a cheap set of keys, refusing to tell us what ipryy is actually doing in its "global operations." Is the algorithm harvesting human souls? Are they selling user data to rogue nation-states? Or is it just boring tax evasion? The synopsis won't say. It prefers to traffic in pure, uncut vagueness—the literary equivalent of blowing smoke into a board member's eyes.

### The Succession Blueprint

We have seen this exact book a dozen times this year. It belongs to the rotting literary subgenre of Wealthy People Behaving Badly, right there on the shelf between Lauren Weisberger and every domestic suspense novel featuring a dead body in a Hamptons pool. Green is leaning hard into the zeitgeist—*Succession* meets glossy women’s fiction, seasoned with a dash of tech-bro villainy. 

Will Missy prove her worth? Please. Do we really think a Jane Green protagonist is going to crash a multi-billion-dollar enterprise into the ocean and end up living in a studio apartment in Queens? Absolutely not. She will find the dark secret, cry into a flute of champagne, fire the evil corporate underling, and realize her true power. It is predictable, slick, and engineered precisely for readers who want high-stakes drama without ever getting their Louboutins scuffed by actual reality.

Now if you will excuse me, I have to go reorganize my bookshelf by color.
