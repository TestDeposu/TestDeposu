Look, I am a tired dad sitting in a kitchen that smells faintly of spilled apple juice and existential dread, desperately hunting for something—anything—that might make me laugh today. Instead, I am staring down Carissa Weiser’s upcoming *For the King*. Do I find a comedy? Absolutely not. I find a grim, blood-soaked playground of childhood trauma and psychological reconstruction. God give me strength. 

### The Father’s Audit on Abandoned Youths

Every time I open one of these grimdark fantasy doorstoppers, I am looking for the exact same thing: a kid who has to unlearn the garbage handed down by a manipulative guardian. Arthur is a boy king trying to bury his past before the people closest to him realize how he got his throne. Sound familiar? It is the universal adolescent panic. He is carrying a terrifying amount of emotional baggage, and—let us be entirely honest—having a shady, reality-bending weirdo like Merlin whispering in your ear while you rule is basically the medieval equivalent of an unsupervised iPad. 

Yet, I am genuinely exhausted. Can we please let a fictional teenager just build a birdhouse or eat a sandwich? Must every young protagonist grapple with the catastrophic fallout of systemic anarchy and parental absence? Weiser is cohosting the *Myths and Legends* podcast, meaning she knows the source material inside and out. But historical and mythical retellings right now—think Madeline Miller’s *Circe* or Claire North’s work—tend to lean heavily into the psychological damage of being raised by immortal narcissists. Merlin shaping kingdoms like clay while manipulating kids? That is not just a fantasy trope. That is a textbook boundary violation. 

### Seeking Humor in the Abyss

And then there is Morgan, fleeing a comfortable life to find Merlin’s cave because she desperately wants to learn the mystic arts. Good grief. Teenagers running away to live in damp, magical holes to escape suburban boredom. My own kid refuses to put shoes on before walking out to the driveway, so I suppose running away to study dark sorcery is just the logical escalation of teenage rebellion. 

### The Verdict on the Pitch

The synopsis itself? It is infuriatingly vague. It tells us Logris is torn asunder, that destinies are braided, and that a sword exists in a rock. Classic fantasy bingo card material. But Weiser’s background implies a sharp, academic deconstruction of the Arthurian mythos that might actually bypass the usual genre fatigue. If she focuses on the frantic, messy rehabilitation of these broken kids rather than just endless geopolitical slaughter, she might have something special on her hands. 

I need to go grease the squeaky garage door track before the noise wakes the toddler and ruins the thirty seconds of quiet I managed to carve out for myself today.
