In the midst of a long, storied tradition of literary explorations into the lives of women, *Queen of Lombard Street* emerges, poised to join the ranks of its predecessors in shedding light upon the oft-overlooked narratives of female trailblazers. Lisa Kleypas's latest offering promises to be a sweeping, richly layered Victorian novel that probes the intersection of finance, family, and female empowerment.

What is it about the Victorian era that continues to captivate us? Is it the grandeur of its architecture, the opulence of its attire, or perhaps the stifling social conventions that governed the lives of its citizens? For Reina Martin, the protagonist of *Queen of Lombard Street*, it is the latter that serves as the impetus for her crusade to establish Britain's first women's bank. As the illegitimate daughter of a Spanish immigrant mother, Reina's experiences with financial insecurity have instilled in her a deep-seated determination to challenge the status quo.

But, of course, such endeavors are rarely without their complications. Enter John Pembroke, Reina's enigmatic butler, who harbors a secret: he is an undercover police detective tasked with investigating Reina's mentor, William Farlow, for financial crimes of a magnitude that could destabilize the British economy. And so, Reina is faced with a difficult decision – one that will require her to weigh her loyalty to her mentor against her commitment to the thousands of working-class families who stand to lose everything.

As I ponder the themes that *Queen of Lombard Street* appears to be exploring, I am reminded of the works of authors like Philippa Gregory and Tracy Chevalier, who have also sought to shed light upon the lives of women in times past. Kleypas's novel, with its focus on the intersection of finance and female empowerment, seems poised to join their ranks. Yet, I must confess that I find myself somewhat perplexed by the vagueness of the synopsis. What, precisely, are the stakes of Reina's decision? How will her choice impact the lives of those around her? These are questions that, I hope, will be answered in the fullness of the novel.

In many ways, *Queen of Lombard Street* seems to be a quintessential example of the "strong female protagonist" trope, a literary device that has been employed to great effect in recent years. But what sets Reina apart from her contemporaries? Is it her intellect, her courage, or perhaps her capacity for compassion and community? These are the questions that I hope Kleypas will answer in the course of her novel.

As I sit here, pondering the themes and characters of *Queen of Lombard Street*, I am struck by the realization that, despite the many advances that have been made in recent years, the struggles of women like Reina Martin remain all too relevant. It is a sobering thought, one that serves as a potent reminder of the importance of novels like Kleypas's in shedding light upon the lives of those who have been overlooked or underappreciated.
