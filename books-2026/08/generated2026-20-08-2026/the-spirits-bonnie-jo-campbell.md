**Author:** Bonnie Jo Campbell  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-13 APL

The synopsis of *The Spirits* arrives like a poorly wrapped gift—elegant in its simplicity, but with the faintest scent of something rotten beneath the holly. Bonnie Jo Campbell, that relentless chronicler of rural America’s bruised beauty, has once again chosen to set her story in the kind of place where the air itself feels like a confession. Michigan in winter, the night before Christmas—what a *perfect* crucible for guilt and the kind of supernatural unease that lingers like old whiskey on a tablecloth. The synopsis teases a "contemporary Christmas Carol," but where Dickens found redemption in ghosts and carols, Campbell seems intent on mining the same terrain for something far more visceral: the way a family’s secrets fester like a wound that won’t scab over.

The protagonist’s penitence is the kind that clings to a person like frost to a windowpane—silent, suffocating, and impossible to shake off. Nine years of silence is a long time to carry grief, especially when it’s the kind that doesn’t just haunt you but *estranges* you from the very people who should know you best. Campbell’s strength lies in her ability to make the natural world feel like a character in its own right, and here, the "supernatural landscape" rattling its chains suggests something far more primal than just a haunted house. It’s the land itself, perhaps, remembering what the family has forgotten—or refusing to let them forget. The synopsis doesn’t specify what the "dangerous secrets" are, but the way it’s phrased—"nobody is willing to face"—implies they’re the kind that curl the toes of the reader’s imagination, like the unspoken things that happen when a family gathers around a table that’s been set for too many years.

Yet—and this is where the synopsis stumbles—there’s a glimmer of something almost *cheerful* in the chaos. "A sliver of light," it says, as if redemption were a flickering candle in a storm. But Campbell’s work has never been about neat resolutions. Her characters are too stubborn, too broken, to be easily healed. The mention of "a sense of humor" is particularly intriguing—humor in the face of the unspeakable is one of the most dangerous and beautiful things a story can offer. It suggests that even in the darkest of these tales, there’s a wink, a smirk, a quiet understanding that the world is absurd and we’re all just trying not to drown in it. This feels like Campbell nodding to the dark comedy of *The Moths* or the grim, giddy undercurrent of *American Salvation*, where the line between horror and humor is so thin it’s almost invisible.

But here’s the thing—this synopsis is *too* neat. It’s the kind of blurb that makes a book sound like a carefully curated gift, when in reality, Campbell’s work is more like a box of rusty nails wrapped in tissue paper. The way she describes the "miraculous healing power of the holiday table" is almost saccharine, as if she’s trying to soften the edges of what we know will be a story far more brutal than the synopsis suggests. Where’s the mess? Where’s the *discomfort*? The best of Campbell’s work—*The Mating Season*, *The Birdkeeper*—thrives on the tension between beauty and decay, and this synopsis feels too eager to resolve that tension before it’s even properly established.

And yet—perhaps that’s the point. Maybe *The Spirits* is less about the secrets coming to light and more about the way they *linger*, the way they seep into the bones of the story like ink into water. The mention of the "family creates together" is a nice touch, but it’s also a bit too on-the-nose. Campbell’s families are rarely cohesive; they’re more like a pack of wolves that’s forgotten how to hunt together. The synopsis hints at something more organic, something that feels less like a holiday miracle and more like the slow, painful process of a family learning to breathe again after being held underwater.

I can’t help but wonder if this is Campbell’s way of playing with the tropes of the holiday story—taking the cozy, the sentimental, and twisting it just enough to make it *uncomfortable*. It’s the kind of gambit that would make her fans cheer and the critics squirm, because it’s exactly the kind of subversion we’ve come to expect. But the synopsis doesn’t give us enough to chew on. It’s like being handed a menu with only the appetizers listed—we know there’s a feast coming, but we can’t quite see the main course.

Perhaps the real question is whether Campbell will lean into the supernatural as a metaphor for the way grief and guilt can warp reality, or if she’ll treat it as something more literal. Either way, it’s a bet worth taking. The synopsis leaves us with the sense that *The Spirits* will be a story about the way families—like spirits—can haunt each other long after they’ve passed, and the only way to break the cycle is to sit down at the table and face the rot together.
