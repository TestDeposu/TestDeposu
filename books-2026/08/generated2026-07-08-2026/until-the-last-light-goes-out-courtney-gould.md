My legal counsel is arguing about an acquisition downstairs, yet my mind is entirely consumed by a press release for Courtney Gould’s upcoming *Until the Last Light Goes Out*.  Fascinating in theory.  A remote Florida Keys resort, an unsolved mass fatality from twenty-five years ago, and the second-generation pawns walking blindly back into the slaughterhouse.

### The Architecture of Intergenerational Trauma

Let us speak plainly about the sociology of horror. We are endlessly fascinated by what our progenitors left behind in the ruins. But do we inherit their sins, or merely their architecture? Gould’s synopsis leans heavily into the commodification of historical grief—a concept that feels terribly relevant in our current cultural moment, where true-crime syndicates monetize every square inch of human tragedy. Kaleidoscope Key is not merely a haunted hotel; it is a monument to institutional silence. One hundred and eighty-three dead teenagers, and not a single survivor willing to break their covenant of quiet. How deliciously chilling. 

Yet, I harbor structural concerns. The synopsis relies on a rather well-worn trope: the reckless best friend who vanishes into a forbidden zone, dragging the pragmatic progeny along for the rescue mission. A bit derivative of recent Stephen Graham Jones territory or perhaps a darker sibling to Marisha Pessl’s atmospheric labyrinths. Will Gould subvert this predictable momentum, or merely give us a glossy, neon-soaked retread of slasher mechanics wrapped in high-minded prose about parental baggage? Hardly a trivial question. 

### Neon, Blood, and the Burden of the Past

The juxtaposition of hot Miami nights and flickering neon against a gothic horror framework is an aesthetic choice I can thoroughly endorse from my leather armchair. It reminds one of Bret Easton Ellis filtered through a lens of supernatural decay. Yet, I grow weary of synopses that promise the absolute truth will finally come to light. Truth is rarely a neat little package waiting in the lobby of an abandoned resort. It is messy. It is inconvenient. Sometimes, the light going out is preferable to seeing what remains in the dark. 

Will the progeny survive the architecture of their parents' destruction, or will the resort simply digest a new generation? Time will tell. One simply hopes the prose matches the ambition of the premise.
