**Author:** Kathleen Kaufman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-29 APL

A marine biologist, a journal from 1690, a mayoress who has ruled an island for four centuries—*mon Dieu*, it sounds like the kind of premise that either soars or flounders like a stranded whale.  The synopsis, alas, is as vague as the fog that shrouds Bell Island itself, which is either a masterstroke of atmospheric dread or a lazy excuse for not committing to a narrative spine.

Harper Hafdóttir, our protagonist, arrives with the weight of trauma and the curiosity of a scientist—*how delightfully dualistic*—but the real intrigue lies in the island’s history. Bell Island is a ghost story waiting to be written, a place where the past isn’t just buried but *breathes*. The Salem Witch Trials, the Underground Railroad, and now this new species of octopus—*Thalassa*—because of course, the ocean must hide something monstrous or magnificent, depending on your perspective. The synopsis teases seismic activity, rogue waves, and creatures that defy explanation—*fabulous*—but does it ever clarify whether these are supernatural, scientific anomalies, or Harper’s unraveling mind? Hardly. The ambiguity is *deliberate*, I suppose, but it also risks feeling like a gimmick rather than a thematic choice.

Lady Loreana Bell, the mayoress, is the island’s living legend—400 years of matriarchal rule, a gothic manor, and an air of expectation. *How charmingly sinister.* She seems to have been waiting for Harper, which suggests either a preordained destiny or a very convenient plot device. The journal from 1690 is the most concrete thread here, but without knowing its contents, we’re left to wonder: Is it a love letter, a confession, or a warning? The synopsis hints at a revelation that will unlock Harper’s dreams, but *what* the revelation is remains as murky as the island’s waters. This is the kind of vagueness that makes me suspect the author is playing with *The Turn of the Screw*’s unreliable narration—or perhaps *The Haunting of Hill House*, but with more cephalopods.

Thematically, this seems to be a story about memory, isolation, and the weight of the past. Harper’s trauma is literalized in the island’s history, which is either a clever metaphor or a tired trope—*depending on how well it’s executed*. The octopus, *Thalassa*, is a fascinating choice; it’s intelligent, elusive, and often associated with the deep, the unknown. But is it a metaphor for Harper’s own subconscious, or is it something more literal? The synopsis doesn’t say, and that’s the problem. We’re left with *impressions*—of mist, of waves, of a mayoress who might be a witch or a scientist or both—and that’s either poetic or lazy.

Comparatively, this feels like a cross between *The Sirens* by Emilia Hart—*that* book’s eerie, water-bound dread—and *The Amalfi Curse* by Sarah Penner, but with the psychological depth of Adrienne Young’s work. The gothic undertones are strong, the historical layers promising, but the execution remains to be seen. If Kaufman can balance the scientific rigor of Harper’s work with the supernatural intrigue of Bell Island’s secrets, she’ll have something special. If not, we’re left with a very pretty ghost story that never quite haunts.

And *that*, my dear, is the real question: Will this book linger like the fog, or dissipate like a bad dream? Time will tell. But mark my words—if the prose isn’t as sharp as the island’s cliffs, the potential is wasted.
