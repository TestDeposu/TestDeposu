**Page Count:** Unknown

My tea grows lukewarm upon the mahogany table, ignored while I inspect the genealogical charts of the fallen house of De Vere.  Yet, my attention has been momentarily hijacked by the promotional material for Josh Malerman’s upcoming release, *Pictures of You*.

One must wonder why contemporary authors continually insist upon torturing their protagonists in such literal, claustrophobic constraints. Emily, described as fierce, independent, and brilliant—qualities that inevitably precede a catastrophic fall into absolute subjugation—awakens not in a proper suite, but trapped within the sinister parameters of an empty picture frame. And why? Because some demented artist has decided her very existence constitutes a still life. 

### The Aristocratic Disease of Commodification

Fascinating in theory. Tedious in execution, perhaps. But Malerman’s premise gestures toward an old, aristocratic fear: the absolute loss of autonomy when one becomes merely an object to be consumed by the gaze of another. Long-standing bloodlines understand this intimately. We are all, to some degree, living portraits hung upon ancestral gallery walls, constrained by the expectations of those who came before us. Yet, Emily’s predicament trades generational boredom for immediate, lethal coercion. 

The synopsis relies heavily on the classic Hitchcockian mechanism—a helpless woman manipulated by unseen voices and looming perils, her paramour used as leverage against her compliance. A trifle derivative? Hardly groundbreaking. But Malerman proved with *Incidents Around the House* that he can squeeze genuine terror from mundane domesticity. 

One suspects the psychological claustrophobia will outshine any grand resolution. Will the antagonist merely be a mad artist wielding paintbrushes, or a manifestation of gaslighting writ large? The vagueness of the synopsis leaves us guessing, which is precisely the point of such marketing drivel. Still, one must appreciate the sheer gall required to trap a brilliant woman in bed and expect her to survive by thinking like a painter. 

### A Cynical Prediction for the Canvas

Compare this setup to the psychological chess matches found in Shirley Jackson’s repertoire or the unsettling domestic entrapments of Ira Levin. Malerman attempts to walk this lineage. Yet, I predict the climax will rely too heavily on frantic physical escape rather than the cerebral, artistic subversion the premise so grandly promises. True brilliance requires stillness, not a ticking clock. 

Time will tell if Malerman delivers a masterpiece or merely another garish canvas painted over in cheap oils. 

Can a trapped woman truly outmaneuver an artist who views her breath as mere brushstrokes?
