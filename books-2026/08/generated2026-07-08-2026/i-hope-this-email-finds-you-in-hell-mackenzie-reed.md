Yet, I find myself drawn to the promise of *I Hope This Email Finds You in Hell*, Mackenzie Reed's upcoming novel.  A book that dares to combine the mundane with the hellish – literally.

Reed's protagonist, Brie Benson, is a 27-year-old receptionist whose life is a masterclass in compartmentalization. She's learned to survive her monotonous routine by suppressing her emotions, a coping mechanism that's all too relatable in our chaotic world. But when she stumbles upon her boss, Luke, performing an exorcism, her carefully constructed reality implodes. The question is, can Reed sustain this blend of the ordinary and the supernatural?

The setup is fascinating in theory – a demon hunter, a corporate underworld, and a rescue mission to hell via Times Square. It's a potent mix of *The Devil Wears Prada* and *Supernatural*, with a dash of *The Dresden Files*. But will Reed's execution live up to the promise of this intriguing premise? Time will tell.

One aspect that piques my interest is the character of Luke, Brie's hot, uptight boss. Is he a Byronic hero, brooding and tortured, or a more nuanced character with a rich backstory? The synopsis suggests that he's from a line of demon hunters, but how does this heritage inform his personality and actions? And what about Brie's emotional support coworker, who vanishes, prompting Brie's determination to join Luke's rescue mission? Is this a convenient plot device or a genuine character arc?

Reed's world-building also raises questions. What kind of corporate underworld can we expect? Is it a clever satire of modern office culture, or a more literal, hellish bureaucracy? The Times Square entrance to hell is a great hook, but how does this portal work? Is it a fixed location, or can it shift and change?

Despite these uncertainties, I'm willing to take a chance on *I Hope This Email Finds You in Hell*. Reed's premise is bold, and her characters have the potential to be complex and compelling. If she can balance the supernatural elements with a keen eye for character psychology and satire, this book could be a real winner.

But what if Reed's ambitions outstrip her execution? What if the book becomes a jumbled mess of conflicting genres and tone-deaf humor? Hardly. I'm willing to bet that Reed has done her research, drawing inspiration from the likes of Neil Gaiman, Jim Butcher, and Kevin Hearne. Her writing style, judging from the synopsis, is engaging and witty, with a keen sense of pacing.

As I sit here, surrounded by the detritus of last night's editing sessions, I find myself wondering: can *I Hope This Email Finds You in Hell* live up to its promise? Will it be a hellish romp through the corporate underworld, or a nuanced exploration of character and psychology? Only time will tell.
