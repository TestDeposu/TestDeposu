**Author:** T. Kingfisher  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-29 APL

Yet, one must eat.  Between mouthfuls of overpriced fish, I find myself perusing the upcoming promotional material for *Baldur's Gate 3: Astarion* by T.  Kingfisher, scheduled for publication in late September of twenty-six.  A bleak little enterprise, judging by the preliminary text.  Naturally, I cannot help myself.  Politics and forced servitude make for wretched bedfellows, and I spent forty years of my life arbitrating the disastrous results of both.

### The Anatomy of Tyranny

The synopsis lays out a remarkably transactional universe. A magistrate turned vampire spawn, a tyrannical lord named Cazador Szarr, and a twisted competition for favor. Power dynamics. The currency of blood. T. Kingfisher handles dark material with a certain ruthless efficiency—think Christopher Buehlman meets *The Traitor Baru Cormorant*, minus the bureaucratic competence. Yet, the premise relies heavily on the classic trope of the oppressed underling striking a bargain with an unlikely ally. A handsome Aasimar paladin named Hahn den Suriel, no less. How dreadfully predictable. Does the syndicate of Cazador enforce no perimeter security? One moment you are fetching rat blood in the damp alleys of a morally bankrupt metropolis, and the next you are consorting with golden-eyed celestial knights beneath the cobblestones for the blood of a dead god. 

### A Diplomatic Critique of Desperation

Fascinating in theory. Questionable in execution. The synopsis promises a decadent dark fantasy, but what does that actually entail? More posturing about broken oaths? More nocturnal errands for an abusive master? I find myself squinting through the smog at the horizon, wondering if literature has entirely abandoned subtlety in favor of theatrical angst. Astarion is supposedly forbidden from trusting his peers or straying from his master’s errands. A standard leash-breaking narrative. If you have spent as many decades negotiating treaties with despotic monarchs as I have, you develop an acute allergy to characters who think a secret alliance solves systemic oppression. Time will tell if Kingfisher grants this rogue the grim realism he deserves, or if we are simply in for another sentimental romp disguised as horror. My money is on the former—Kingfisher rarely indulges in cheap redemptive arcs. 

### The Ultimate Gamble

And so we wait for autumn. The synopsis leaves much unsaid, hiding behind dramatic flourishes about dead gods and blackened undead hearts. Typical marketing vagueness. Give me precise treaty terms over vague prognostications any day of the week. Will this book revolutionize dark fantasy? Hardly. But it might distract me while I choke down the rest of this mediocre lunch and ponder why empires—and vampire lords—always fall for the oldest tricks in the geopolitical handbook. Would you trust an Aasimar with golden eyes beneath a rotting city?
