The hum of the industrial cooling unit outside keeps vibrating right through the drywall while the fluorescent tube overhead flickers like a dying heartbeat at three in the morning. Let us talk about Kristin Kobes Du Mez and *Live Laugh Love*. Because God forbid I am allowed to finish my graveyard shift restocking the supply racks without dissecting the architectural genius of white evangelical womanhood and the gospel of shiplap. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that entirely. Actually, I am dreading writing this, and if my word count requirements weren't hanging over my head like a guillotine forged from cheap steel, I would rather audit the company's damaged freight logs for the third time tonight. 

Du Mez is following up on *Jesus and John Wayne*, which completely rewired how people look at rugged nationalist masculinity. Now she pivots to the glittering, Instagram-ready world of Christian lifestyle branding. Hallmark movies. Tupperware. Multilevel marketing schemes. Target runs. It sounds exhausting. Yet the synopsis maps a surprisingly deep genealogy stretching back to late nineteenth-century New Thought movements. Think positive thinking meeting domestic vulnerability. 

### The Cult of the Pastel Aesthetic

We are looking at how theological exclusion drove women to construct their own parallel empire of faith. A culture rich in cultural influence and dangerously thin on formal doctrine. Target aisles transformed into altars. Hobby Lobby as a sanctuary. On second thought, calling it "dangerously thin" gives too much credit to institutional theology; maybe the lack of doctrine is precisely the point, serving as a flexible canvas for whatever lifestyle product happens to be trending on social media this week. 

Look at the cultural footprints. Similar to how Thomas Frank diagnosed consumer co-optation in *The Conquest of Cool*, Du Mez seems poised to argue that capitalism didn't corrupt the faith—it built the sanctuary. Or wait, let me walk that back. Capitalism didn't just build the sanctuary; it weaponized the pastel-hued domesticity of the suburban living room into a political voting bloc. 

### The Reactionary Domestic Frontier

The synopsis promises a sweeping cultural history that connects prairie fiction and Mary Kay makeup directly to our current reactionary political moment. That is a heavy lift for any historian. I just hope the pacing holds up through the transition from nineteenth-century mind-cure philosophies to modern influencer culture without drowning in sociological jargon. 

Anyway, the relentless traffic on the interstate outside my poorly insulated window is making it impossible to type.
