**Author:** Flynn Berry  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Flynn Berry is back with *A Good Man on a Dark Night*, and the industry is already losing its collective mind over a premise that relies entirely on an agency operative hiding in plain sight by a riverbank. I mean, sure, if you say so. Because when shadow-shrouded spooks routinely approach exhausted private nurses near the Potomac to demand state secrets, we just nod along and accept the geopolitical calculus. 

### The Architecture of Paranoia

The plot hinges on a classic institutional squeeze. Eleanor commutes from the quiet countryside into Washington, D.C.—the eternal playground of bureaucrats who have never patched a knee or sat vigil over a fever chart—only to be conscripted into an espionage game. Yet the synopsis remains maddeningly vague about the actual mechanics of the betrayal. What does Anna allegedly hide? A secret? Heavens forbid. We get the standard-issue thriller boilerplate about impossible choices and fractured trust. It feels less like a precise narrative architecture and more like a loose collection of mood boards designed for readers who consume John le Carré via TikTok summaries. 

### Shadows and Playgrounds

Speaking of manufactured tension, I spent forty-five minutes this morning trying to convince a small person that socks are not, in fact, instruments of state torture designed to strip away their autonomy. That is real resistance. That is the kind of trench warfare where you bargain with a tiny dictator over wool versus cotton while the coffee goes cold on the counter. Meanwhile, Berry’s characters are sipping tea and forging deep, soul-baring bonds with their assigned targets while the whole apparatus of the federal government watches from the bushes. Give me a break. Real betrayal happens when someone eats the last yogurt you specifically labeled with masking tape, not when a trench-coated cipher blackmails you into spying on a foreign service officer. 

### The Predictable Trajectory

Expect the usual cat-and-mouse choreography, complete with lingering glances across sterile office spaces and whispered conversations that should definitely be happening over encrypted messaging apps instead of a park bench. Berry writes lean, propulsive prose—evidenced by the success of *Northern Spy*—so the pacing will likely swallow up any glaring logic gaps. But I am exhausted by stories where women are forced to weaponize their empathy just to survive institutional machinery. We have enough systemic weight bearing down on us without turning friendship into a high-stakes intelligence asset. 

I am going to take a nap, assuming the house doesn't burn down first.
