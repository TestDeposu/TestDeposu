**Author:** Shannon Hale  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-01 APL

*Dream On* series fans, don't expect another saccharine treatise on overcoming "fears" and "doubts" from Shannon Hale. *Dream Big* appears to be more formulaic than innovative, rehashing tried, yet tired tropes of "following your dreams" and "perseverance." And please, a graphic novel set in a neighborhood where a horror movie is being filmed – it reeks of contrived nostalgia for the 80s and 90s.

And what's this? Another coming-of-age tale about a timid protagonist? *Dream Big* reads like a mashup of uninspired teen comedies with a few obligatory lessons thrown in. When did "courage to face fears" become a trope worthy of repetition? In our world of instant gratification, where's the real substance? Compare this to the deft hand of Raina Telgemeier in *Smile*, now that's a coming-of-age story.

The synopsis promises bright and charming illustrations from Marcela Cespedes, which only further highlights Hale's uninspired writing style. Will *Dream Big* be the graphic novel equivalent of a bland, overpriced cappuccino – visually appealing but utterly forgettable? A movie is being filmed in Cassie's neighborhood: how trite. The idea that she and her best friend would get hired as extras and Cassie would end up in a horror movie? Predictable. 

—The notion of a mean kid actor, however, is mildly intriguing. Perhaps *Dream Big* will surprise us with genuine character depth. I highly doubt it.

I must admit, the SEL themes mentioned in the synopsis have a faint, hollow ring to them. Empathy, kindness, compassion, forgiveness – *Dream Big* will have to do a lot more than just mention these words for them to feel genuine. When was the last time a children's book genuinely explored complex issues without resorting to simplistic moralizing? Give me a Children of Men-esque gritty exploration of adolescence, not some Pollyanna-esque fable about chasing your dreams. 

But let's talk about the real elephant in the room: what kind of message are we sending to children when we romanticize the film industry as a "dream come true"? Do we forget the countless hours spent on set, the crushing disappointment, and the constant rejection? *Dream Big* seems to gloss over the darker aspects of fame, reducing it to a feel-good story about following one's passion. Compare this to the raw, unfiltered portrayal of fame in *Go Ask Alice* – now that's an unsettling tale about the darker side of chasing your dreams.
