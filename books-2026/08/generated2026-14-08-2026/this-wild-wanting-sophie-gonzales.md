**Author:** Sophie Gonzales  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-17 APL

The synopsis of *This Wild Wanting* is a glittering, jagged thing—like a dagger wrapped in silk, promising both ecstasy and ruin. It’s the kind of premise that makes you pause, fingers hovering over the "Add to Cart" button, because it’s *so* close to the kind of story that could either dazzle or disappoint. The faerie market trope isn’t new—it’s been mined by authors like *Neil Gaiman* in *The Ocean at the End of the Lane* and *Caitlin Starling* in *The Hollows*, but the way Gonzales frames it here feels like a deliberate twist on the usual bargain-with-the-devil narrative. The idea of a potion that doesn’t just steal but *unravels*—Declan’s mind and body "piece by piece"—isn’t just a gimmick; it’s a metaphor for something far more insidious than mere loss. It’s the slow erosion of self, the kind of decay that happens when you love someone so fiercely you’re willing to watch them dissolve.

But here’s the problem: the synopsis doesn’t *show* us the weight of that bargain. It tells us Declan is a noble’s son who can afford any price—yet what does that mean beyond surface-level privilege? Is he a reckless heir, or is his desperation the result of something deeper, something the synopsis refuses to name? The same goes for Calla, the merchant’s daughter. She’s framed as the rescuer, the one who sees Declan as a person instead of a transaction—but is she a victim of her father’s cruelty, or is there a layer of complicity there? The synopsis hints at a forbidden alliance, but alliances are rarely pure. Truth be told, the most interesting dynamics in books like this—*The Cruel Prince* by Holly Black, *The Invisible Life of Addie LaRue* by V.E. Schwab—come from the cracks between characters, not the grand gestures.

And then there’s the faerie market itself, which feels like it could be either a lush, immersive world or a hollow set piece. The synopsis lists its wares—skills, memories, years—like a menu, which is fine, but where’s the *strangeness*? Where’s the moment when the reader realizes this isn’t just a marketplace, but a living, breathing entity with its own rules? The best faerie markets—like the one in *The Bear and the Nightingale* by Katherine Arden—aren’t just places to buy things; they’re places where the boundaries of reality bend. Here, it feels more like a pawn shop with a side of existential dread.

But—here’s where I pivot—maybe that’s the point. Maybe Gonzales is going for a different kind of tension, one that’s more psychological than atmospheric. The idea of a stolen potion that *always* pays the price, no matter who drinks it, is a brilliant twist on the usual "you get what you bargain for" trope. It’s the kind of rule that could make the story feel inevitable, even tragic, because the system is rigged from the start. And if Calla’s entire life has been spent fearing her father, then her alliance with Declan isn’t just about rescue—it’s about defiance. That’s where the real stakes lie, if the synopsis is to be believed.

Yet—

Literally.

The vagueness here is maddening. We’re told there’s a "terrible price," but what is it? Is it literal? Metaphorical? Both? The synopsis doesn’t say. We’re told Calla has spent a lifetime fearing her father, but what does that fear look like? Is it the fear of a child, or the fear of someone who’s already been broken? The answers aren’t here, and that’s the problem. A synopsis should tease, not just list. It should make you *feel* the weight of the story, not just describe it.

I keep thinking about how this could work—how it *should* work—because the bones of it are good. The tension between Declan’s privilege and Calla’s captivity, the slow unraveling of his mind, the faerie market as a place where nothing is safe. But the synopsis doesn’t give us enough to hang our hopes on. It’s like being handed a single, beautiful feather and told it’s a whole bird.

And now I have to go patch a hole in the drywall from last night’s indoor football incident. The paint’s already peeling, and I’m pretty sure I just realized I don’t have the right kind of filler.
