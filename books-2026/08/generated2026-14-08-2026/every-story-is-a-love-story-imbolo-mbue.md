The espresso machine is shrieking like a dying banshee and someone just ordered an oat milk lavender matcha at peak rush, which means I am currently staring at Imbolo Mbue’s *Every Story Is a Love Story* with the kind of boundless, soul-crushing joy usually reserved for a root canal. 

Look. I like my literature dark. I want ghosts dragging entrails across Victorian parquet. I want Stephen King at his most unhinged or Shirley Jackson making suburban paranoia feel like a knife between the ribs. But what are we serving up here? Grief. Remorse. Forgiveness. It is the literary equivalent of a warm chamomile tea when you asked for double-shot gasoline. 

### The Architecture of Guilt

Wolo lost his wife in a car crash three years ago. Tragic. Devastating. Absolutely ripe for psychological degradation. Yet the synopsis immediately ruins the potential for delicious, rotting angst by introducing—wait for it—hope. 

A letter arrives from the driver who killed her. Cue the pearl-clutching from the family. The pastor screams devilry. The mother slams doors. The Cameroonian stepmother phones in from across continents to say burn it. This is peak daytime soap opera nonsense masquerading as high-art emotional excavation. And yet—Mbue can write. *Behold the Dreamers* was a gut-punch of socioeconomic despair. So why are we pivoting to Hallmark channel catharsis where the widower meets the killer and they bond over shared suffering? Make it make sense. It is giving emotional whiplash. It is giving toxic positivity wrapped in literary prose. 

### Anticipating the Bleed

We are looking at a masterclass in bourgeois emotional processing. The questions posed by the synopsis—what do we owe those who hurt us? can forgiveness ease suffering?—sound less like a searing psychological inquiry and more like a book club trap designed to make people weep into their chardonnays. 

Where is the rot? Where is the festering resentment that rots a man from the inside out until he is just a hollowed-out husk of bitterness? No, we have to go on the uneven path from heartbreak to healing. Yawn. Give me a protagonist who burns the letter and drinks himself into oblivion. Give me a narrative descent into madness where forgiveness is an illusion and trauma is a permanent parasite. 

Instead, we will likely get a deeply moving, beautifully told treatise on grace. Riveting. Truly, nothing screams edge-of-your-seat dread quite like the prospect of two people talking through their feelings over lukewarm tea. 

I need to get back to wiping down the pastry case and pretending I cannot hear the teenager at register three butchering the pronunciation of macchiato. Also, I really need to patch a hole in my favorite, impossibly worn tweed jacket before the elbow completely disintegrates into the void.
