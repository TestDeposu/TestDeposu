**Page Count:** Unknown

The rain in Paris never falls like it should—too heavy for a city that prides itself on elegance, too slow for the kind of urgency that might actually wash away the sins of its streets. And yet, here we are, staring at this *Mothsblood* synopsis like a particularly stubborn stain on a priceless tapestry, wondering if Lynn D. Jung has the audacity to believe this mess of alchemists, cults, and immortal headmistresses will hold a candle to the kind of prose that doesn’t make you wince at its own pretensions.

The premise is, of course, *deliciously* familiar—another young woman fleeing her past, another university steeped in secrets, another murder that smells suspiciously like a plot device waiting to be unspooled. But where is the originality? Where is the *bite*? The synopsis reads like a fever dream stitched together from the leftovers of *The Night Circus* and *The Shadow of the Wind*, with a dash of *The Alchemist* thrown in for good measure—because nothing says "literary depth" like a protagonist who must choose between power and morality, as if that’s not the entire foundation of every YA novel since *Twilight* decided vampires were the new gothic romance.

Larkspur Lee, then. A name that sounds like it belongs on a label of absinthe, not a protagonist’s shoulders. She’s an alchemist, which is either a brilliant detail or a lazy way to imply she’s "special" without actually having to explain why. And her past? Oh, it’s *mysterious*, of course—because nothing says "character development" like a void that’s been carefully left unfilled for the reader to fill with their own existential dread. The fact that her mentor is back in her life, playing some kind of game with her, is either a clever twist or the kind of contrivance that makes you wonder if Jung has ever actually read a book where a mentor isn’t a walking red flag.

But then—*the Promethean Genesis*. A cult. Because nothing says "high stakes" like a group of people who worship fire and chaos. The synopsis doesn’t even bother to name-drop what they *actually* do, leaving us to assume it’s something horrifying, something that will make Lark’s past look like a Sunday picnic. And yet, the real question is: does Jung have the chops to make this more than just another "dark academia" pastiche? Because if she doesn’t, we’re left with a story that feels like it was written by someone who’s read too many synopses and not enough books.

And then there’s the university itself—a place where the headmistress is immortal, where the walls might whisper, where the very air is thick with secrets. It’s the kind of setting that should scream *Gaiman* or *Le Guin*, but instead, it’s reduced to a backdrop for Lark’s internal monologue, which, based on this synopsis, is going to be about as compelling as a love letter to a stranger.

But—*the pivot*—perhaps that’s the point. Maybe Jung is playing with the very idea of "dark academia," twisting it into something sharper, more jagged. Maybe the alchemy isn’t just about turning lead into gold, but about turning words into something that *burns*. If so, then this could be the kind of book that makes you forget you’ve read a thousand others before it. But if it’s just another exercise in atmospheric dread with no real substance, then we’re all just waiting for the next trend to come along and bury it under a pile of overhyped YA.

The real crime here isn’t the murder of Petronelle Flamel—it’s the murder of originality. And if Jung can’t deliver something more than a synopsis that reads like a Wikipedia page in progress, then she’s already lost.
