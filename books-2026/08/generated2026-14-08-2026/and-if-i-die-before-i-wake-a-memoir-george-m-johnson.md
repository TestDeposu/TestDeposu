George M. Johnson's *And If I Die Before I Wake* is shaping up to be the literary equivalent of a tabloid headlines. 'HIV diagnosis, alcoholism, tremendous loss, and nationwide book bans' – you had me at 'HIV diagnosis', actually, but it's this laundry list of suffering that makes me wonder: can this memoir possibly be anything more than a litany of grievances?

Yet, the author promises a 'testament' – and if that means what I think it does, we're about to get a searing, unvarnished account of what it's like to be human in a world designed to silence you. The problem is, the language around it is curiously, almost aggressively vague. 'Transformation, healing, and hope' is all very inspiring, but what, exactly, does that look like in the context of Johnson's life?

The parallels with authors like Toni Morrison and Ta-Nehisi Coates are obvious, and it's telling that Johnson cites these writers – Morrison's mastery of the painful and the beautiful, Coates's unwavering commitment to the Black experience. But is *And If I Die Before I Wake* more than just a well-made imitation? I'm skeptical, given the way the synopsis feels designed to elicit a Pavlovian response from potential readers. This isn't exactly subtle – and that's precisely what makes it so effective as a marketing tool.

Still, for all my reservations, there's something undeniably compelling about witnessing an author grapple with the darkness that's been thrust upon them. The question, of course, is whether Johnson will use this platform to explore the genuinely profound and the merely dramatic. It's a tightrope we've seen many authors walk before, but one that requires an unflinching commitment to honesty – and a willingness to confront the abyss head-on.

### 'Disintegrating Under the Weight of Its Own Significance'

Johnson mentions the 'Black family' as a recurring theme, but what does that even mean anymore? Hasn't this concept been endlessly dissected and reinterpreted by every author who's followed the Coates-Morrison-Ward triumvirate? It's becoming clear that what's needed is some fresh blood, some bold experiments in storytelling – not just another memoir about the author's unending struggles, however well-written it may be.

Speaking of fresh blood, have you seen that notorious 1979 opera, *Ragtime*? That was a bold experiment in fusion, bringing together disparate elements from different worlds to create something entirely new. Not that I see Johnson following in Harrison Birtwistle's footsteps just yet...

### 'A Memoir Without Metaphor'

I should mention that the author's use of metaphor in this synopsis feels curiously underdeveloped – there's no grand overarching narrative or even a particularly strong thread of symbolism tying the various elements together. It's all very grounded, very concrete, but in a way that feels oddly, almost willfully, bare-bones.

Now, I'm about to make a truly outrageous prediction: Johnson's book will be compared to Jesmyn Ward's *The Song of Achilles* at some point in the near future. Not because the two books have any obvious thematic connections, but because they both deal with trauma in a way that feels almost shockingly, almost disturbingly, honest. And if that's the case – if Johnson's memoir is more about the brutal, unvarnished truth of suffering than about making some grand point about the human condition – then I might just have to revise my opinion of this book entirely.

At any rate, I need to get back to the stacks – I've got to reorganize these towering chaos of reference books before I lose my mind entirely. All this talk of *And If I Die Before I Wake* has given me an existential crisis about my own writing process...
