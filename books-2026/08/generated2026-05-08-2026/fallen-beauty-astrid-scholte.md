**Author:** Astrid Scholte  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-03 APL

The sun bleeds through the blinds of my office, casting jagged gold across the spine of a first-edition *The Picture of Dorian Gray*—because, of course, I’m still waiting for the right book to justify its purchase. And here we are, with *Fallen Beauty*, a synopsis that smells of old oil paints and unpaid rent, the kind of premise that makes you wonder if Scholte has been reading too many *Crimson Peak* afternoons by the pool. But let’s be honest, the real question is: does it have the *gravitas* of a true gothic romance, or is it just another summer blockbuster waiting to be drowned in a glass of prosecco?

The setting, if we can call it that, is a prison of a tower—hardly a new trope, but the execution here is *potentially* interesting. Thorelea’s life is one of forced artistry, a modern-day *Sisyphean* artist trapped in a cycle of painting the dead. The imagery is rich, the stakes clear: she’s not just a prisoner, she’s a *captive muse*, and the moment that dead animal stirs back to life? That’s the kind of narrative hook that could either elevate or embarrass. It’s the kind of twist that makes you think of *The Night Circus*’s slow-burn mystery, but with the emotional weight of a *Rebecca* protagonist—if Rebecca had been a painter instead of a wife. The problem? The synopsis doesn’t *show* us the horror of it. It tells us Thorelea is "forced," but does she *resist*? Does she *hate* the act of painting? Or is she, like so many tragic heroines, complicit in her own suffering? That’s the kind of psychological depth that separates a good gothic romance from a forgettable one.

And then there’s the court. A royal court where immortality is *bought* with portraits—because nothing says "love" like a magical portrait contract. The idea of a painter sustaining the monarchy’s eternity is *deliciously* dark, a twist on the classic "artist as doomed soul" trope. But—*and this is critical*—does Scholte have the chops to make this feel fresh? The royal court is a well-worn setting, but the synopsis doesn’t hint at whether it’s being subverted. Is this a *Game of Thrones* meets *The Secret History* vibe, or is it just another fairy tale with fancier costumes? The lack of specificity here is *frustrating*. Where’s the political intrigue? The courtly betrayals? The synopsis gives us Kelter, the prince desperate for immortality, but is he a love interest, a pawn, or just another man with a deal too good to be true? The way the relationship is framed—"they meet at night in secret"—sounds like it could be *lush* or *cliché*, depending on how Scholte handles it. And let’s not forget, the deal itself is a *dangerous gamble*: Thorelea paints, Kelter escapes, but what’s the catch? Is it her soul? Her art? Her *heart*? The synopsis doesn’t say. It’s all too vague, like a menu with only the prices circled.

Yet—*and this is where the romance in me takes over*—the core of this story is the tension between love and freedom. That’s the kind of dichotomy that makes a gothic romance *sing*. Think *Jane Eyre*’s Bertha Mason, but with a paintbrush instead of a wild heart. Or *The Ten Thousand Doors of January*, where the protagonist’s desires are tied to a larger, magical world. But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t *show* us the emotional stakes. Is Thorelea in love with Kelter? Is she just using him? Is she *terrified* of what her powers might cost her? The way it’s written, it reads like a transactional romance, which is fine—*The Hunger Games* did it well—but it’s not *Fallen Beauty*’s job to prove it. Scholte’s got a chance to make this *haunting*, but right now, it’s just a haunting *promise*.

And then there’s the art itself. The synopsis mentions Thorelea’s powers, but does it *feel* like we’re getting a sense of her genius? Is she a *real* artist, or just a plot device? The best gothic romances—*The Turn of the Screw*, *Dracula*—make the art *matter*. They make the brushstrokes *bloody*. Here, we’re told she’s talented, but we’re not shown how. Is she a *Watteau* of melancholy, or a *Goya* of rage? The synopsis doesn’t tell us. It’s a shame, because the potential is *there*. A painter who can bring the dead to life? That’s the kind of magic that could make this book *unforgettable*.

But will it? The summer market is *cruel*. There’s *The Silent Patient* and *The Vanishing Half* and a dozen other books vying for the same gothic romance shelf. *Fallen Beauty* needs more than just a pretty premise—it needs *bite*. It needs *anger*. It needs to make us *feel* the weight of Thorelea’s choices. Right now, it’s just a shadow of what it could be.
