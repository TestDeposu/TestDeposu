Sweat drips down my face as I scrub the last of the pots from the lunch shift, my mind wandering to the latest literary obsession – Mariko Turk's *The Museum of Modern Love*. A romance novel, how quaint. Yet, the synopsis hints at something more – a deconstruction of the very notion of destiny and love.

As I dry my hands, I ponder the setup: Miki Kawamura, a hopeless romantic, plans to confess her feelings to her best friend Auden by retracing the path her grandparents took through the Metropolitan Museum of Art the night they fell in love. It's a clever conceit, leveraging the mystique of a famous museum to heighten the emotional stakes. But, of course, destiny has other plans – Miki's ex, Lou McSweeney, shows up instead of Auden, and the story careens off in a different direction. Fascinating in theory.

The use of a museum as the primary setting is a bold choice, imbuing the narrative with the weight of history and art. It's a space where people often go to escape the mundane, to lose themselves in the grandeur of human creativity. And yet, for Miki, it's a place of intense personal significance, a connection to her family's past. I wonder how Turk will balance the character's emotional journey with the backdrop of the museum – will it feel like a gimmick, or a genuine exploration of the human experience?

The character dynamics are, of course, the real draw. Miki's plan to confess her feelings to Auden is already a high-stakes gamble, but the introduction of Lou McSweeney adds a layer of complexity. What's the nature of their past relationship? Is Lou truly the "world's most jaded cynic," or is that just a facade? And how will Miki's feelings for Auden evolve in the face of this unexpected twist? Hardly a straightforward romance, it seems.

The comparison to authors like Lynn Painter and Jenny Han is telling – Turk is likely aiming for a similar blend of wit, charm, and heartfelt emotion. But will *The Museum of Modern Love* transcend the familiar tropes of the genre, or simply rehash them in a new setting? Time will tell.

As I clock out of my shift and head into the sweltering summer evening, I find myself wondering: will Turk's novel be a nuanced exploration of love and destiny, or just another iteration of the same old romantic cliches? Can she balance the character's emotional journeys with the demands of the plot, or will it all feel like a contrived exercise in sentimentalism? And what's with the title – is it a reference to the museum, or something more abstract?
