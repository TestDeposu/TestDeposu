Dirt under my fingernails, the afternoon sun beating down hard on my tomato cages—August in the garden is a sweaty, frantic business, but my mind was completely snagged on Jane Schoenbrun’s upcoming novel, *Public Access Afterworld*. 

And what a premise to ponder while staking heavy beefsteak vines! 

### Anticipatory Preview

The official hook drops us straight into 1988 with a televangelist-style weatherman blowing his brains out on live television after muttering about a mysterious broadcast. Grim? Absolutely. But Schoenbrun—the brilliant mind behind *I Saw the TV Glow*—has always possessed a rare knack for unearthing the profound terror lurking inside our flickering analog screens. Yet we fast-forward through decades, moving from lonely suburban teens watching the analog-to-digital transition in 2009 to a trans content moderator named Bethany toiling away at a bleak Austin tech campus seventeen years later. 

Fascinating in theory. But what actually *is* this rogue pirate network? 

### Thematic Analysis

The synopsis leaves the core mechanics wonderfully, maddeningly opaque. Yet the thematic trajectory already cuts deep. We are looking at a sprawling autopsy of media consumption, isolation, and the electronic tethers that bind our collective psyche. Bethany reviews horrific videos all night for a faceless megacorp called GlobalVill—a deliciously scathing touch of modern corporate purgatory. And then a young streamer starts broadcasting distress calls to the very same mythic static that swallowed those kids back in the basement. 

Will the execution match the ambitious scope? Hard to say from a blurb alone. 

### A Bold Prediction

Expect a dizzying, jagged artifact of speculative fiction. Think Mark Z. Danielewski crashing headfirst into the late-night dread of David Lynch, spliced with the hyper-online alienation explored by writers like Patricia Lockwood. It feels destined to polarize readers who demand neat plot resolutions while thrilling those of us who prefer our existential horror dripping with nostalgia and systemic critique. Time will tell if the narrative weight buckles under its own massive timeline spanning decades and realities. But if anyone can pull off this kind of psychedelic, VHS-static-soaked descent into an electric hell, it is Schoenbrun. 

Now, back to these tomatoes before the heat gets the better of me. Can a book about a cursed TV signal actually save us from the algorithmic monotony of our current reality?
