**Author:** Philippa Gregory  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-20 APL

The central AC is wheezing like an old retriever in this August humidity, pushing eighty degrees of sticky dead air across my desk while I stare at Philippa Gregory’s latest pitch. My chest still aches—a dull, stubborn fracture from the kind of loss that leaves furniture sitting too heavy in empty rooms—and yet here I am, parsing historical fiction about fourteenth-century court intrigue. Or fifteenth-century, technically. The synopsis says 1420. Not that accuracy changes the weight of the thing. 

Gregory is returning to her stomping grounds with *Royal Witch*, tracking Eleanor Cobham from a nobody girl at Henry V’s court to a woman eyeing the English crown itself. And how does she get there? Ambition, sorcery, treason. The usual trifecta for a Tudor-adjacent bloodbath, even if we are backing up a bit chronologically from her usual stomping grounds. 

### Thematic Analysis of Power and Survival

What catches my eye through the haze of this heat is the psychological survival arc. The synopsis outlines a classic trauma-adjacent pivot—the moment a young woman realizes the nice, safe domestic future she dreamed up is a fairy tale whispered by men who intend to sell her out. Working for a Countess who commands an army does something to a person's neurology. It forces a hard recalibration. Suddenly, compliance looks less like virtue and more like suicide. 

Yet Gregory’s synopsis relies heavily on generic thriller beats. Sorcery and darker arts. It is a bit lazy, frankly. We have seen this playbook before from Hilary Mantel or Sharon Penman, where female agency gets hand-waved away by a sudden dabble in witchcraft when the historical record gets too narrow to climb out of otherwise. Does Eleanor actually practice the occult, or is it just the label stuck on her by hostile men terrified of a sharp mind? The synopsis refuses to say. 

### A Critical Look at the Pitch

Fascinating in theory. Frustratingly vague in execution. Gregory knows how to construct a fast-paced historical ride—think Philippa Gregory meets Alison Weir at a particularly cutthroat dinner party—but I worry about the character psychology here. When a person survives a broken system, when their foundational expectations shatter into jagged pieces, they do not usually pivot straight to ruling the country via black magic. They stutter. They adapt. They break down behind closed doors while keeping up appearances in the grand halls. 

Will Gregory grant Eleanor that messy, human interiority, or will she flatten her into a pantomime villain riding high on ambition alone? Time will tell. October is a long way off, and this apartment is not getting any cooler. How many more manuscripts will I read this afternoon while waiting for the compressor outside to kick back on?
