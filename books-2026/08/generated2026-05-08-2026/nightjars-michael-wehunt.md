**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-29 APL

Summer afternoons can be excruciating, the air conditioning struggling to keep up with the oppressive heat outside. It's days like these that I crave intellectual stimulation – and fortunately, my latest obsession fits the bill: Michael Wehunt's *Nightjars*, a literary endeavor that promises to mash up the psychological thrill of *Memento* with the dark, Gothic horror of *Dracula*. But what, exactly, does that mean for this book?

At first glance, Wehunt's premise seems tantalizingly familiar – the old photographs, the repressed memories, the sense of impending doom that haunts the protagonist, Luke Oshel. And yet, I'm left wondering: what specific mechanisms will Wehunt employ to craft the unique narrative experience he claims to be delivering? Will he rely on the same old tropes that have been doing the rounds in horror and suspense for years, or will he inject something entirely new into the mix?

One aspect that does pique my interest is the notion of family legacy – specifically, the hidden, potentially monstrous aspects of one's own identity. Think *The Fisherman* by John Langan and its exploration of cosmic horror through the lens of family secrets. This theme speaks to my current preoccupation with the darker aspects of human nature, and I'm intrigued to see how Wehunt tackles this idea.

Now, let's talk about Luke Oshel. We're told he's a man exposed for crimes he doesn't remember committing – but what does that say about his mental state, his sense of self? Does this narrative thread evoke the works of Thomas Ligotti, with his eerie, existential explorations of the human psyche? Or is Wehunt drawing from a more contemporary wellspring of psychological terror?

And what of the supporting cast, not least Wehunt's North Georgia Appalachians backdrop? Will this rural, isolated setting become a character in its own right – a la *The Wicker Man* or *The Ritual*?

Ultimately, of course, this is Wehunt's *Nightjars*, and its success or failure will depend on how effectively he balances these disparate elements. I wouldn't be surprised if we see a more experimental, avant-garde approach, a la Jeff VanderMeer's *Annihilation* – but then again, that might be precisely what's so terrifying about this concept.
