**Author:** L.J. Andrews  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Sweat is dripping down my nose, pooling in the dirt right beside my prize Early Girl tomato plants. August in the garden is a relentless, humid endurance test. Yet my mind is entirely somewhere else—fixated on the ancient, stubborn tropes of Fae folklore. I am muttering to myself, glaring at a rogue weed, because the synopsis for L.J. Andrews’s upcoming *The Stolen Crown* just riled me up. Why must every ancient myth of powerful immortals inevitably boil down to two stubborn fools getting shipwrecked together? 

Consider the setup. Earth-fae princess Mira has the entire world at her feet. But does she care about political power or ancient bloodlines? Hardly. She fixes her gaze entirely on Tait—the miserable, brooding cousin of the Ever-King who apparently radiates pure hostility. A classic grumpy-versus-sunshine dynamic. Groundbreaking stuff. Or perhaps, utterly predictable. 

Fascinating in theory. Tiresome in execution. 

We have seen this exact mythological dance before. It is the literary equivalent of throwing two mutually allergic cats into a burlap sack and waiting for sparks. Think of the sweeping romantic tension in Sarah J. Maas or the sharp-edged banter of Jennifer L. Armentrout. Yet Andrews relies heavily on the age-old shipwreck trope to force proximity. Marooned on an island. Just the two of them. How original. One can practically predict the exact chapter where they are forced to share a single blanket while complaining about the dampness. 

And yet, I find myself oddly invested while shoving compost around my tomato roots. Why? Because folklore thrives on isolation. Ancient deities and mythical beings always discover their true vulnerabilities only when stripped of their kingdoms, their courts, and their titles. Mira has to pierce that thorny exterior of his. A dangerous gamble. Time will tell if Andrews actually gives these characters genuine emotional grit or simply relies on a lazy 2-out-of-5 spice rating to carry the momentum. 

Let us not ignore the looming threat mentioned at the tail end of the blurb. A mysterious new danger threatens the fragile peace between their lands. Naturally. Because a simple enemies-to-lovers island excursion is never enough without a geopolitical crisis interrupting their brooding. It is a standard formula for this genre. Slow burn. Found family. Grumpy men learning to smile because a cheerful princess refuses to be ignored. 

I suspect this fourth installment in *The Ever Seas* series will lean heavily into comfort-read territory. Fans of romantic fantasy will devour it on release day next year without blinking an eye. Me? I will likely be sitting right here, covered in garden dirt, wondering why authors insist on stranding royal heirs on deserted islands when they could be plotting actual mythological revolutions. 

Will readers ever demand more than manufactured proximity and forced island bed-sharing?
