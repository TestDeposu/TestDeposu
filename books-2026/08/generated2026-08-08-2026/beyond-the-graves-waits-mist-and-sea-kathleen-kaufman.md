**Author:** Kathleen Kaufman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-29 APL

Oh, the way this synopsis *lingers*—like the tide pulling back from the shore, revealing something slick and unknown beneath. Harper Hafdóttir, a marine biologist with the quiet precision of a woman who’s spent too long staring into the abyss, isn’t just chasing an octopus. She’s chasing *herself*, and the island of Bell is the perfect mirror for that kind of reckoning. The fact that she’s drawn to a place rumored to be haunted by "spirits and *other things*"—that’s not just atmospheric fluff, is it? That’s a *romantic* detail, the kind that makes your pulse quicken like the first note of a song you can’t name but know you’ll hum for days.

And Lady Loreana Bell—oh, *there’s* a woman worth studying. A matriarchal ruler of an island that’s been a sanctuary for women fleeing persecution for centuries? That’s not just world-building; that’s *power dynamics* with a capital P. The synopsis doesn’t spell it out, but I’m already imagining the tension between Harper’s scientific detachment and Loreana’s centuries-old mysticism. It’s the kind of dynamic that could either feel like a *fantastical love story* (think *The Night Circus* meets *The Secret History*) or a slow-burning clash of ideologies (à la *The Shadow of the Wind*). The fact that Loreana seems to have been *expecting* Harper? That’s not just convenient—it’s *deliciously* ominous.

But let’s talk about the *dreaming*. Harper’s haunted by visions, and the synopsis drops the journal of a young woman in a cult like it’s a clue in a murder mystery. That’s not just foreshadowing; that’s *psychological horror* with a literary twist. It’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder: Is Harper’s trauma tied to the island’s history, or is she the one who’s been haunting *it* all along? The way the synopsis weaves in seismic activity, rogue waves, and creatures that defy explanation—it’s not just sci-fi fluff. It’s *mythic*, the kind of stuff that makes you think of *The Fisherman* by John Langan or *The Only Good Indians* by Stephen Graham Jones. The island isn’t just a setting; it’s a *character*, and it’s breathing.

Yet—here’s where the synopsis stumbles. It’s *too* vague. The tsunami threat? The true purpose? The "other things" lurking in the fog? It’s all left dangling like a baited hook. That’s not a flaw—it’s *promise*. But it also means we’re left wondering: Is this a gothic horror? A slow-burn romance? A psychological thriller? The synopsis doesn’t commit. And that’s *fine*, because the best books leave you craving more. But I can’t help but compare it to *The Last House on Needless Street* by Catriona Ward, where the mystery is so tightly wound you can feel the tension in every comma. This feels more like *The Haunting of Hill House*—beautiful, eerie, but still clinging to its secrets.

And then there’s the *romance*. Oh, the romance. Harper’s trauma, the island’s history, the way Loreana’s gaze seems to see *through* her—it’s all the ingredients for something tender and dangerous. But the synopsis doesn’t say *how* it unfolds. Is it slow-burning, like the way saltwater evaporates on skin? Or is it a storm that hits all at once? The way it’s framed, I’m leaning toward the former. But I also can’t shake the feeling that this could be the kind of book where the love story isn’t the point—it’s the *consequence* of the truth Harper uncovers. Like in *The Starless Sea* by Erin Morgenstern, where the romance is woven into the fabric of the mystery itself.

The fact that it’s compared to *The Sirens* and *The Amalfi Curse* is *chef’s kiss*. Those books are all about women who are both scholars and seekers, drawn to places that feel like they’re holding their destinies. Harper’s a marine biologist, but she’s also a woman with dreams that won’t stay buried. That duality is *gold*. And the way the synopsis plays with the idea of the island as a liminal space—where history, nature, and the supernatural blur—it’s the kind of thing that makes you want to press your forehead against the page and *feel* the fog.

But here’s the thing: I’m not sure if this is a book about love or survival. Or maybe it’s both. The way it’s written, it feels like Harper’s journey is as much about *letting go* as it is about *finding*. And that’s the kind of emotional core that makes a book linger in your mind long after you’ve turned the last page. It’s the kind of thing that makes you want to curl up on a sofa with a glass of something strong and *finally* read it.
