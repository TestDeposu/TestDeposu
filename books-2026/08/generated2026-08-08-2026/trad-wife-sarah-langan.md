**Author:** Sarah Langan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-29 APL

The air in August is thick with the scent of damp earth and overripe peaches—perfect for the kind of suffocating dread that clings to a story like *Trad Wife*, where the very soil seems to whisper secrets. Sarah Langan’s upcoming novel, billed as a razor-sharp dissection of performative domesticity, arrives with the weight of a gilded cage. The synopsis teases a journalist, Jenny Kaplan, descending into the pastoral nightmare of Black Swan Farm, where Mia Wright’s "trad wife" persona is less a lifestyle and more a carefully curated abyss. But here’s the rub: the prose doesn’t just *describe* the horror—it *performs* it, mirroring the very illusion it critiques.

The synopsis leans heavily on the gothic tradition of the "perfect house that hides something worse"—think Shirley Jackson’s *The Haunting of Hill House*, but with the added layer of modern femininity as both armor and cage. Mia Wright’s farm isn’t just a setting; it’s a character in itself, a sprawling, sun-bleached monolith where the children’s laughter curdles into nursery rhymes and the camera never lies—except it does, doesn’t it? The tension between the *seen* and the *unseen* is the novel’s true villain, a theme Langan shares with the likes of Carmen Maria Machado, whose *In the Dream House* exposed the fractures beneath queer domesticity. But where Machado’s work is introspective, *Trad Wife* promises to be a scalpel, dissecting the performative nature of womanhood with the same clinical precision as a Victorian-era dissection manual.

Yet—*yet*—the synopsis is frustratingly vague. We’re told Jenny loses time, her hair, her grip on reality, but we’re not given the *texture* of that unraveling. Is it psychological? Supernatural? A slow-burn descent into madness, or something far more sinister? The lack of specificity feels deliberate, a nod to the unreliable narrator trope that’s been mined ad nauseam, but also a missed opportunity. Compare this to *Mexican Gothic*, where Shirley Jackson’s influence is palpable in the way the decay isn’t just physical—it’s *cultural*, a rot that festers beneath the surface of polite society. *Trad Wife* risks becoming just another cautionary tale about the dangers of social media, unless it leans harder into the gothic’s love of ambiguity.

And then there’s the class angle—oh, the class angle. The "trad wife" aesthetic isn’t just a lifestyle; it’s a *performance of privilege*, a way for women to signal their compliance with patriarchal structures while still wielding a certain power. The synopsis hints at this, but it doesn’t explore whether Jenny’s own career desperation makes her complicit in the very illusion she’s meant to expose. Is she the outsider, or just another predator in a landscape of curated lies? The tension between Jenny and Mia feels like it could be the novel’s emotional core, but the synopsis doesn’t give us enough to chew on.

My prediction? *Trad Wife* will be a masterclass in atmospheric dread, but only if Langan resists the urge to explain everything. The best gothic horror—*The Turn of the Screw*, *We Have Always Lived in the Castle*—lets the reader fill in the blanks. If this stays true to its synopsis, it’ll be a chilling, if slightly undercooked, critique of modern femininity. But if Langan dares to be bold, it could be something far more unsettling—a novel that doesn’t just ask *what’s real*, but *who gets to decide?*
