Ah, another adaptation of the Danish prince. Must we? Yet, when the ink smells of old library dust and unrequited longing, my aristocratic sensibilities simply surrender. Kat Dunn’s upcoming *Rottenheart* arrives draped in velvet shadows, promising a sapphic haunting that steals straight from Shakespeare’s bones. Taylor Jenkins Reid demands to inject it directly into her veins. But what about us, darlings? What about those of us who prefer our poetry dripping with decay and our tropes strictly traditional?

### The Architecture of Melancholy

Herne House sounds magnificent. Suffolk is awfully drafty, of course—my estate there has drafty corridors that positively echo with ancestral judgment—yet it provides the ideal backdrop for madness. The synopsis sketches a familiar architectural tragedy: two grand homes, a suffocating mother named Lydia, and a childhood friendship between Odette and Cecilia that surely blossomed into something forbidden before the rot set in. 

And oh, the rot. 

Dunn leans heavily into the Gothic machinery we adore. A sudden death. A cold aunt taking the reins—literally marrying into the tragedy, one assumes, given the *Hamlet* framework. But let us pause. Is the synopsis terribly vague about the actual mechanics of the haunting? It is. We are told Lydia never truly left, hovering ready to exact revenge. Naturally. But how does this manifest? Does the wallpaper bleed? Do the floorboards groan in pentameter? One prays for lyrical perfection, not mere cheap thrills. 

### Sapphic Shadows and Spectral Spite

We have seen this before. The gothic romance saturated in queer yearning is practically the house wine of our current literary salon. It whispers of Shirley Jackson, occasionally brushes shoulders with Carmen Maria Machado, and nods politely to gothic classics over an evening glass of port. Yet, if Dunn manages to capture the claustrophobic dread of aristocratic confinement—the absolute terror of a mother’s parasitic devotion—*Rottenheart* might just salvage an exhausted trope. 

A prediction, from one who knows the weight of old silver and older grudges: the prose will either be insufferably purple or exquisitely sharp. There is no middle ground when ghosts and grieving maidens are involved. I suspect the dynamic between Odette and Cecilia will provide the requisite emotional devastation, while the spectral mother acts as a clumsy catalyst for domestic ruin. One can only hope the poetry holds. 

Must fly now. I simply must go organize my walk-in closet—the cashmere has not been properly aired, and the impending autumn damp waits for no one.
