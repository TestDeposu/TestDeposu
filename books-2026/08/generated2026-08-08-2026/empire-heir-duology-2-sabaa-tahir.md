**Author:** Sabaa Tahir  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Darling, my first-edition collection of *An Ember in the Ashes* occupies a rather climate-controlled nook in my Mayfair townhouse, bound in rare morocco leather, of course. Yet here we are, casting an elitist eye toward Sabaa Tahir’s upcoming sequel, *Empire*. Or is it merely *Empire*? The marketing materials are delightfully opaque about the actual subtitle, a trend I find distressingly pedestrian—reminiscent of Leigh Bardugo’s earlier publicity stunts before she learned proper cadence. 

Let us examine the text through a sociological lens. What do we actually know from this publisher-fed brief? Absolutely nothing substantial. But oh, the class dynamics! We have slum-dwelling orphans and destitute trackers crossing paths with crown princes. How very Dickensian, albeit dressed in synthetic fantasy tropes. 

### The Architecture of Despair

Aiz in the Kegari slums. Sirsha banished for an unforgivable crime. Quil trapped by a tragic lineage. It is a classic trichotomy of social strata—the bottom, the margin, and the apex. Tahir relies heavily on these rigid socioeconomic brackets to manufacture friction. But does she transcend the genre's predictable trappings, or merely rehash the tired structural violence we already consume with our morning Darjeeling? 

One must wonder if the publisher’s summary is deliberately vague to conceal a lack of structural innovation. Yet—and this is where my curiosity flickers—the psychological weight assigned to systemic greed feels genuinely potent. Power corrupts. Yawn. We have heard this since Euripides. But packaged with what the blurbs promise is a "ruthless" romantic edge? Perhaps there is hope for the prose yet. Or perhaps it is merely fodder for the masses who buy paperbacks at airports. 

Sorry, lost my train of thought. 

My assistant just handed me the uncorrected proof in custom slipcase, smelling faintly of imported ink and ambition. One expects the prose to crackle with the same kinetic energy that made Tahir a darling of the *New York Times* bestseller lists, though I privately suspect the romantic entanglements will eclipse the sociological critique. After all, the bourgeoisie love a good rebellion until it threatens their dividends. 

Watch closely how she handles the prince's redemption arc. It is a tightrope walk between genuine political awakening and exhausting white-savior-adjacent monarchism—even in secondary worlds. If Quil does not accidentally ruin the entire state apparatus by chapter four, I shall be profoundly disappointed. 

My sommelier just arrived with the new vintage.
