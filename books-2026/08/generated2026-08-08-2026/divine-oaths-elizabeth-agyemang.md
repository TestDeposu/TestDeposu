**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-01 APL

Teaching eighth-graders means watching kids teeter on that brutal cliff edge between childhood and the terrifying unknown of growing up. Naturally, when a synopsis crosses my desk that promises a brooding academic setting where a teenager suddenly inherits supernatural burdens, I pay attention. Elizabeth Agyemang’s upcoming *Divine Oaths* lands on my radar not just because of the publisher's glossy comparisons, but because it taps directly into the psychological wreckage my students face daily—the crushing weight of expectations. 

### The Heavy Burden of Teenage Agency

Look at the setup. Sianna Adu loses her family and instantly gains the supernatural ability to peer into lifelines, pasts, and futures. And what does she do? She takes dead-end jobs. How wonderfully, frustratingly realistic. Teenagers rarely get to choose their tragedies, yet they are expected to march forward as if their internal worlds haven't just been detonated. 

Dark academia as a genre—think Leigh Bardugo’s *Ninth House* or Tracy Deonn’s *Legendborn*—succeeds precisely because it treats adolescent anxiety with deadly seriousness. Westfield University isn't just a spooky campus hidden in a Virginia swamp. It is a metaphorical pressure cooker for youth culture. Yet, the synopsis relies heavily on familiar genre scaffolding. Secret societies? Check. Centuries-old curses? Double check. But does it reinvent the wheel? The jury is out. 

### Romance as a Trap Door

And then, naturally, we have Kasem Saint James. The "undeniably handsome heir" who ropes our protagonist into his mess. Sianna worries her newfound powers are tethered directly to him, forcing a proximity that screams enemies-to-lovers or reluctant allies. 

Here is my bold prediction as a reader who watches kids navigate hallway politics every single day: the romantic tension will either elevate this book into a masterclass of YA angst or drag it down into trope-heavy quicksand. The synopsis gives very little away about Sianna's internal voice outside of her trauma and her crush. I want bite. I want cynicism. If Sianna simply melts every time Kasem enters a room, I will throw the book across my classroom. 

Ultimately, Agyemang is playing in a crowded sandbox. The blending of supernatural destiny with institutional corruption mirrors the exact powerlessness twelve- and thirteen-year-olds feel within rigid school systems. They are forced to play games whose rules were written centuries before they were born. 

Now, if you will excuse me, I am going to go make a terrible pun about swamp-dwelling curses to annoy my wife.
