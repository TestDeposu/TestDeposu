**Author:** Laura Piper Lee  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-15 APL

Oh, *Doomsdate*—because nothing says feminist empowerment like a woman’s first priority being a *bucket list orgasm* while the world burns. Maisie Bennett, our doomsday prepper with a *taser* and a *utility belt full of weapons*, is framed as this badass survivalist—until we realize her entire emotional arc hinges on whether she can *act normal* on a date. And by "normal," we mean *not* brandishing a stun gun like a 1980s action heroine. The synopsis leans *hard* into the "feral rom-com" tagline, which is code for: *Here’s a woman who’s prepared for the apocalypse but not for the fact that her love interest might actually like her.*

Everett Greer, the "soft, liberal arts professor," is the classic male character who exists solely to be *taught* by the woman—*of course*—but the twist is that his lesson plan includes *sex education*. Because nothing says "equality" like a man’s emotional labor being reduced to a *curriculum*. The synopsis plays up the "deal" trope—she’ll teach him survival, he’ll teach her *how to live*—which is a neat way of saying she’s the one who’s *actually* functional, and he’s the one who needs *rescuing* from his own grief. But where’s the subversion? Where’s the moment where Maisie *doesn’t* need to be the one holding the taser? The synopsis treats her competence as a *quirk*, not a *strength*—because if she weren’t so *anxious* about being "normal," she’d already be the one in charge.

And let’s talk about the *zombies*—because nothing says "romantic tension" like a shared road trip with a *cat* as the emotional support animal. The GI virus is the perfect stand-in for modern dating anxiety: it *traps* people in their own bodies, turns them into *monsters*, and then *reanimates* them. Maisie and Everett’s dynamic is framed as a *partnership*, but the power imbalance is *deliberate*. She’s the one with the *practical skills*, he’s the one with the *emotional baggage*—and the only way he’s getting over it is through *her*. It’s the same old story: the woman’s survival instincts are her *superpower*, but the man’s *growth* is what matters.

The synopsis also leans into the *nerdy professor* trope, which is always a fun way to signal that the male lead is *intellectual but emotionally stunted*. Everett’s tragedy is that he’s *lost all lust for life*—but not for *sex*, apparently, because the plot demands he *wants* Maisie. The fact that he’s *forced* into dating her (thanks to his best friend’s catfishing) makes his eventual attraction feel *earned*—but only because the synopsis *promises* it will be *spicy*. The emotional stakes are reduced to: *Will they have sex before the zombies eat them?* Because that’s the real question, isn’t it?

The *cat* is the only character here who seems *actually* in control—Chicken, the feline sidekick, is the only one who doesn’t need to be *fixed* or *taught* or *rescued*. The synopsis treats animals as *companions*, not *characters*, which is a shame—because in a world where humans are failing, maybe the *real* lesson is that we don’t need to be *saved* by each other. But no, the focus is on Maisie’s *growth*—which, according to this synopsis, is learning to *let go of her weapons* and *trust a man*.

I’m already predicting this will be the kind of book where Maisie *finally* sheds her "anxious prepper" persona by *enduring* Everett’s emotional baggage, because nothing says *feminist empowerment* like a woman *adapting* to a man’s needs. And the *zombies*? They’re just a backdrop—because the *real* horror is that Maisie’s *only* goal is to *get laid* before the world ends. Because that’s what matters, right? Not survival, not agency—just *orgasm*.
