**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-22 APL

Ah, gather ‘round, my curious kin, while the cicadas shriek their summer hymns outside—let’s talk of *The Henchperson’s Guide to Unionizing*, a tale that smells of damp graveclothes and the faintest hint of roasted chestnuts over an open fire, like something straight out of a tavern brawl where the ale’s strong and the stories stronger. Marshall J. Moore has spun us a yarn that dances between the grotesque and the tender, a rare beast indeed—something like if *The League of Extraordinary Gentlemen* had a drunken, half-dead cousin who just wanted to unionize the undead.

Now, the synopsis leaves us with more questions than a village elder at a witch’s trial—*why* are these creatures huddled in Ravenwald Manor like rats in a barn?—but the bones of the story are there, sharp and gleaming. We have Igor, the everyman of the macabre, the kind of fellow who fixes his master’s electrocution problems while secretly stitching corpses together like a mad tailor. And then there’s the Count and his bride, Cornelia—*oh*, Cornelia—who sounds like she’s been carved from the same stubborn oak as the witch’s familiar, Grimalkin. These three, bound by necessity, begin to see past the chains of their masters, past the pitchforks of the villagers, and into something far more dangerous: *each other*.

The synopsis hints at a quiet revolution, one where the oppressed—whether they’re undead, cursed, or just tired of being ignored—band together. It’s the old story of the underdog, but Moore twists it with a wink. Where other tales might have us rooting for the Count’s grand scheme, here we’re given a choice: *do we cheer for the villain’s plan or the henchmen’s rebellion?* That’s the kind of tension that makes a story sing, like a lute played just out of tune—deliberately, beautifully.

But let’s not pretend this is all fire and fury. There’s warmth here, too, the kind that seeps into your bones like a mug of spiced wine on a winter’s night—*though we’re not in winter, are we?*—no, it’s the summer warmth of camaraderie, the kind that forms when strangers share a secret. The synopsis doesn’t tell us *how* Igor, Cornelia, and Grimalkin find their footing, but I’ll wager it’s as messy as a blacksmith’s forge. They’ll stumble. They’ll argue. They’ll probably burn down the manor at least once. And yet, there’s something in their stubbornness that feels *true*, like the old tales of the folk who refused to bow to kings or gods.

Moore’s touch here is light but sure, like the brushstrokes of a painter who knows when to leave a shadow in the dark. It’s the kind of story that makes you laugh one moment and hold your breath the next—*You can’t make this stuff up*—and yet, somehow, it feels inevitable, like the sun setting over the fields. It’s the kind of book that will sit on the shelf beside *The Graveyard Book* and *The Cruel Prince*, but with the edge of something new, something that doesn’t just tell a story but *feels* like a story you’ve always known, even if you’ve never heard it before.
