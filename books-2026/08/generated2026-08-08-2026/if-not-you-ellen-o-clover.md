**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-15 APL

Sweat is dripping down the back of my neck while I prune these late-summer tomato vines in the sticky August heat, and frankly, I am wondering why two rival copywriters need a whole month of travel just to pitch a luxury resort account. Does the modern advertising industry really operate on such bloated carbon footprints? Ellen O'Clover's upcoming *If Not You* drops this September, right as we harvest our root vegetables and wind down the growing season. And yet, I am sitting here on the porch, staring at a premise that feels distinctly manufactured.

Enemies-to-lovers. Office rivalry. Forced proximity on the open road. 

We have seen this blueprint a thousand times. Emily Henry built an empire on it; Ali Hazelwood practically patents the academic variant every six months. But O'Clover steps into the ring with a corporate sheen—swapping rural charm or dusty labs for sleek Chicago agencies and luxury hospitality accounts. 

### The Concrete Jungle Meets the Open Highway

Let us look at the mechanics of the pitch. Mattie French is the scrappy underdog from an independent shop. Campbell Porter is the golden-boy heavyweight from slicker pastures. A classic David versus Goliath dynamic, except Goliath has a smirk and a tailored suit. 

Yet, I find myself squinting at the synopsis with the same skepticism I reserve for a bag of pesticide-laden fertilizer. What are they actually selling on this four-week road trip? The synopsis remains frustratingly vague about the physical journey itself. Are they driving fuel-guzzling SUVs across interstate highways? Stopping at neon-lit motels? O'Clover leaves the geography blank, leaning entirely on the psychological crutch of "hidden wounds and softest parts." 

### The Artifice of the Corporate Pitch

Here is my core critique from the dirt rows: the book treats capitalism's luxury marketing arm as a backdrop for emotional healing. Irony, thy name is modern romance. Mattie wants to beat Campbell to earn a promotion, but the prize is landing a resort account—presumably paving over some pristine coastal ecosystem or cutting down old-growth trees for cabanas. 

Does the narrative care about the ecological absurdity of luxury resorts? Unlikely. O'Clover is interested in the tight, claustrophobic space of a shared itinerary. The forced proximity trope relies on stripping away urban distractions until only the two combatants remain. 

Still, O'Clover possesses sharp prose. If her previous work is any indicator, the dialogue will crackle with enough kinetic energy to power my homestead for a week. I just hope the resolution avoids the tired trope of abandoning one's career ambitions for a country house. Some of us actually like the soil under our fingernails. 

Hold on. Do you smell that? 

Damn it, I left the hickory wood chips too close to the direct flame on the smoker. If I do not go flip those beef briskets right now, our entire Friday night dinner is going to turn into charcoal.
