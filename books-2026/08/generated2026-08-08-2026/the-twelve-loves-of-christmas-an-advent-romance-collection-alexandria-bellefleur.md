**Author:** Alexandria Bellefleur  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-29 APL

This *The Twelve Loves of Christmas* promises to be a trifling exercise in holiday romance, a predictable yet endearing series of short stories that will no doubt tickle the fancy of those who insist on sentimentalizing December.

And yet, I find myself drawn to the collection's forced proximity romance, a trope I've always considered a cheap trick for authors to get their characters in close quarters—what is it about confined spaces that sparks intimacy? But here, it's not just a device; it's a holiday tradition. I envision a cast of characters who've learned to appreciate the warmth of their own company, much like those of Helen Hoang's *The Kiss Quotient*, where sex and intimacy are merely a means of connection.

The inclusion of a second-chance romance between exes stranded at the airport hints at the possibility of *The Twelve Loves of Christmas* being more than just a surface-level holiday collection—perhaps it will touch on the complex, often messy nature of relationships, much like Tessa Bailey's *Fix Her Up*. But we must be cautious not to assume too much from a synopsis that barely scratches the surface.

But then again, is that the point of this book? Is it merely to offer a palate cleanser for those who crave lighthearted holiday reads? The fact that each story is sealed, to be opened one at a time, speaks to our desire for structure, for a clear beginning, middle, and end. It's a format not dissimilar to those *Dear John* letters, where each installment is carefully crafted to elicit a specific reaction.

As I ponder the significance of this format, I'm reminded of the works of Emily Henry, who weaves together intricate, character-driven narratives that often subvert our expectations. But will *The Twelve Loves of Christmas* push the boundaries of the romance genre, or will it play it safe, relying on our nostalgia for holiday tales?

And so, as we await the release of what promises to be a pleasant, if predictable, read, I find myself asking: what is the value of these fleeting, often saccharine stories? Is it merely a distraction from the drudgery of daily life, a temporary reprieve from the monotony of our routines?

I predict that *The Twelve Loves of Christmas* will be a moderate success, appealing to those who crave a quick holiday fix. It will not, however, challenge the reader to question the nature of love or relationships. Here we go again.

The caviar I ordered was far too salty.
