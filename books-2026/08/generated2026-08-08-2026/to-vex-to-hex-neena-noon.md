**Page Count:** Unknown

The Coleman stove is hissing a frantic, blue-rimmed tune while ten feet away, a tetherball pole rattles like a loose tooth in the autumn wind. And I am sitting here on a blue nylon folding chair with three missing leg caps, staring at a printout of Neena Noon’s upcoming 2026 release, *To Vex & To Hex*. My youngest just tried to roast a hot dog wrapper over the embers. Kids are loud. God, they are loud. So excuse me if my mind immediately glommed onto the synopsis's promise of sanctuary, quiet stacks of paper, and an old man named Charles who needs protecting. 

We all look for safe houses. Sometimes they are brick-and-mortar secondhand bookstores. Sometimes they are just a moment of silence between the juice-box distribution and the tent zipper jams. 

### The Architecture of the Shield

Lucy Ansari is a bookseller witch. But look past the magical prose for a second. What is she actually doing? She is erecting a barricade. An estranged grandson—Grayson—arrives from Ireland because the patriarch is hurt. And what does Lucy do? She goes into hyper-vigilant, defensive lockdown. She wants to hex him. Why? Because when the people who anchor our safe spaces get broken or old, we lash out at the new variables. We build moats. 

And Grayson doesn't break down the door with an axe. He uses annoyance. He vexes her. 

It is a classic push-pull dynamic that feels less like a manufactured rom-com trope and more like the defensive posturing humans adopt when they are terrified of being displaced. Or worse—disappointed. Noon is trading on comfort here, leaning heavily into the vibe of Laurie Gilmore’s *The Pumpkin Spice Café* or Sawyer Bennett. Yet I suspect the emotional core relies entirely on whether Noon can make that sudden shift from "protective warden" to "vulnerable human" feel earned rather than rushed. 

### Healing Through Friction

Can we talk about the utility of a good argument? Adults hiding behind snark and "heated banter" are usually just scared kids wearing oversized coats. Yet the synopsis hints at suppression. *Desperate to suppress.* That phrase is doing heavy lifting. 

If a childhood teaches us anything, it is that suppression is just a pressure cooker waiting for a campfire spark. 

We read books like this not for the magic—though book magic sounds delightful when your own reality involves scraping melted marshmallows off a fleece jacket—but for the friction. Two stubborn people orbiting an injured elder, bumping their bruised elbows together until the armor cracks. Will it be deep? Probably not. It is marketed as cozy and high-spice, which means the emotional stakes will likely resolve right before a tidy, predictable sunset. And honestly? I can respect the architecture of a safe landing. 

### The Blueprint of Softness

Let us be real for a second. The synopsis is painfully sparse on actual plot mechanics. It relies entirely on the enemies-to-lovers beats we could write in our sleep. But sometimes, mid-camping trip, you don't want Dostoevsky. You want a warm mug and a predictable trajectory where the prickly exterior yields to a tender touch. 

My ten-year-old just asked if he can eat a marshmallow off the dirt. I need to go handle that before the raccoon population moves in. And then, frankly, I need to go home and water my dying cactus.
