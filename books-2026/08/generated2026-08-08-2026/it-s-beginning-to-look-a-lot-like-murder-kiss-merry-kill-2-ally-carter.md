**Author:** Ally Carter  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-13 APL

Here we sit in the sticky, unrelenting heat of August, yet the literary market insists on dragging us headfirst into forced-proximity holiday cheer. Ally Carter is back at it with *It’s Beginning to Look a Lot Like Murder*. Another festive excursion into the lives of the obscenely wealthy and the working class who dust their mantelpieces. 

### The Feudal Architecture of Romance

Consider the optics. A billionaire’s grandson versus the housekeeper’s daughter. Classic upstairs-downstairs friction wrapped in glossy genre packaging. We are supposed to swoon while devouring a narrative power dynamic built on inherited capital and domestic labor. Yet, Carter utilizes this tired class dichotomy to orchestrate a game of cat-and-mouse that feels remarkably geopolitical. Liam fakes his own demise. Ella takes the fall in the court of public opinion. And suddenly, we are knee-deep in a small-town Cold War where the weapons are rumors and the collateral damage is emotional. 

Do we applaud the subversion? Or do we critique the reliance on a decade-old grudge? Ten years of animosity. A decade of petty grievances between social strata. It reminds one of diplomatic stalemates in the UN Security Council—petty, entrenched, and ultimately absurd when faced with an actual crisis. Namely, a missing body and a creeping snowstorm. 

### Tropes as Cartography

Childhood enemies-to-lovers. Forced proximity. A slow burn that takes its sweet time while a potential murderer lurks in the eggnog. Carter deploys these tropes like territorial markers on a disputed map. The synopsis leans heavily on familiar genre architecture. But vagueness plagues the pitch. Who is the killer? We have no earthly idea. The synopsis guards its secrets like a Swiss bank account, offering only a "bloody spot on the drive" and a storm that traps everyone inside. 

Yet, I suspect this book will dominate the autumn bestseller lists precisely because it weaponizes predictability. Think Lucy Foley meets Emily Henry on an oligarch’s private estate. The tension does not stem from originality. It stems from the friction between two people who know each other's historical vulnerabilities too well. 

### The Geopolitics of Faked Deaths

Why do heirs always fake their own deaths in these books? Is it a scathing critique of late-stage capitalism? Or merely a convenient plot device to force two stubborn fools into a reluctant alliance? Liam uses his own disappearance to hunt his would-be assassin. Ella uses her survival instinct to clear her name. They are both operating covert intelligence operations within the confines of a quaint mountain town. 

And yet, as I sweat through this August afternoon, the prospect of a blizzard feels almost luxurious. Even if it is merely ink on a page. Carter knows how to pace a thriller, balancing the snark of forced proximity with the high-stakes adrenaline of a whodunit. 

One thing remains painfully clear. When the billionaire class falls out, the domestic staff always ends up cleaning the blood off the driveway.
