**Author:** Vikki VanSickle  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-08 APL

I pull espresso shots all morning for people who think oat milk is a personality trait, and honestly, I survive on a steady diet of extreme splatterpunk and cosmic nihilism where everybody dies screaming in the dark. So why am I staring at the press release for Vikki VanSickle’s upcoming *The Mystic and the Missing Girl* with my jaw literally unhinged? Read the room, please. This is supposed to be a cozy little lakeside mystery about tarot cards and small-town grudges. Yet I am sitting here, utterly paralyzed by the sheer, unadulterated dread of a protagonist whose mother disapproves of her. Forget ghosts. Forget cold cases. The real horror is familial disappointment and past-due utility bills. 

### The Aesthetics of Small-Town Suffocation

VanSickle sets up a quaint postcard nightmare. A picturesque town. A metaphysical shop. An astrology-obsessed employee who probably cleans crystals with sage and good vibes. But underneath the cute pastel aesthetic lies a psychological pressure cooker. And I respect that. Twenty years of cold-case baggage dumped right into the lap of a reluctant psychic who just wants to sell quartz and dodge a local rake named Foster Banks? It has all the ingredients of a Stephen King minor-key nightmare—minus the rabid dogs and interdimensional spiders, unfortunately. 

### The Weaponization of Nostalgia

Let us talk about the narrative mechanics here. The synopsis leans hard on the sins of the past. Hannah Mystic—what a gloriously tragic moniker—taking the fall for a local disappearance two decades ago. That is not just a plot point. That is generational scapegoating. Drew Benson is trapped in the amber of her dead aunt’s ruined reputation. Every tarot reading and object-reading psychometry session is a forced excavation of trauma she abandoned in high school. But now an arch-nemesis turned big-city reporter is rolling into town to dig up the bones. Literally. 

I am usually allergic to cozy mysteries. They lack the sweet, sticky copper scent of genuine human ruin. Yet VanSickle’s premise feels less like a cozy tea party and more like a slow, agonizing tightening of a noose made of hemp and locally sourced incense. Think Shirley Jackson meets an episode of *True Detective* written by someone who actually knows how to run a small business. The financial ruin angle—bills piling up, a local vandal hating the witchy storefront—gives it grit. It grounds the supernatural nonsense in the bleak, crushing reality of modern capitalism. 

Will the cold case actually expose the whole town? The synopsis promises it. And I am weirdly, sickeningly invested in seeing how many local pillars of the community get dragged down into the mud. Give me ruined reputations. Give me exposed hypocrisy. Give me the total collapse of lakeside tranquility. 

Anyway, I would write another paragraph, but the blinding afternoon sun is hitting my POS terminal at an ungodly angle and the glare is currently burning a hole straight through my retina.
