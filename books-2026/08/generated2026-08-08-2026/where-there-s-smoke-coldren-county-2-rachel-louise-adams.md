**Author:** Rachel Louise Adams  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

Fifteen minutes. That is the exact window I have before the fluorescent lights of this godforsaken newsroom flicker back to life and demand more of my soul. Yet here I am, eyes burning, dissecting Rachel Louise Adams and her upcoming *Where There’s Smoke*. 

Let us be brutally honest about the synopsis we have been handed for this Coldren County sequel. It reads like a checklist designed by a focus group trying to capture the modern procedural market. Dolores Hawthorne is divorced. She left the pathology lab for a medical examiner gig. She relocated to small-town Wisconsin—because apparently, women seeking a psychological reset are legally required to move closer to dense forests and cold lakes. 

### The Geography of Female Burnout

And what does she find in her pastoral Wisconsin exile? A dead man named Benedict Marcy. Affectionately called "Mr. Mercy." Oh, the irony. It drips from the page like cheap grease from Marcy’s Meats. 

Adams is leaning hard into the tropes that made *No Rest for the Wicked* a bestseller. But let us look at the underlying structural subtext here. Dolores has shrugged off the marital dead weight. She has recalibrated her career. And yet, the instant a high-profile corpse drops into her jurisdiction, her FBI friends swoop in to validate her worth. Why must female competence always be co-signed by federal law enforcement? Can a woman just dissect a slaughterhouse victim in peace without the alphabet agencies turning it into a geopolitical pissing match? 

### The Predictable Machinery of Suspense

The synopsis warns us—and trust me, Don't say I didn't warn you—that someone is going to go to extreme lengths to hide the truth. Groundbreaking. Truly, a revelation in the genre of rural noir. We are looking at a narrative framework that feels entirely beholden to the ghosts of Karin Slaughter and Tana French, yet lacks the psychological claustrophobia that makes their work actually sting. 

The prose of the blurb is maddeningly vague. "Full of contradictions and surprises." What does that even mean? Is the slaughterhouse a metaphor for late-stage capitalism, or are we just getting another gritty run-of-the-mill body count with a side of folksy regional charm? The synopsis hides behind plot mechanics because it refuses to commit to Dolores's interiority. Divorce and a geographic pivot do not automatically equal character growth. Sometimes a relocation is just a relocation, and a dead meat-mogul is just a lazy inciting incident. 

If Adams wants to salvage this series from the abyss of mid-list procedural fatigue, she needs to let Dolores be genuinely messy. Not "quirky small-town divorcee" messy, but feral. Unapologetic. Stop wrapping her trauma in the tidy bow of an FBI investigation. 

My heart is literally vibrating against my ribs right now. I am surviving entirely on two hours of fragmented sleep and a lukewarm, chemically hazardous berry-flavored energy drink that tastes like battery acid and crushed aspirations.
