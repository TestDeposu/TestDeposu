**Author:** Jim Butcher  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

My knees are jammed against the sticky plastic seat of the Greyhound while the engine rattles like a dying washing machine, and yet, somehow, my primary concern is whether Butcher actually wrote this garbage or if an overworked marketing intern at Penguin Random House fed a bunch of millennial buzzwords into an LLM. Let us be entirely serious here. The official copy for *Viral Hex*—floating somewhere out there in the ether as the supposed nineteenth entry of *The Dresden Files*—reads like a desperate executive trying to explain TikTok to a board of directors who still use dial-up. 

### The Algorithmic Panopticon

And what a magnificent trainwreck of high-concept desperation it is. Look at the mechanics. A broke kid accidentally casting spells with discount ramen? It is practically dripping with the kind of forced contemporary relevance that makes true editors want to throw their red pens out the emergency exit. Yet, beneath the cringe-inducing invocation of "memes" and "followers," there is a genuinely ruthless mechanism at play. Attention economy as literal thaumaturgy. 

Notice how the synopsis leans hard into the transactional nature of modern existence. In older, dirtier mythologies, you sold your soul to a horned proprietor for gold or influence. Now? You trade it incrementally, one heart icon and algorithmic boost at a time, until your corporeal form rots from digital exposure. That is not just urban fantasy. That is late-stage capitalism stripped of its pleasantries. 

### When Intellectual Property Loses the Plot

Let us address the elephant in the bus aisle. This is supposedly a Dresden book, yet Harry Dresden is completely absent from the text provided, replaced instead by a broke college student named Reed and a snarky digital demon named Zyx. Is this an off-shoot? An editorial misattribution? A cosmic prank? Who knows. But the thematic DNA is transparent. It borrows heavily from the ironic detachment of Ben Aaronovitch’s *Rivers of London* and mashes it into the cynical, screen-addicted nightmare-scape of Charlie Brooker’s *Black Mirror*. 

The subtext here is violently blunt. Engagement metrics dictate magical potency. If your reach is broad enough, you rewrite reality. But art—even trashy, viral art—requires a victim. The soul rot eating away at Reed's body is a neat little metaphor for the hollowed-out affect of every chronically online influencer currently dancing in front of a ring light for rent money. 

### A Bold Bet on the Slush Pile

My prediction? This thing is going to sell half a million copies to exhausted twenty-somethings who feel entirely seen by a book about demonic discount ramen, while purists froth at the mouth over the tonal shift. It is garish, it is loud, and it smells faintly of desperation. 

Still, it beats looking out the frosted window at another stretch of desolate highway. My landlord in Chicago is threatening to evict me by Friday if I do not wire three months of back rent, and here I am, dissecting promotional copy on a moving vehicle for pennies, wondering why I ever majored in English instead of computer science.
