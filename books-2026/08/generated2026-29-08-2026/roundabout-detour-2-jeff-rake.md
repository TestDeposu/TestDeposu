**Author:** Jeff Rake  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The rain is pooling right along the northern fence line again, threatening to wash out the newly planted winter kale before the taproots even stand a chance against the clay. Yet I am supposed to care about six stranded astronauts leaping across parallel versions of a dirt ball they apparently ruined to begin with. Jeff Rake is bringing us *Roundabout*, the second installment of his *Detour* series, and the premise reads like someone threw a sci-fi blender at a corporate board meeting. A power-hungry billionaire? Branding the spacefarers as traitors because they accidentally landed on the wrong planetary twin? Groundbreaking stuff. Truly. We have never, not once, encountered the trope of the mustache-twirling billionaire industrialist exploiting catastrophic systemic failure for private gain while the actual laborers of the cosmos scramble for scraps in the mud. It feels less like an ambitious speculative thriller and more like watching a tractor rust in real-time, heavy and predictable under a gray sky. 

And don’t even get me started on the absolute disaster that was last season’s third-grade school board budget meeting where Mrs. Gable tried to convince us that composting cafeteria napkins would somehow solve the district's structural deficit, completely ignoring the fact that the actual roof leaks every time the wind blows out of the east—much like how this synopsis glosses over actual planetary physics to shove a nationwide manhunt into a narrative that should be meditating on the sheer, crushing loneliness of deep space.

Atmosphere? Ignored. Ecological consequence? Merely a backdrop for a high-speed chase involving guys in jumpsuits and a tech mogul with too much venture capital. 

Uninspired.

The synopsis leans heavily on the safety net of mass-market panic—unexplained phenomena happening all over the globe, because nothing says high art quite like an amorphous green-screen catastrophe threatening both Earth prime and Earth backup. It lacks the quiet devastation of Kim Stanley Robinson or the sharp, biting social commentary of early Margaret Atwood. Instead, we get a breathless sprint from two captured crew members to a desperate race to save two worlds at once, as if saving one is too pedestrian for a creator whose previous credits include airplane mysteries and high-concept warehouses. I can already smell the stale popcorn and see the Netflix adaptation greenlit before the ink on the manuscript dries. 

Now, if you will excuse me, I have to go out into the drizzle and assemble a profoundly confusing piece of Swedish furniture that currently consists of thirty-two identical dowels and a hex wrench made of cheese-grade metal.
