Oh, *Christ*, here we go again—another Christmas novel synopsis that’s basically a Wikipedia page for *holiday literature* with a side of existential dread. The e-artnow blurb reads like a drunken librarian’s dream list: *The Gift of the Magi* next to *The Little Match Girl*, *A Christmas Carol* (Dickens, not Coleridge) sandwiched between *Mother Holle*—which, by the way, is a fairy tale, not a rom-com. And yet, here we are, pretending this is some kind of *Willow Branch Mountain* exclusive when it’s just a repackaged anthology of classics with a dusting of snowflake sentimentality.

But fine. Let’s play along. The synopsis doesn’t actually say *what* *Take My Hand* is—just that it’s part of a series, which, given the *Willow Branch Mountain* brand, probably means another small-town Christmas romance where the heroine is either a baker, a librarian, or a woman who “just happens” to run the local inn. The thematic baiting here is *deliberate*—Christmas as a metaphor for love, sacrifice, and the kind of clichéd redemption that makes you want to unplug your Wi-Fi and live in a cabin with no cell service. The fact that they’re listing *The Nutcracker and the Mouse King* alongside *The Little Match Girl* suggests they’re banking on the same audience that thinks *Frozen* is deep literature.

The real question is: *Why?* Why would Brooke Montgomery—who, let’s be honest, has a knack for weaving cozy, emotionally resonant stories—suddenly lean into this *literary Christmas buffet* approach? Is this a marketing stunt? A desperate bid for algorithmic traction? Or is she genuinely trying to prove she can write a *holiday epic* that doesn’t feel like it was ghostwritten by a committee of Hallmark executives? The synopsis doesn’t tell us. It just drops us into a void of *potential*—the same void that makes me want to scream when a book’s blurb reads like a grocery list of influences.

But here’s the thing: *Willow Branch Mountain* has a reputation for *subtle* emotional beats, for the kind of character-driven storytelling that doesn’t rely on *The Twelve Days of Christmas* as its emotional core. So if this book is actually going to deliver, it’ll have to do something unexpected—maybe a twist on the *magical realism* angle, or a protagonist who’s not just *waiting* for Christmas to fix their life. Because right now, the synopsis reads like a *Christmas movie* script from the 1980s, not a novel. And that’s not a knock on Christmas movies—it’s a knock on the fact that this feels like it was written by someone who’s never actually *lived* through a holiday season without a single cliché.

The most interesting part? The absence of *A Christmas Carol* (Dickens) in the list—only Coleridge’s version is included, which is *already* a niche choice. This suggests the book might be playing with *ghost stories* or *haunted holiday tropes*, but again, the synopsis doesn’t say. It’s all *implied*, which is frustrating because I’d rather have a clear direction than a *mood board* with no narrative.

And let’s not forget the *meta* here: the fact that this synopsis is *literally* a list of other books means the book itself is probably going to be *about* books, or at least *feel* like a bookish homage. Which, if done right, could be *brilliant*—think *Little Women* meets *The Christmas Carol* with a dash of *The Night Circus*’s atmospheric tension. But if it’s just *another* small-town Christmas romance where the heroine finds love under the mistletoe, then we’re all just waiting for the inevitable *snow globe* moment where the camera zooms out and the narrator sighs, *“And that’s how Christmas saved them.”*
