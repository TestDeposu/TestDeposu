**Author:** Ruby Dixon  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The air smells like ozone and old books—someone’s left a window cracked in the study, and the heat of August presses against the glass like a lover who won’t take no for an answer. *Villain Origin Story* arrives with the kind of synopsis that reads like a fever dream scribbled in the margins of a *Game of Thrones* fanfic draft, the kind where the author clearly thinks "dark fantasy" is just "more blood, less forethought." Joslen, the "poor village girl," is the kind of protagonist who exists solely to be a vessel for the author’s unchecked fantasies about power dynamics—because nothing says "literary depth" like a girl with a "crooked back" (a detail that, let’s be honest, is just a way to imply she’s "cursed" without actually committing to the worldbuilding).

The synopsis leans *hard* into the "villain mentor trope," but not in the way of something like *The Lies of Locke Lamora*—where the mentor’s cruelty is a tool, a game, a *performance*—no, this is the kind of dynamic that feels like it was lifted straight from a *Vampire: The Masquerade* roleplaying session where someone decided to write it as a novel. Vilos Saumien, the "Grim Lord," is the kind of character who would’ve been hilariously over-the-top in a *League of Legends* lore dump, but here, he’s just a placeholder for whatever the author thinks "evil" looks like: unhinged, seductive, and—most importantly—*entertaining*. The fact that his "rules" include "let him seduce her" isn’t just a plot device; it’s a *tell*. This isn’t a story about power or transformation. This is a story about the author’s personal kink for "dark romance" fantasy, where the real villain isn’t Vilos—it’s the synopsis itself, which treats Joslen’s agency like a checklist: check, poor; check, cursed; check, wants revenge; check, gets seduced by a demon lord.

And yet—here’s the thing—there’s a *glimmer* of something interesting buried under the mess. The synopsis hints at a world where Joslen’s frustration isn’t just a trope but a *force*, something that could be sharpened into something like the rage of *The Poppy War*’s Rin, or the quiet fury of *The Priory of the Orange Tree*’s Sabran. But instead of leaning into that, the author defaults to the easiest fantasy: the "poor girl gets lucky" arc, where the real magic isn’t in Joslen’s growth but in Vilos’s *entertainment value*. It’s the kind of story that would make *Bridgerton*’s Lady Whistledown roll her eyes—because this isn’t seduction, it’s *exploitation*, and the synopsis doesn’t even have the decency to pretend it’s anything else.

The real crime here isn’t the lack of originality—it’s the lack of *ambition*. Where’s the political intrigue? Where’s the moral ambiguity? Where’s the *stakes* beyond "will she be a good villain or not"? This feels like a *Shadow and Bone* fanfiction where the author decided to add "more demon sex" instead of actually thinking about what makes a villain compelling. And don’t even get me started on the "poisonous court" trope—because nothing says "original" like a world where the only thing standing between Joslen and her destiny is a bunch of nobles who exist solely to be bitchy.

But fine. Let’s pretend this is a *game*. Because if *Villain Origin Story* is going to be the next big thing, it’ll have to outlast the kind of fantasy that gets buried under the weight of its own clichés. And right now? It doesn’t even have the charm of a *Wicked Lovely* novel—because at least those had the decency to make their villains *funny*.

I need to go scrub oatmeal off the antique desk. Again.
