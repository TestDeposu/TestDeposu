**Page Count:** Unknown

The sun bleeds into the city’s skyline like a half-melted candle, the kind you’d find in some Parisian apothecary—expensive, unnecessary, but impossible to resist. The air smells of grilled octopus and the faintest hint of ozone, the kind that clings to your skin after a storm that never quite arrives. Over here, on this rooftop where the wind still carries the weight of summer’s last stubborn heat, I’m trying to decide whether *The Isle of Lonely Monsters* is the kind of book that belongs in a leather-bound first-edition collection or tucked into a beach bag for that one week in August when you want to pretend you’re not an adult.

Meixin’s dilemma—this quiet, gnawing tension between the hunter and the hidden—is the kind of emotional core that makes you wonder if Julie Leong has been secretly reading the private journals of *The Night Circus*’s own romantic entanglements between the performer and the magician. But where Kelly Link’s work thrives on the uncanny and the surreal, Leong’s strength lies in the way she makes the mundane monstrous. The synopsis doesn’t reveal much beyond the surface—just enough to know this is a story about secrets and the cost of hiding, about the way a person can become both the predator and the prey in their own narrative. Yet the real question isn’t whether the dragon exists or whether Faran’s sorcery is genuine—it’s whether Meixin will ever stop running.

And that’s where the synopsis stumbles. It teases the political subtext—*the Crownless Kingdom*, after all, sounds like a world where the crown is just another kind of cage—but it doesn’t say a damn thing about what happens when the villagers realize their monster hunter is also a monster. Is this a *The Bear and the Nightingale* moment, where the line between savior and villain is drawn in blood? Or is it something grittier, closer to *The Priory of the Orange Tree*, where the cost of belonging is measured in lives? The synopsis leaves it all too vague, which is either a sign of restraint or a missed opportunity. Leong has done this kind of slow-burn character study before—see *The Keeper of Magical Things*—so why not lean harder into the tension? Why not make us *feel* the weight of Meixin’s choices, instead of just nodding along as she makes them?

But then—*and this is the pivot*—maybe that’s the point. Maybe the beauty of this story isn’t in the grand revelations but in the quiet moments, the way a character like Meixin could exist in a world that’s already written her as a villain. There’s something deeply satisfying about a protagonist who doesn’t need to be redeemed, who just needs to be *seen*. That’s the kind of emotional labor that makes you think of *The House in the Cerulean Sea*, where the monsters aren’t the problem—they’re the heart. Yet Leong’s work has always had a sharper edge, a darker undercurrent. If this book follows that trajectory, then Faran isn’t just a love interest; he’s a mirror, reflecting back the parts of Meixin she’s spent a lifetime hiding.

The synopsis doesn’t name the stakes, but it *does* name the ache. And that ache—it’s the kind that lingers after you’ve closed the book, the kind that makes you wonder if you’ve just read something that will sit on your shelf like a well-loved first edition of *The Shadow of the Wind*. But then again, maybe it’ll just end up in a box in the attic, gathering dust because you couldn’t quite decide if it was worth the price. The real question is whether Leong can make the reader care enough to keep it out.
