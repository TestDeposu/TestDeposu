**Author:** Evan Leikam  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Mud caked beneath my fingernails after clearing the irrigation ditch, I stare at the freshly printed publisher's blurb for *Anji in Shadow* and wonder why contemporary fantasy insists on treating political collapse as a ledger of cash transactions where dead monarchs and jingling coins somehow equate to actual systemic change, completely ignoring the fundamental material reality that no amount of stolen gold or tavern-brawling revenge can ever replace a slaughtered ecosystem, fix poisoned topsoil, or heal the rotting foundational roots of a society that has spent generations devouring its own commons. 

Spare me.

Because according to the promotional text for Evan Leikam’s upcoming sequel to *Anji Kills a King*, we are supposed to believe that tossing a sack of blood-money into a muddy camp on some riverbank will magically jump-start a glorious worker's revolution, as if structural oppression is just a poorly maintained tractor waiting for a sudden influx of spare change rather than a deep, systemic rot requiring actual, exhausting, generational cultivation of the land and community. 

Give me a break.

We get it—the king is rotting in the dirt, the edgy mercenaries are conveniently butchered to clear the narrative chessboard, and our protagonist is left hoarding her pocket change in a soot-stained metropolis of dark alleys and desperate cliches that smells entirely too much of Joe Abercrombie fan fiction crossed with the cynical posturing of modern grimdark trends. 

Trite.

Real power doesn't grow from a severed throat or a mercenary's purse. It grows from the stubborn persistence of clover breaking through asphalt, from composting the debris of the old, corrupt guard instead of recycling their tired tropes into yet another sprawling trilogy about gloomy assassins stumbling through the dark. Leikam clearly wants us to marvel at the grit of it all—the constant surviving of a dozen different deaths, the edgy nihilism of a character who treats mass slaughter like a minor inconvenience—while completely missing the irony that a revolution funded by stolen assassination money is just a lateral move for the ruling class. 

Preposterous.

I have seventy tomato plants currently fighting a losing battle against late blight out in the north bed, and I guarantee there is more authentic tension, desperate struggle, and genuine systemic collapse happening in that single patch of damp earth than in the entirety of this upcoming urban wasteland where nobody ever stops to consider where their grain comes from or who cleans the blood off the cobblestones. 

Barnaby is stuck in the branches of the ancient white oak by the chicken coop again.
