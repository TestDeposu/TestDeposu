**Author:** João Guimarães Rosa  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

There is something profoundly amusing about watching the literary establishment rediscover João Guimarães Rosa every few decades as if he were a newly unearthed artifact from some undiscovered literary continent. And yet, Alison Entrekin taking another swing at translating *Vastlands: The Crossing* feels less like a standard reissue and more like an absolute cultural necessity. Colm Tóibín provides the obligatory framing, though Mario Vargas Llosa’s old blurb still does the heaviest lifting—calling it a linguistic tour de force. Naturally.

### The Dust and the Devil

Set entirely within the lawless expanse of the Brazilian *sertão*, the narrative apparently unfolds as one massive, relentless monologue by Riobaldo, a reformed *jagunço* who traded youthful wandering for vigilante chief status. Or perhaps he just traded it for an existential panic attack about the devil. One must appreciate a good Faustian bargain delivered straight through the teeth of a horseback war. But wait, I completely take back my earlier assessment of the monologue structure—it is not merely a stylistic choice, but rather a suffocating, brilliant cage designed to trap the reader right alongside Riobaldo’s moral decay. The synopsis hints at an epic of high plateaus and a tender love story wrapped in blood. And honestly? The prose is so dense with verbal acrobatics that attempting to parse the metaphysics while checking my crypto portfolio feels like a delightfully unhinged way to spend a Tuesday afternoon.

### The Geometry of Longing

At the cold, beating heart of this text lies a profound obsession with an enigmatic figure named Diadorim. A love story forged in the fires of vengeance and territorial violence. Yet, one must wonder how much of this mystical territory—haunted by the living and the dead alike—will actually translate across linguistic divides without losing its teeth. Entrekin is attempting the impossible here, wrestling with a text that has historically terrified English-language publishers. It gives me major *Moby-Dick* meets *One Hundred Years of Solitude*, but written by someone who dropped out of Coimbra to do shrooms in the desert. Vibes. Immaculate, terrifying vibes. 

Power always demands a blood tax. Riobaldo knows it. The feuding warlords know it. We all know it, sitting here in our climate-controlled dens sipping aged bourbon while reading about high-stakes frontier justice. 

I really must step away from my espresso now because someone left a calculus textbook on the marble kitchen island, and trying to explain derivatives to an anxious teenager when my own brain is halfway through a baroque Brazilian monologue is frankly a sick joke.
