Sweat is permanently glued to the back of my neck right now, thanks to the oppressive humidity radiating off the asphalt outside while the community center's sole window unit rattles on its last legs. People keep dropping by with clipboard complaints about municipal zoning laws and park funding cuts, as if I have a magic wand to fix the concrete heat islands we are forced to inhabit in August. Yet here I am, staring at a press release for *Hamlet & Juliet* by Clare Edge, wondering why modern publishing insists on treating classical literature like a messy closet of props to be rummaged through for a quick serotonin hit. 

### The Mashup Industrial Complex

We get it. Dead playwrights cannot sue for copyright infringement. But mashing up star-crossed teenagers from Verona with the brooding prince of Denmark feels less like an artistic statement and more like fanfiction written by an algorithm that ingested thirty high school English syllabi in one sitting. Edge drops Juliet into Castle Elsinore because apparently, geographical continuity is for cowards. And honestly, I am so exhausted by this exact creative crutch. It reminds me of the time the neighborhood association tried to merge our annual block party with the stray cat rescue drive—chaos ensued, nobody got a hot dog, and three people left in tears. Except instead of bewildered neighbors, we get Bottom the Weaver and Cordelia tossed into a supernatural blender. 

### Bureaucracy and Malevolent Magic

The synopsis promises a grand conspiracy threatening "the entire Globe"—pun entirely intended, I assume, because subtlety left the building years ago. We are supposed to care about malevolent magic taking root across the Continent. But what does that even mean in structural terms? Are we talking about a systemic failure of infrastructure, or just a ghost with a grievance? Hamlet wants to sniff out a villain in the royal family, which is roughly as original as discovering that a local zoning board meeting will run twenty minutes over schedule. Give me actual systemic critique instead of another magical monarchy falling apart because of daddy issues. 

### The Slow Burn Trap

Bar Fridman-Tell calls it a "gloriously slow-burn romantic fantasy," which usually translates to four hundred pages of people staring intensely across crowded rooms while a vague threat looms in the background like an uncollected pile of recycling on a Tuesday morning. I refuse to see the appeal. Give me direct conflict, or give me nothing at all. Edge is leaning hard into the irreverent twist angle, echoing the same tired playbook we saw when every YA author decided to rewrite Jane Austen with zombies or fae courts. 

Now if you will excuse me, I have to go convince a four-year-old that submerging his sticky limbs in tepid water is not a brutal human rights violation.
