Listen to me, you ungrateful whelps, stop slouching over those glowing rectangles and pay attention. My own grandchildren have the attention span of a drowning gnat, entirely obsessed with flashing lights and hollow noise instead of a proper, sweeping narrative built on blood, hubris, and immortal vengeance. Now they hand me *27 Stars* and expect me to nod along like some senile fool thrilled by a number that belongs on a jersey rather than a gravestone. James Patterson is at it again, marching out his exhausted *Women's Murder Club* lineup for another round of San Francisco high-society butchery. 

### The Geography of Aristocratic Rot

Look at this premise. A basketball star in a luxury tower. Two girls on a pristine private campus. A bartender outside a trendy club. How terribly original. We are supposed to gasp at the juxtaposition of elite privilege and brutal violence, as if Homer didn't invent the entire concept of the mighty falling from high places millennia ago. But no, modern readers need a sanitized checklist of wealthy zip codes to feel alive. And honestly, speaking of lazy recycling, my grandson spends twelve hours a day watching blue hedgehogs run in circles collecting shiny rings while completely ignoring the tragic irony of Sisyphus, which brings me right back to this twenty-seventh installment of industrial-grade crime fiction. Why twenty-seven? Why not fifty? Why stop when the horse has not only died, but decomposed into mineral dust? Wait, scratch that entirely. Numbers do not matter in these franchises; bodies are merely interchangeable props designed to be swapped out before the next quarterly royalty check clears.

### The Illusion of Collective Genius

Sharing information is how they survive. That is the marketing pitch. A tight-knit sisterhood of professionals combining forensic brilliance and intuition to conquer chaos. Yet, the synopsis relies on the oldest, laziest trope in the thriller trade: *If only the dead could talk…* Oh, please. If the dead could talk, they would probably complain about how poorly sketched their character arcs are. James Patterson writes less like a novelist and more like a factory foreman assembling flat-pack furniture with half the screws missing. Michael Connelly understands how to build a city’s rotting soul on the page, whereas this factory line churns out airport paperbacks designed to be forgotten before the plane even lands. 

### The Inevitable Carnage

Multiple victims. Too many suspects to count. A mathematical equation for boredom. Patterson will undoubtedly serve up a brisk, three-page chapter structure that treats high-stakes murder with the intellectual depth of a cereal commercial. Yet here we are. Waiting for the machinery to grind forward. The sheer existential dread of staring at a blinking cursor while my descendants demand I explain the nuances of modern procedural garbage is frankly too much to bear.
