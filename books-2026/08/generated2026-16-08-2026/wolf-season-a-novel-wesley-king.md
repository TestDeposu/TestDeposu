Flour is currently coating every single horizontal surface in this kitchen because the children demanded cinnamon rolls in the oppressive heat of August, which makes zero logical sense unless you enjoy sweating directly into yeast dough. Yet here I am, scraping dried batter off the counter while staring at the synopsis for Wesley King’s *Wolf Season*. 

### The Illusion of the Prodigal Return

Every single publisher in New York insists on recycling this exact same tired trope. The prodigal son comes home. The sheriff father is missing. The small Montana town hides dark secrets. It is entirely uninspired laziness masquerading as suspense. Why do we pretend that hiding bodies in the woods constitutes high art? People act like rural isolation automatically breeds supernatural phenomena and generational curses, but half the time it is just poor plumbing and repressed emotions. Give me a locked-room drawing-room puzzle with three eccentric spinsters and a missing teacup any day over another brooding man staring pensively at a pine tree.

The flour has now somehow migrated onto my favorite cookbook, which is fantastic. Speaking of messes, I am still seething over that garbage recommendation last month where the detective miraculously solved the locked-vault murder by remembering a childhood sunburn. Absolute narrative insolvency. It made me want to throw my mixing bowl straight through the drywall. Honestly, why are we rewarding authors who cannot construct a logical timeline without relying on ghost sightings or spectral wolves? 

### The Romance Trap

And naturally, we get the mandatory former flame. Because an investigation into a missing father obviously cannot proceed without longing glances over cracked floorboards and unresolved sexual tension with Bernadine Armel. Call me cynical—and heaven knows my spouse would agree with that diagnosis—but nobody solves a homicide while simultaneously rekindling a teenage romance in the middle of a remote wilderness. It simply strains credulity. Real love involves arguing over who forgot to empty the dishwasher, not whispering about shadowy female clans guarding prime acreage under the pale Montana moon. 

Expect this book to wrap up with a neat little bow that explains away the supernatural elements as mere human malice, or worse, leave them frustratingly ambiguous because the writer painted themselves into a corner. I refuse to be fooled. Happy endings are just unresolved plot points wrapped in marketing. 

Wait. That loud crashing sound from the playroom demands an immediate investigation before someone permanently glues the family cat to the rug.
