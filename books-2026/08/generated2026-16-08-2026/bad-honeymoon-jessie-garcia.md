Sitting here folding another tiny pair of socks while the news drones on about another missing person, another tragedy reduced to a soundbite, I keep thinking about how easily a human life becomes content. Jessie Garcia is giving us *Bad Honeymoon* in 2026, and based strictly on the premise, it feels like a sharp, cynical mirror held up to our desperate need for a ratings bump and a good scare before bedtime, echoing the brisk, commercial paranoia of Freida McFadden or Mary Kubica, though I honestly suspect Garcia's multi-POV framing will rely too heavily on withholding simple timeline facts just to trick us. 

And yet, looking at Becca Larson packing her bags for Florida to exploit a stranger's ruined vacation, I find myself strangely exhausted by the sheer machinery of modern storytelling where every single marriage must strain under doubt and every career must unravel because of a missing source. 

Actually, wait. 

Maybe the marriage isn't the point at all. Maybe Garcia is trying to say something much darker about the way grief and tragedy are commodified by people who claim to be looking for the truth, even if I complained a second ago about cheap narrative tricks. 

The synopsis tells us the story starts with a compelling, disturbing interview and ends with a corpse and a vanishing act, which sounds less like a deeply moving exploration of human loss and more like a slick, disposable beach read that forgot how to feel like anything real. 

### The Currency of Despair

We consume horror and sudden death like candy because we are desperately trying to inoculate ourselves against the quiet terror of losing someone we love, hoping that if we watch enough fictional reporters get framed for enough fictional murders, we might somehow understand how a life can vanish into thin air between one phone call and the next.
