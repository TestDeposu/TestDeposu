Look at this red clay caked onto my boots. It is three days past the equinox, the soil is still too stubbornly sodden to turn with a spade, and I am supposed to sit here parsing promotional blurbs about Eleventh-century demons. People think working the earth is a romantic exercise in mindfulness. It is actually just a relentless war against mud, bureaucratic water restrictions, and crows that have somehow evolved an immunity to every scarecrow design invented since the Bronze Age. 

And then a publisher sends over a synopsis for *Bones of Jade, Flesh Like Ice*. 

### The Transactional Cost of Soil and Soul

Gracie Marsden wants us to look at an eleventh-century Kangzhu where youth is bartered away to shapeshifting entities like sacks of grain. A girl named Yu’er gets sold off because a demon promised her parents unearned wealth. Naturally. Because nothing says domestic stability quite like economic mobility founded on supernatural extortion. 

Shelley Parker-Chan compares the construction to a finger trap. A clever blurb. But let us be entirely honest about what a finger trap actually requires. You pull away in a panic, and the weave tightens. You submit, and you are stuck. Or maybe the real tragedy here isn't the demon bargain at all. Maybe the tragedy is the sheer predictability of humans trading love for autonomy the second the floorboards start rotting. We pretend we are different from our medieval ancestors just because our exploitation comes with a corporate retirement plan.

Actually, scratch that entire line of reasoning. The demon bargains are obviously just clever metaphors for predatory lending practices in contemporary housing markets. No, wait. That is completely absurd and pretentious. It is a historical fantasy with mythical beasts, not a seminar on municipal zoning laws. I am just projecting my sheer exhaustion with the local county council denying my permit for a rainwater catchment tank onto a book about dynastic court politics. 

Speaking of infuriating things, has anyone forced themselves to read the latest climate-apocalypse YA trilogy currently dominating every bestseller list? It is four hundred pages of privileged teenagers complaining about melting glaciers while sipping synthetic oat lattes in a glass-domed biodome, completely ignoring the fundamental physics of soil microbiome collapse. Every single page reads like a McKinsey report written by a depressed seventeen-year-old who just discovered Marx on TikTok. At least Marsden has the decency to set her devastation in a historical past where people actually had to bleed for their firewood, instead of just crying into an iPad about carbon footprints. 

### The Royal Court and the Rot

Yu’er leaves her husband, leaves her home, and trades her domestic sanctuary for absolute power in the royal court. A classic literary trajectory. Power always demands the shedding of one's softer edges—the bones of jade, the flesh like ice. It is a striking aesthetic paradox. Jade is brittle if struck incorrectly. Ice melts the moment the temperature shifts. 

Expect this book to sell a million copies to people who have never had to shovel muck in the freezing rain at dawn. They will read about Yu’er sacrificing her humanity for agency, sigh deeply into their designer throw pillows, and order takeout. 

The draft coming through the ill-fitted seal on the north window is currently slicing straight through my flannel shirt like a rusty saw blade, and I am entirely done pretending this wind is going to drop before sundown.
