The anticipation is almost palpable as we await the return of Candice Carty-Williams's iconic character, Queenie Jenkins, in her highly anticipated sequel, *Queenie Is Working on It*. While the original *Queenie* has been adapted into a television series on Hulu, the allure of Carty-Williams's prose is undeniable—especially for those who have fallen under the spell of authors like Zadie Smith and Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie.

As I sit in quiet contemplation, surrounded by the warmth of a late summer evening, I find myself drawn into the world of this beloved and flawed protagonist. Queenie, at thirty-three, is the editorial director of The Good Sis, a media network championing the voices of Black women—a stark contrast to her toxic past. And yet, as she confronts the possibility of motherhood, her own desires and uncertainties come to the forefront. 

What I find intriguing is the way Carty-Williams weaves together the themes of time, identity, and purpose. Queenie's struggles to define herself are all too relatable in today's fast-paced landscape where careers, relationships, and societal expectations converge to confound us. Yet, as I ponder the synopsis, I'm struck by the opacity of Carty-Williams's narrative trajectory. The plot threads seem tantalizingly thin, leaving me wanting—much like Queenie, who finds herself torn between love, family, and her own autonomy.

But this is where the true brilliance lies: in the messy, imperfect, and unapologetic portrayal of Queenie's inner world. Carty-Williams has a remarkable talent for capturing the nuances of her character's emotions, which are raw, authentic, and all the more captivating for their vulnerability. I'm reminded of the works of authors like Angela Flournoy and Akwaeke Emezi, who have skillfully probed the depths of the human experience.

Now, I must confess—having read only the synopsis, I'm left with a nagging sense of uncertainty. Will Carty-Williams successfully navigate the complexities of Queenie's life without sacrificing the character's agency? Or will she succumb to the pitfalls of predictable storytelling? These are questions I'm eager to have answered.

One thing is certain: as a literary community, we're witnessing a seismic shift in the way Black women are being represented in literature, and Carty-Williams is at the forefront of this movement. Her unflinching dedication to exploring the intricacies of identity and the Black female experience is both a testament to her craft and a beacon of hope for readers seeking more nuanced portrayals.

Literally. My mind is racing with the possibilities.

In *Queenie Is Working on It*, Carty-Williams seems poised to deliver a sequel that is both a love letter to her character and a scathing critique of the societal pressures that constrain us. As I await the release on September 8th, I'm left wondering: will Queenie finally find her footing in this chaotic, beautiful, and utterly confounding journey called life?
