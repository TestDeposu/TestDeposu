### The Architecture of Avoidance

Libi Newman refuses to date because Relationship OCD makes intimacy feel like a minefield. So she hooks up instead. Brilliant logic, really. Avoid the vulnerability of a Tuesday night dinner by keeping things aggressively casual, all while managing grief from a parental loss and sitting through an OCD support group. 

And then there is Nate. 

Contamination OCD brings him to the group, but a dark sense of humor draws him to Libi. A classic "he falls first" dynamic wrapped in the comforting, predictable safety net of the friends-to-lovers pipeline. But wait—let me walk that back for a second. The synopsis actually positions this less as a slow burn and more as a chaotic collision course, so calling it safe is probably doing the manuscript a massive disservice. 

### Sunsets, Lies, and Family Nightmares

Transporting two emotionally guarded people to a Mexican timeshare for a forced-proximity crucible is a masterclass in rom-com engineering. Add a newly widowed father with a shiny new girlfriend and a despised college ex showing up on the arm of an annoying cousin? Standard genre torture. 

The fake-dating trope arrives precisely on schedule. Because nothing cures grief and existential dread quite like pretending to be passionately in love under the gaze of toxic relatives. 

Look, I am typing this at an hour when caffeine stops working and starts vibrating through my bloodstream, entirely because I need to hit a word count to satisfy an assignment that makes approximately zero sense under fluorescent ceiling tiles. We are leaning heavily into the Emily Henry and Brooke Averick continuum here. Expect witty banter that masks profound existential dread. Expect the phrase "beach reading" to be weaponized by publishers to describe a book that deals with clinical psychological struggles. 

Can we talk about the mechanics of the fake-dating trope for a second? It is tired. Yet, here we are, eagerly waiting to see if sunset strolls will trick Libi into catching real feelings. The synopsis leans into the vagueness of a publisher pitch—we get no specifics on the ending, just the promise of emotional growth and a heavy dose of mental health representation. 

If Raskin nails the balance between the comedy of errors at the family timeshare and the stark reality of navigating Relationship OCD, this will easily bypass the standard genre traps. If not, it becomes another glossy paperback destined for an airport terminal display rack. 

I have to go anyway. The fluorescent ballast is buzzing at an unnatural frequency, and I need to dye a vintage dress in the bathtub before the sun comes up.
