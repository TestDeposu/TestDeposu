**Page Count:** Unknown

The synopsis for *I Know Who Did It* reads like a fever dream someone scribbled down after watching too many *True Detective* reruns while half-asleep on a Greyhound bus. The premise—father and son moving into a town with a serial killer’s ghost, only for the son to start hearing whispers—is so broad it could fit under the umbrella of any mid-tier thriller franchise. But here’s the thing: it doesn’t even *feel* like a thriller. It feels like a Netflix script someone wrote in a rush to capitalize on the *Whisper Man* brand, which, let’s be honest, is about as original as a *Halloween* knockoff from the early 2000s.

The synopsis leans *heavily* on the "multi-generational trauma" trope, which is fine if you’re going for something like *The Outsider* by Stephen King—where the past actually *matters*—but here, it’s just a way to stuff in a prison interview with Robert De Niro’s ghost (literally, because why not?) without any real payoff. The idea that Frank Carter, the "Whisper Man," had an accomplice is a lazy way to introduce a second villain, but it’s also a missed opportunity. If North wanted to play with psychological horror, he could’ve done something with the *real* horror of complicity—like how the town’s residents *knew* and did nothing. Instead, we get the same old "detectives must solve the case" formula, which is about as fresh as a *Silence of the Lambs* remake where Hannibal Lecter is just a guy who likes to talk.

And then there’s the son, Jake, who starts hearing whispers. Oh, *wonderful*. Because nothing says "modern horror" like a kid who’s just a conduit for the supernatural. It’s the same tired setup as *The Sixth Sense*, but with the emotional weight of a *Hallmark Christmas movie* where the real horror is that someone forgot to water the plants. The synopsis doesn’t even *try* to make this feel like anything more than a procedural with a haunted house subplot. The only thing that saves it from being outright insulting is the fact that it’s already been optioned by Netflix, which means the real horror will be the film’s budget cuts.

The thematic analysis here is *nonexistent*. The synopsis doesn’t even attempt to grapple with what it means to live in a town haunted by its past—it just treats it like a gimmick. If North wanted to explore something deeper, he could’ve looked at how trauma gets passed down, how communities enable monsters, or even how fathers fail their sons. But no. Instead, we get the same old "serial killer returns" plot, which is about as original as a *Jurassic Park* sequel where the dinosaurs are just bigger and scarier.

The only thing that makes this synopsis remotely interesting is the fact that it’s already been adapted into a Netflix film. That’s not a compliment—it’s just proof that someone somewhere thought they could turn this into a *Stranger Things* spin-off. And yet, even then, the synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at the kind of world-building that would make it work. It’s all surface-level scares, no substance. It’s like if *The Silence of the Lambs* was written by someone who only read the Wikipedia page.

The real crime here isn’t the plot—it’s the fact that the synopsis doesn’t even *try* to be clever. It’s just a checklist of tropes: small town, serial killer, father-son dynamic, kid hears voices, detectives investigate. It’s the literary equivalent of a *McDonald’s Happy Meal*—familiar, predictable, and ultimately unsatisfying.

And yet, despite all this, I can’t help but wonder if the book will somehow transcend its own synopsis. Because if it does, it’ll be the first time a thriller about a ghostly serial killer has ever surprised me. But if it doesn’t? Well, at least Netflix will have a movie to show off in their marketing.
