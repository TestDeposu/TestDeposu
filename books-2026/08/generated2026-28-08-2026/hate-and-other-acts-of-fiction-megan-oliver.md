**Author:** Megan Oliver  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-10 APL

Four roommates sharing a single bathroom mean the radiator clanks in C-minor and hot water is an ephemeral myth, much like the pristine pacing promised in contemporary romance synopses. Megan Oliver is coming back at us in late twenty-six with *Hate and Other Acts of Fiction*, a text that smells faintly of bargain-bin Emily Henry trying to share a studio apartment with late-night fanfiction. 

### The Architecture of Petty Grudges

Enemies-to-lovers works only when the animosity tastes like actual battery acid. Sadie Stockton hates Ryan Pearson because of a smug T-shirt and a short-lived college fling. Groundbreaking. We are asked to buy into a profound existential feud born from a local pub crawl and a disputed legacy of a recluse author named Hazel Wilmont. Look, the epistemic weight of historical rumor—did she poison her husband(s)?—gets entirely sidelined by the sheer hormonal whiplash of mixing cocktails in a murder mystery tourist trap. 

And yet. The proximity trope remains undefeated. 

Late-night cocktail tastings act as the primary catalyst for emotional excavation. Naturally. Because nothing says unhealed developmental trauma quite like shaking martinis while pretending you do not remember someone's shoulder blades from a college mattress on the floor. Oliver leans hard into the second-chance romance industrial complex. The synopsis tells us every single beat before the spine is even cracked. Sadie has a promotion on the line. Ryan has a smug grin. The past is buried. We get it. It is giving very much basic literary calories. We are eating junk food while staring at a damp ceiling leak.

### Deconstructing the Literary Syndicate

The framing device of the Wilmont House—a literal shrine to a literary recluse—is doing heavy lifting here. It gestures toward an intellectual metanarrative about how we curate dead women's histories for tourist consumption. But let us be entirely real for a second. This book is not about feminist historiography. It is about sexual tension in a faux-Victorian parlor. 

Oliver previously gave us *Secret Nights and Northern Lights*, a title so aggressively atmospheric it sounds like a scented candle from a big-box retailer. This new offering follows the exact same blueprint. Take two people who refuse to communicate like adults—throw in a contrived professional proximity constraint—add a dash of repressed yearning over artisanal mixology—and boom, instant bestseller status for people who read exclusively on Kindle Paperwhites while commuting on delayed subway lines. 

The prose in the synopsis alone trades in tired cliché — "all clues indicate a reigniting attraction" — which makes me want to scream into a thrifted wool sweater. But sometimes we do not want high modernism. Sometimes we want garbage narrative trash that hits the dopamine receptors just right when the landlord slides another rent increase under the warped wooden door. 

I have to go because the dye bath for that 1950s silk dress is simmering on the hot plate and if I miss the window the color is going to look like sad mustard instead of midnight plum.
