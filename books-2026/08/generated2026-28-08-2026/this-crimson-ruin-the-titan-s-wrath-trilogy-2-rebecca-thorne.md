**Author:** Rebecca Thorne  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

The synopsis of *This Crimson Ruin* arrives like a half-remembered dream—vivid in its fragments, but stubbornly refusing to coalesce into anything resembling a full narrative. It’s the kind of prose that leaves you with the unsettling sense of having just witnessed a car accident: you know there were bodies, there was blood, and someone screamed, but the details are already slipping through your fingers like sand. The domed city of Fall, abandoned and flooded, is a setting that could belong in a Lovecraftian horror novel or a post-apocalyptic thriller, but the way it’s framed—*"ruins of a once-grand city"*—feels less like a world-building choice and more like a placeholder for something far more ambitious. Where’s the *soul* of this place? Where’s the *weight*? The synopsis doesn’t tell us. It just drops us into a void and says, *"Here. Figure it out."*

And then there’s the psychology of it all. Nix Marr, the protagonist, is a woman who signed up for bodyguard duty expecting death, only to find herself trapped beneath the ocean with her ex—because of course it’s her ex—while a *"deeper evil prods Nix’s own mind."* This is the kind of internal conflict that could be *brilliant*, if it weren’t so telegraphed. The idea of a character haunted by something *within* her while navigating the ruins of a city is ripe for psychological depth, but the synopsis doesn’t even attempt to *show* us what that might look like. It’s all implication, all shadow—like reading a synopsis for *The Shining* and being told, *"A man goes to a hotel. Something bad happens."* Where’s the *redrum*? Where’s the *blood on the stairs*? We’re left with the hollow promise of tension, the ghost of a narrative that hasn’t yet been fleshed out.

Yet—and this is where the synopsis starts to unravel—there’s a moment where it *almost* works. The line *"Welcome to Fall."* is delivered with the kind of dry, weary fatalism that could belong in a *Noir* novel or a *cyberpunk* dystopia. It’s the kind of line that makes you sit up straight, because it suggests the author knows how to balance tone with menace. But then the synopsis undercuts it by retreating into vagueness again: *"every answer creating more questions."* That’s not a thematic hook—that’s a *plot device*. It’s the kind of thing you’d find in a *mystery* synopsis, not a *psychological horror* or *lesbian survival thriller* (which, by the way, is a genre that’s been sorely underrepresented in mainstream publishing, so let’s hope Thorne doesn’t treat it like a gimmick).

The real crime here isn’t the lack of detail—it’s the lack of *ambition*. The synopsis reads like it was written by a committee of people who all agreed that *"tension"* and *"lesbian romance"* were the only ingredients needed, and then they threw in a *"deeper evil"* because they remembered *Silent Hill* once. But *This Crimson Ruin* isn’t *Silent Hill*—it’s supposed to be something else entirely. Something darker. Something more *personal*. The fact that we’re not given even a hint of what that *"deeper evil"* might be—whether it’s a literal monster, a psychological breakdown, or something in between—isn’t just frustrating. It’s *dismissive*. It’s the literary equivalent of handing someone a half-empty glass of wine and saying, *"Drink up. It’s good."*

And then there’s the matter of the *title*. *This Crimson Ruin*. It’s a mouthful, isn’t it? It sounds like something out of a *Victorian gothic* novel, or perhaps a *punk* band’s album. It’s the kind of title that makes you wonder if Thorne is trying to signal that this is going to be *heavy*—but the synopsis doesn’t do anything to justify that weight. It’s all *potential*, all *promise*, and none of the *delivery*. It’s like being handed a menu at a Michelin-starred restaurant and being told, *"We have *food*."* Where’s the *flavor*? Where’s the *texture*?

But—here’s the pivot—I might be overreacting. Maybe the synopsis is *intentionally* sparse. Maybe Thorne is playing with the reader’s expectations, letting them *fill in the blanks* like a choose-your-own-adventure novel. And if that’s the case, then *This Crimson Ruin* could be a masterclass in *atmospheric dread*. It could be the kind of book that makes you *feel* the weight of the ocean pressing down on the characters, the way the domed city’s ruins *echo* with the absence of its people. It could be the kind of story that lingers in your mind like a half-remembered nightmare.

Yet—

Literally.

The synopsis doesn’t give us enough to *literally* go on. It’s like being handed a *blank canvas* and being told, *"Paint something dark."* Where’s the *brushstroke*? Where’s the *color*? We’re left with the hollow satisfaction of knowing that something *will* be there, but not knowing what it will look like.

Someone just touched the thermostat. I’m going to investigate this crime.
