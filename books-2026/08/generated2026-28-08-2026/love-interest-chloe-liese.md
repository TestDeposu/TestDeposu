Another afternoon spent staring at a blinking cursor while condensation drips off an iced oat milk latte that costs twelve dollars and tastes like wet cardboard. August humidity clings to the windows like plastic wrap. My brain is completely fried. I am supposed to be conceptualizing a slasher script where nobody dies because they are too anxious to leave their apartments, but instead, I am looking at promotional copy for *Love Interest* by Chloe Liese. 

### The Architecture of the Cliché

The premise rests entirely on the librarian-side-character trope—a literary ghost haunting the periphery of her own existence until a brooding, arrogant writer arrives to validate her worth. Groundbreaking stuff. We are promised a forced-proximity bickering dynamic over email that somehow pivots into a plot-doctoring partnership for a thriller novel. Helen Holloway loves books so much she forgot to have a personality outside of offering recommendations and being the fun aunt. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that. Actually, scratch the cynicism about the trope, because maybe we all secretly yearn to be rescued from our mundane administrative duties by a grumpy man with a keyboard and a hidden heart of gold. 

Or maybe I am just projecting my absolute hatred for contemporary romance pacing. The synopsis leans heavily into the metadata of the genre—characters who are hyper-aware of their own narrative archetypes. Helen reads enough to know she is the supporting player. This meta-awareness is exhausting. It feels less like character depth and more like a neon sign flashing at the audience: LOOK, SHE KNOWS SHE IS IN A ROMANCE NOVEL. Give me characters who are blissfully ignorant of their genre conventions. Give me chaos. Give me someone who commits arson or at least stops smiling while organizing the Dewey Decimal system. 

### The Anticipation Trap

Liese usually delivers the emotional beats—readers of *Two Wrongs Make a Right* know the drill. Yet this setup feels aggressively safe. The antagonistic email exchange sounds like every single enemies-to-lovers indie release currently choking the bestseller lists alongside Ali Hazelwood and Emily Henry clones. The stakes are a library fundraiser. A fundraiser. Where is the blood? Where is the genuine, existential dread of staring down a blank page while the rent is due and the espresso machine keeps making a terrifying grinding noise that sounds like a dying cat? 

The psychological transition from side character to heroine relies entirely on the validation of a man who initially treated her with disdain. That is a tired engine. She helps him with his writer's block—oh, the sweet irony, considering I would trade my left kidney for someone to fix mine right now—and suddenly she discovers her inner fire. How convenient. 

The thematic undercurrent wants to be about reclaiming agency. Yet it hinges on a romance arc where the woman's awakening is catalyzed by a brooding male antagonist. A bit contradictory for a character supposedly learning to stand on her own two feet. But who cares about thematic consistency when you can have witty banter over Gmail? 

Anyway, I have to go deal with the fact that the bathroom faucet has been leaking a steady, maddening rhythm for the last three days and I finally have the energy to hit it with a wrench.
