**Author:** Molly Fader  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-07-14 APL

Four dollars for a shot of espresso at this establishment, and the foam looks like a diseased cumulus cloud. Yet here I sit, clutching a leather folio and glaring at the publisher's advance brief for Molly Fader’s *Lady X*. 

Let us discuss the pedigree of vengeance. 

### The Chronological Whiplash of Domestic Despair

Seventeen-seventy disco polyester meets present-day Calabasas PR management. Truly, a vibe shift for the ages. They understood the assignment. We are promised the gritty underbelly of Manhattan—back when the tax bracket meant something and the cobblestones were actually stained with arterial grease—juxtaposed against a modern celebrity divorce that screams generic streaming-service filler. Why must we always drag the ghosts of radical feminism out of the attic just because some C-list actor cheated on his wife with an extra from a toothpaste commercial? 

Fader attempts to bridge decades through a mysterious box of clippings. Groundbreaking. One expects the prose to oscillate wildly between high-octane grit and suburban gaslighting. And frankly, the synopsis leans heavily into that tired trope where a woman only discovers her own spine because some dusty memorabilia fell on her head. Give me a break. Real bloodlines do not require archival research from a childhood basement to understand retribution. 

### The Aesthetics of Vigilante Vandalism

Consider the structural mechanics of justice here. Vandalism. How deliciously quaint. They spray-paint terrible men, or perhaps throw bricks through brownstone windows, until gravity intervenes and someone plummets five stories. Did she push him? Did he trip? Who cares. The synopsis drops this little narrative grenade like it is high art, yet remains infuriatingly vague on the actual mechanics of the vanishing act. Classic mid-list mystery obfuscation. 

One shudders to think of the demographic that will unironically tweet about this while sipping boxed wine. It reeks of mid-century nostalgia repurposed for the algorithm. Think Gillian Flynn meets a lesser-known Erica Jong novel, minus the intellectual rigor and soaked in pure, unadulterated stan culture. The wealthy protagonist hiding from the paparazzi in her childhood home is a tired cliché that smells faintly of desperation and poor asset management. 

### The Inheritance of Rage

Blood tells. Or so they say. Yet this text relies on the cheapest narrative crutch available: the ancestral awakening of female fury. As if rage were a recessive gene carried exclusively in old newspaper clippings and faded Polaroid photographs. One moment you are shopping for cashmere, and the next you are moonlighting as an urban terrorist because your husband bought a boat with his mistress. 

The prose itself, judging by the blurb, will likely coast on the coattails of Jodi Picoult-esque emotional manipulation. High-concept premise, deeply melodramatic execution, and a twist that you can spot from the mezzanine level of the Met. Still, the 1970s New York backdrop almost saves it. Almost. If the disco sequences read like a Pinterest board of Studio 54, I am throwing this galley straight into the nearest recycling bin. 

I have no time for this amateur hour nonsense any longer. My sommelier just acquired a remarkably rare batch of 1991 vintage bourbon, and it requires my immediate, undivided palate.
