The air hanging thick and heavy through the screen door this August afternoon smells faintly of scorched lawn and the lingering detergent from the third load of damp socks I pulled from the dryer twenty minutes ago while trying to quiet the hollow ache that sits right behind my sternum whenever the house gets too quiet. Iain Reid is coming back with *A New Kind of Clean*, and if his previous unsettling little trap *I'm Thinking of Ending Things* taught us anything, it is that he specializes in stretching reality until it snaps right in your face like a wet elastic band left out in the blistering sun. He drops us straight into the pathetic, frayed desperation of an aspiring actress named Missy Djalo—running from audition to audition, broke, haunted by a fractured relationship with a Cameroonian mother that she cannot quite outrun—before walking right into a cryptic, predatory establishment called Air Free. And do not even get me started on the absolute predictability of these psychological thriller premises where the ambitious young woman willingly hands over her autonomy to some charismatic suburban couple just because her bank account is empty and her self-worth is hovering somewhere near absolute zero, because we have read this exact cautionary tale a thousand times over in every domestic noir paperback cluttering the tables at Barnes & Noble since Gillian Flynn changed the entire landscape of modern suspense. 

Tragic.

### The Price of Admission

There is something aggressively exhausting about authors using "memory" and "alienation" as shiny speculative keys to pry open the human skull when we are all just trying to navigate the mundane weight of losing people we love without turning into abstract philosophical concepts stripped of our identities by mysterious organizations with corporate-sounding names. Reid writes in spare, hypnotic prose—or so the marketing copy promises us—which usually translates to three hundred pages of intentionally sparse nothingness designed to make pretentious book clubs gasp over coffee while ignoring the actual messy mechanics of human grief. But Missy wants to play Helena in *A Midsummer Night's Dream* so badly that she is apparently willing to forfeit her privacy, her relationships, her individuality, and her memories to a house that blurs the line between fantasy and reality. 

Surreal.

Give me realism. Give me the messy kitchen table covered in half-eaten string cheese and permission slips that need signing instead of a minimalist fable about a cryptic couple testing the boundaries of human endurance. 

Oh god. The minivan doors are slamming in the driveway. The neighborhood kids are already spilling across the porch, and I completely forgot to cut the apple slices.
