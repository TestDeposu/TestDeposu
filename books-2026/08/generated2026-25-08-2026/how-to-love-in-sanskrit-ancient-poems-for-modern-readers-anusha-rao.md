**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-03 APL

August afternoons smell of chalk dust, overheated laminators, and the desperate, low-level panic of twelve-year-olds who have spent their entire summer watching TikTok choreography instead of reading a single page. Yet here we are, examining *How to Love in Sanskrit* by Anusha Rao and Suhas Mahesh, a volume promising 3,000 years of romantic verse translated with enough snark to wake up the dead. Because apparently, we need an ancient guide to tell us that a lover's tiff functions as a whetstone. Give me a break. I watch fourteen-year-olds dramatically slam heavy metal lockers over text message misunderstandings every single day, so I hardly need a courtly poet from antiquity to explain the mechanics of teenage angst. At least Kalidasa had the decency to write on palm leaves instead of Snapchat.

The premise relies heavily on the radical notion that ancient writers cared about romance just as much as modern teenagers obsess over their crush's Instagram stories. And yet, the promotional material offers barely a glimpse of the actual text, leaving us to guess whether the translations are genuinely witty or just dressed up in modern irony to appeal to people who buy books purely for the aesthetic value of their coffee tables. It feels awfully convenient to slap "a perfect gift for any loved one" on the back cover. Nobody buys poetry for their significant other unless they forgot an anniversary and panicked in the middle of a bookstore. 

Honestly, why do publishers insist on marketing historical poetry like it is a self-help manual for struggling couples? It reminds me of that dreadful YA dystopia everyone forced me to read on the bus last month where the protagonists rebel against a totalitarian regime by growing rooftop tomatoes—as if agricultural rebellion makes up for paper-thin world-building and dialogue that sounds like it was generated by a malfunctioning automated lawnmower. Bring back real literature where people simply stare out rain-streaked windows and make bad life choices without turning every single emotional tremor into a masterclass in resilience. 

Pass.

I need to go rescue my favorite vintage fountain pen from my grandson's inexplicably sticky hands before he uses it to write his name in peanut butter on the living room rug.
