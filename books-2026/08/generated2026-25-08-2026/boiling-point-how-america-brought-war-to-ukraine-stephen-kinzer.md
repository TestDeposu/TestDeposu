**Author:** Stephen Kinzer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-03 APL

The synopsis of *Boiling Point* reads like a Cold War-era spy novel, the kind that would’ve made Graham Greene jealous—except Greene at least had the decency to make his villains more than just footnotes in a geopolitical chess game. Kinzer’s book promises to unearth a secret history, one where Ukrainians are not just pawns but *characters*—flawed, desperate, and occasionally heroic, but always tangled in the same web of American ambition that’s been pulling them apart for generations. The problem? The synopsis doesn’t tell us *who* these characters are, only that they’re a mix of rebels, exiles, and traitors. That’s the kind of vague, cinematic framing that makes you wonder if Kinzer is writing a novel or a *National Geographic* special.

And yet—there’s something thrilling about it. The idea of a 75-year covert campaign is the kind of narrative that begs for a *Wild Bill Donovan* energy, the kind of mythos that turns intelligence work into something almost romantic. But romance is the last thing this book should be. Kinzer’s track record—*All the Shah’s Men*, *Overthrow*—proves he’s better at dissecting American hubris than at crafting sympathetic villains. So if *Boiling Point* leans into the "wild stories and improbable characters" bit, I’ll be waiting for the moment when a CIA asset turns out to be just another American in over his head, just like the last one.

The synopsis hints at a structure that could’ve been *The Good Fight* meets *The Post*—except where *The Post* at least had the decency to make its journalism feel like a moral victory, Kinzer’s book seems destined to be a cautionary tale about how easily good intentions go to war. The CIA’s obsession with Ukrainians as "their most effective weapon against Moscow" reads like a love letter to American exceptionalism, but also like a warning. The question is: will Kinzer make it feel like a tragedy, or just another chapter in the "America did it first" narrative?

Then again, maybe that’s the point. The synopsis doesn’t just promise a story—it promises a *revelation*. The idea that both sides kept this secret until now is the kind of twist that would’ve made *The Parallax View* feel like a textbook. But if Kinzer pulls it off, it’ll be because he’s not just exposing a conspiracy; he’s making you *care* about the people caught in the middle. And that’s where the real risk lies. Because if the characters are as compelling as the synopsis suggests, then the book won’t just be about war—it’ll be about the cost of believing in a cause so fiercely that you forget the people it’s supposed to protect.

But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t tell us *how* Kinzer will make us care. It doesn’t tell us if the Ukrainians in this story are heroes or victims, or if the Americans are the villains or just another set of players in a game they didn’t fully understand. And that’s the problem with a book that promises to tell a secret history—it’s easy to forget that history is only as compelling as the people who lived it. If Kinzer can make the CIA operatives and the Ukrainian rebels feel like real people, with real flaws and real regrets, then this could be his masterpiece. If he can’t, it’ll just be another book about how America brought war to Ukraine—because, let’s be honest, the synopsis already does that.

I’m holding out hope that Kinzer will surprise us. That he’ll find the human stories buried in the archives, the ones that make the geopolitics feel less like a board game and more like a tragedy. But I’m also bracing for the moment when the book feels like a lecture, when the CIA agents become just another set of footnotes in the "America’s Fault" narrative. And if that happens? Well, at least we’ll have a good story to tell.
