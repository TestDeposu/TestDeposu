**Author:** Kim Stanley Robinson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

The synopsis here is a masterclass in *how not to sell a book*—because it’s so vague it reads like a museum plaque about a lost expedition. "Interweaving memoir and ice adventure"? That’s the kind of vague, self-congratulatory phrasing that makes you wonder if Robinson’s editor let him near the final draft. But then again, the man’s spent decades writing *Mars* trilogies where the prose is so dense you need a shovel to dig through it. So maybe this is intentional—like a snowdrift hiding something buried beneath.

Cherry-Garrard’s *The Worst Journey in the World* is the book that got Robinson hooked, and the way the synopsis frames this as a "side trip" is telling. It’s not just about following in their footsteps; it’s about the *failure* of those footsteps, the way the Antarctic landscape turns any human endeavor into a cautionary tale. Robinson’s own trip in ’95 sounds like a middle finger to the whole "first to the pole" obsession—he’s not chasing glory, just wandering, taking notes, and occasionally getting lost in his own thoughts. That’s the kind of thing that makes his books feel like they were written by someone who’s been to the moon and back, but also just really likes a good hike.

Yet the synopsis drops a line that’s either genius or a cop-out: *"searching for what can, and cannot, be found again."* That’s the kind of philosophical meandering you’d expect from Robinson, but it’s also the kind of thing that could make this book feel like a *New Yorker* essay stretched into novel form. The problem? The synopsis doesn’t tell you whether this is a travelogue, a meditation on climate change, or a love letter to the idea of exploration itself. It’s all over the place, like Robinson’s own writing when he’s not writing about Mars.

And then there’s the photography—because of course there’s photography. Robinson’s books are always illustrated, but this one sounds like it’s trying to be *National Geographic* meets *The Snows of Kilimanjaro*. The idea of "earthly holy places" is either profound or pretentious, depending on whether you believe in the sacredness of ice or just the sacredness of writing about ice. It’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if Robinson’s really there, or if he’s just quoting himself from a 1995 journal entry.

But here’s the pivot: maybe the vagueness is the point. Maybe Robinson’s not trying to sell you a book, but to sell you an *experience*—the kind of experience that only someone who’s been to Antarctica could possibly understand. And if that’s the case, then the synopsis is working exactly as intended. It’s not about the plot; it’s about the *vibe*. The way the words sit in your mouth like snow on your tongue.

Yet—*yet*—the mention of "people who also feel a kinship to a place worthy of preserving" feels like a direct nod to the current climate anxiety trend. Robinson’s always been the guy who writes about the end of the world, but this time, it sounds like he’s trying to make it feel *personal*. And that’s where the synopsis starts to feel less like a book and more like a manifesto. Which, fine, but it’s not exactly *The Best Journey in the World*—it’s just a really good synopsis for a really good book that hasn’t been written yet.
