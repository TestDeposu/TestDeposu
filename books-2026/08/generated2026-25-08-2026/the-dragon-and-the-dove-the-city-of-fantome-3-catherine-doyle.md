The expected publication of *The Dragon and the Dove*, the final installment in Catherine Doyle's *The City of Fantome* trilogy, is looming, and I'm left wondering if this romantasy series will deliver a satisfying conclusion to Sera and Ransom's tumultuous love story. As a fan of Kristen Ciccarelli's lush world-building and the atmospheric *Caraval* series, I'm hoping Doyle will bring the heat — but the synopsis has me questioning the narrative's direction.

Sera's Saint-maker gift, a powerful force that could be her greatest strength or most crippling weakness, threatens to consume her. Ransom, bound to nightmarish shadows, teeters on the edge of despair. What are they willing to lose? The stakes are high, but the vagueness of the synopsis has me scratching my head. Are we looking at a classic case of 'Chosen Ones' syndrome, where the fate of the world rests on the shoulders of two angsty teens? Or will Doyle subvert our expectations and deliver something more nuanced?

The enemies-to-lovers trope, while tired, can be effective when executed well. The push-and-pull of Sera and Ransom's relationship has been the driving force behind the series thus far, but I worry that the 'volatile powers' pulling them apart might feel like a contrived plot device. Still, I'm willing to give Doyle the benefit of the doubt — after all, the best romantasy series know how to balance action, romance, and fantasy elements.

One thing's for certain: the themes of power, sacrifice, and the blurred lines between good and evil will be front and center in *The Dragon and the Dove*. The People's Saint, a self-proclaimed messiah with a dark agenda, is a compelling villain archetype. I'm curious to see how Doyle will explore the complexities of his character and the moral gray areas that Sera and Ransom will inevitably find themselves in.

As I wait for the release of *The Dragon and the Dove*, I find myself drawing parallels to other series that have tackled similar themes. The *All Souls Trilogy* by Deborah Harkness, for example, masterfully wove together historical fiction, fantasy, and romance elements. Will Doyle's series follow suit, or will it carve out its own unique niche in the world of romantasy?

### The Cost of Power

The true test of *The Dragon and the Dove* will be its ability to balance the fantastical elements with the emotional depth of its characters. If Doyle can pull it off, this series might just become a modern classic in the world of romantasy. But if the narrative relies too heavily on convenient plot devices or clichéd character archetypes, it risks feeling like just another forgettable series in a crowded market.

I'm willing to take the risk, though. There's something about the promise of *The Dragon and the Dove* that has me hooked. Maybe it's the prospect of a satisfying conclusion to Sera and Ransom's story, or maybe it's the hope that Doyle will surprise me with a bold, unconventional narrative. Whatever the reason, I'll be counting down the days until November 24th.
