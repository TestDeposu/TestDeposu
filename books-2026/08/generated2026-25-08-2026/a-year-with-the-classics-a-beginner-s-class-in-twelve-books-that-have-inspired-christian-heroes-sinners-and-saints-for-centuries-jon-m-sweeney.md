The mystique of Christian spirituality, as if the answers to life's existential questions lie hidden within the pages of dusty tomes penned by saints and sinners. Jon M. Sweeney's *A Year with the Classics* promises to be a revelatory journey through twelve foundational works of Christian literature, guiding readers toward a deeper understanding of faith and spirituality. But can this "beginner's class" truly unlock the secrets of the past, or will it merely regurgitate tired dogma?

One cannot help but wonder if Sweeney's approach will be akin to a Cliff's Notes version of the classics, stripping the original works of their nuance and complexity in favor of a more palatable, modern interpretation. The promise of "simple daily and weekly practices" drawn from each text raises an eyebrow – will these exercises be shallow imitations of the original works, or genuine attempts to integrate the wisdom of the past into our frenetic, tech-obsessed lives?

The inclusion of female spiritual writers like Teresa of Avila and Julian of Norwich is a welcome respite from the patriarchal dominance of Christian literature. Yet, one cannot help but wonder if their contributions will be relegated to the periphery, or if Sweeney will truly delve into the ways in which their experiences and perspectives shaped the spiritual landscape of their time. 

And then, of course, there's the elephant in the room: the problematic legacy of Christian spirituality. Can Sweeney's book truly reconcile the historical injustices and contradictions of the faith with its purported message of love and humility? Or will it gloss over these issues in favor of a more sanitized, feel-good narrative? 

But perhaps that's the point – perhaps *A Year with the Classics* is not meant to be a comprehensive, historically accurate exploration of Christian spirituality, but rather a personal, subjective journey through the works that have shaped Sweeney's own faith. If that's the case, then the book's value lies not in its academic rigor, but in its ability to inspire and guide readers toward a deeper understanding of themselves and their place in the world.

In that sense, *A Year with the Classics* might be compared to the works of authors like Thomas Merton or Annie Dillard, who have managed to distill the essence of spirituality into accessible, beautifully written prose. But will Sweeney's book be able to transcend the boundaries of Christian spirituality, speaking to a broader audience in the way that, say, a book like *The Power of Now* by Eckhart Tolle has? Only time will tell.

Until next time, when the smoke has cleared and the coffee has been consumed, I remain...
