Girl Number 8 is going to be a wild ride – I can already feel it. A missing child case in a class-divided Nigeria, where superstition and corruption are as rampant as the politicians' smirks. Let's not kid ourselves, this is a powder keg of a story just waiting to blow. The setup's already got my attention: a strong-willed detective, Sola Adeyemi, investigating the disappearance of a four-year-old girl, Frieda, whose light green eyes make her a prime target in a world where ritualistic murders are a thing.

What I love about this premise is its potential to explore the darker side of human nature – the parts we try to keep hidden behind a veneer of civility. The fact that Sola heads up the Department of Ritualistic Murders and Human Sacrifice already tells me that this book's going to be a deep dive into the heart of darkness that lurks within us all. And the connection to the upcoming election? That's just the cherry on top – a deliciously sinister plot twist that's got me salivating.

But what really gets my goat is the vagueness of the synopsis. I mean, what's the deal with the house mistress, the wife of local politician Samson Buzugbe? Is she involved in Frieda's disappearance? And what's the significance of Frieda's light green eyes, exactly? Is it just a random detail or a crucial plot point? I'm left with more questions than answers, and honestly, it's a bit frustrating. Can't a girl get some clarity around here?

And don't even get me started on the comparisons to Tana French and Oyinkan Braithwaite. I mean, those are some big shoes to fill – Tana French's Dublin Murder Squad series is a masterclass in atmospheric tension, and Oyinkan Braithwaite's My Sister, the Serial Killer is a darkly comedic gem. So, the pressure's on, Janice Okoh – can you deliver?

I'm going to take a wild guess here, but I think Girl Number 8 is going to be a thought-provoking exploration of maternal guilt, systemic corruption, and the deadly price of superstition. It's going to be a book that makes you uncomfortable, that challenges your assumptions, and that leaves you feeling like you've been punched in the gut. And honestly? I'm here for it.

### The Price of Superstition

One thing that's got me intrigued is the way the book seems to be tackling the theme of superstition. I mean, ritualistic murders and human sacrifice are some heavy stuff – it's not exactly the kind of thing you'd find in your average police procedural. But what I think is interesting is the way the book seems to be using superstition as a metaphor for the ways in which our deepest fears can be exploited. It's like, when we're afraid of something, we're more likely to believe in the supernatural, the unknown – and that's exactly what these ritualistic murders are preying on.

And then, of course, there's the connection to the election. I mean, it's no secret that politicians are masters of manipulation – they know exactly how to push our buttons, how to make us afraid, and how to exploit those fears for their own gain. So, the idea that the missing girls and the election are somehow connected? That's just a deliciously sinister plot twist that's got me hooked.
