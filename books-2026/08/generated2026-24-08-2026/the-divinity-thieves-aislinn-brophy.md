**Author:** Aislinn Brophy  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The sweet scent of freshly baked chocolate chip cookies wafts through the air, mingling with the faint tang of anticipation, as I ponder the upcoming release of *The Divinity Thieves*. Aislinn Brophy's latest YA fantasy has all the makings of a thrilling page-turner, with its promise of mortal teens competing to become gods, and I must admit, my curiosity is piqued.

In a world where the gods hold all the power, Fawn Gallo and Mackie Prince are two unlikely candidates vying for divinity. Fawn, a con artist, has grown accustomed to a life of scrapping by, while Mackie, a demigod, has been denied the privileges of her heritage. Their complicated past, marked by a devastating betrayal, threatens to derail their chances of success in the Ascension Trial, a once-a-century competition that promises godhood to the winner.

As I delve into the synopsis, I'm struck by the similarities between *The Divinity Thieves* and other YA fantasies like *Lightlark* and *Lore*. The themes of power, identity, and the struggle for recognition are all too familiar, and I wonder if Brophy will be able to bring a fresh perspective to the table. The character dynamics, particularly the complicated history between Fawn and Mackie, show promise, but I'm concerned that the plot may rely too heavily on convenient coincidences and contrivances.

And yet, there's something about the premise that resonates with me. Perhaps it's the idea of two underdogs, forced to navigate a treacherous world of gods and mortals, that speaks to my own experiences as a... well, as someone who's had to juggle multiple tasks at once while trying to keep their kids from destroying the kitchen. The marketing team worked overtime on this one, that's for sure.

But I digress. As I consider the themes and character arcs in *The Divinity Thieves*, I'm reminded of a rather frustrating experience with my kids' favorite cartoon, *Adventure Time*. The show's creator, Pendleton Ward, has a tendency to introduce complex, thought-provoking storylines, only to abandon them in favor of more lighthearted, humorous fare. I worry that Brophy may fall into a similar trap, sacrificing depth and nuance for the sake of a more marketable, crowd-pleasing narrative.

Still, I remain cautiously optimistic. The premise is intriguing, and the character dynamics show promise. If Brophy can balance the action, suspense, and emotional depth, *The Divinity Thieves* may just become a standout in the YA fantasy genre.

As I finish writing this, I notice that my youngest has managed to get a jump rope tangled in the ceiling fan. Again. Sigh. I suppose it's time to take a break from speculating about *The Divinity Thieves* and attend to the more pressing matters at hand.
