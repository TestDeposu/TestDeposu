Deception and poison are a potent cocktail, and *Like the Moon We Rise* seems to be serving it up with a twist of rebellion. Auriel, the protagonist, is a poison maiden – a profession that sounds more like a dark fairy tale than a viable career path. But in this world, poison is an art, and Auriel is its reluctant master.

The synopsis promises a story of freedom, revenge, and the blurring of lines between loyalty and deception. Auriel's predicament – being sold to the enigmatic Ambrose and forced to pretend to be the prophesied Khelani – raises questions about the nature of identity and the cost of playing a role. Is Auriel's growing attraction to Faolan a genuine feeling, or is it just another layer of deception? And what does it mean to be a powerful woman in a world where magick is controlled by the Empress?

These are the kinds of questions that *Like the Moon We Rise* seems to be asking, and they're the same questions that authors like Leigh Bardugo and Roshani Chokshi have explored in their own works. The idea of a powerful female protagonist navigating a treacherous world of politics and magic is nothing new, but it's a trope that continues to captivate readers.

### The Shadows We Keep

But what really catches my attention is the mention of a "dark presence" within Auriel. What does this mean, exactly? Is it a metaphor for her own darkness, or is there something more sinister at play? The synopsis is frustratingly vague on this point, leaving me to wonder if the book will explore the complexities of Auriel's inner world.

And then there's the issue of prophecies and dead gods. It's a familiar trope, but one that can be done well or poorly. Will *Like the Moon We Rise* subvert our expectations, or will it follow a predictable path? I'm not sure, but I'm willing to give it a chance.
