August sweat drips onto the secondhand paper on my desk, sticking the pages together while the radiator downstairs clanks its useless summer song. Paul French is giving us *The Last Emperor of China: Twilight of the Forbidden City* later this year, and my apartment feels just as claustrophobic as Puyi's gilded cage in the 1920s. Except he had eunuchs and I have a landlady who bangs on the wall whenever my typewriter keys click past midnight. 

### Gilded Cages and Crumbling Plaster

A monarch without a crown, a ruler without a people—we have heard this refrain before. But French apparently found some dusty letters and an English teacher's diary to prove the marriage between Puyi and Wanrong wasn't entirely a cold political sham. Or maybe he didn't. The synopsis dances around the actual historiography, leaning heavily on the romance angle to sell copies to people who watch too many period dramas. Actually, scratch that—intimacy behind palace walls is precisely what everyone wants to read about while sweating through the dog days of August. We crave scandal in closed rooms. 

The architecture of power fascinates me more than the pillow talk anyway. The Forbidden City survived fires, looting, and artillery shells, remaining a redoubt while the rest of the world burned down. It is a brick-and-mortar metaphor for stubborn obsolescence. We all live in our own crumbling citadels, do we not? My crumbling citadel has peeling yellow wallpaper and a view of a brick alleyway that smells perpetually of fried garlic. Yet French treats the citadel like a living character rather than a mere backdrop, which gives me hope this won't read like a dry academic thesis padded out for the Christmas bestseller lists. Think Peter Hessler meets Dan Baum, but with more silk robes and existential dread.

### The Myth of the Imperial Bedroom

And yet, relying on private diaries to rewrite established historical consensus feels like a publisher's desperate hook. Every biography promises previously unpublished letters these days. It is the mandatory marketing sticker on the cover. Did the tutor and the English teacher really possess unbiased panoramic views of imperial marital bliss, or did they simply project their own Victorian sensibilities onto teenagers trapped by history? I suspect the latter. Historians love finding a secret love story where none existed just to make the tragedy sting a bit more sharply for the modern commuter. 

The prose in the promotional copy promises a sweeping descent into madness and paranoia—servants scheming, eunuchs whispering, the constant threat of an assassin's knife in the dark. That is the stuff that keeps you turning pages by the flickering amber glow of a single desk lamp while the humidity hangs in the room like a wet blanket. Whether French can sustain that atmospheric tension without collapsing into melodrama remains the real question. Publishers love a good doomed dynasty, almost as much as they love a manufactured controversy about a loveless marriage. 

I need to finish binding these signatures for a friend's chapbook before the glue dries in this relentless heat.
