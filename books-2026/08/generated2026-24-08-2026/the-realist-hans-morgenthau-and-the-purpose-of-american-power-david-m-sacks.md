A biography of Hans Morgenthau, the father of realist international relations theory—just what I needed to liven up my crowded subway commute. As I squeeze into a seat, wedged between a suited businessman and a harried mom, I find myself drawn to the story of this German émigré who defied the odds to become one of America's most influential thinkers.

The Realist: Hans Morgenthau and the Purpose of American Power promises to be a meticulously researched and masterfully written account of Morgenthau's life and ideas. But as I scan the synopsis, I'm struck by how little I actually know about the man behind the theory. We're told that Morgenthau's experiences in post-World War I Germany, where he was ostracized and spat on by his classmates, shaped his worldview and led him to conclude that "power only yielded to countervailing power." But what about the nuances of his personality, the contradictions and complexities that make a person more than just a set of ideas?

And then, of course, there's the elephant in the room: the Cold War. Morgenthau's book, *Politics Among Nations*, became the defining textbook on international relations in the country, shaping generations of scholars and policymakers. But how did Morgenthau's ideas influence the course of American foreign policy? Did he ever grapple with the moral implications of his realist worldview, or was he too focused on the grand game of geopolitics?

As I flip through the pages of my mind, I'm reminded of other thinkers who have grappled with the complexities of power and morality. Henry Kissinger, Morgenthau's protégé, comes to mind, as does the great diplomat and historian George Kennan. But Morgenthau's story feels different, somehow. Perhaps it's the fact that he was an outsider, a German Jew who fled Nazi Germany and had to remake himself in America. Or maybe it's the fact that his ideas continue to shape the course of international relations to this day.

As I near my subway stop, I find myself wondering: what secrets will *The Realist* reveal about Morgenthau's inner life? Will we see the man behind the theory, or just a series of abstract ideas and historical events? I'm willing to bet that David M. Sacks' book will be a page-turner, full of insights into the making of American foreign policy. But I'm also hoping for something more: a nuanced, multidimensional portrait of a man who helped shape the world we live in today.

Time will tell, I suppose. For now, I'll just have to ride the waves of anticipation and speculation. And speaking of riding, I think it's time for me to grab my bike and hit the streets.
