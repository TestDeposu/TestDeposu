The lives of radical women artists during Roosevelt's New Deal – what an incredible, yet largely unexamined chapter in American history. *Bohemians on the Breadline*, Lauren Arrington's upcoming book, promises to shed light on the extraordinary stories of women like Alice Neel, Augusta Savage, and Pablita Velarde, who defied conventions and challenged the status quo through their art. But will it truly deliver on its promise?

I mean, think about it – we're talking about a group of women who, despite facing racism, inequality, and sexism, still managed to create radical art that spoke truth to power. And yet, their work was often destroyed, forgotten, or absorbed into the cultural landscape, stripped of its context and significance. It's a tragic reminder of the ways in which women's contributions to art and history are so often marginalized and erased.

But what I'm really curious about is how Arrington plans to bring these women's stories to life. The synopsis mentions "vivid, eloquent prose" and "archival research," but it's hard to gauge just how immersive and engaging the book will be without more details. Will we get to experience the intimate struggles and triumphs of these women artists, or will they remain distant figures, relegated to the footnotes of history? And what about the art itself – will we get to see images, or will we have to rely on Arrington's descriptions to imagine the works?

And then there's the issue of context – how will Arrington situate these women's stories within the broader cultural and historical landscape of the time? Will she draw connections to other radical women artists, like Frida Kahlo or Georgia O'Keeffe? Or will she focus more on the specific challenges and opportunities presented by the New Deal programs?

Literally.

I'm reminded of other books that have attempted to reclaim the stories of marginalized women artists, like Whitney Chadwick's *Women Artists and the Surrealist Movement*. But *Bohemians on the Breadline* seems to be tackling a much more specific, and potentially more fraught, topic. Can Arrington pull it off?

I'm skeptical, but also intrigued. There's something about the intersection of art, politics, and feminism that feels particularly relevant right now. And if Arrington can deliver on her promise to bring these women's stories to life, *Bohemians on the Breadline* could be a game-changer.

Sigh. But only if she can avoid the pitfalls of dry, academic writing and truly make these women's stories sing.
