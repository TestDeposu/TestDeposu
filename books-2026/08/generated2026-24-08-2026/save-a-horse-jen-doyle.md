**Author:** Jen Doyle  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Save a Horse* reads like a love letter to the kind of romance that gets dusted off every few years—because apparently, the world still needs another cowboy who rides in on a white horse (metaphorically, since we’re not dealing with actual horses here, just the emotional equivalent) to sweep a city girl off her feet. And yet—*and yet*—it’s not even original. The trope is so well-worn it’s practically a genre unto itself, like a well-loved sweater that’s seen better decades. Molly Parker’s arrival in Bitterroot Valley is the kind of setup that makes me wonder if the author’s ever heard of *The Horse Whisperer*—because let’s be real, this is just *The Horse Whisperer* with a professor instead of a therapist and a saloon instead of a barn.

The problem isn’t that the premise is bad—it’s that it’s *predictable*. Molly’s a woman on the run from her own life, Gabe’s a man tied to the land like a barnacle to a ship, and the whole "will they/won’t they" dynamic is so cliché it’s almost comical. But here’s the thing: clichés aren’t inherently bad. They’re the literary equivalent of a well-worn path—familiar, comforting, and sometimes even necessary. The question is whether the author has the chops to make it feel fresh. And so far, the synopsis doesn’t give me much hope. It’s all sunshine and horseback rides, with no real stakes beyond "she’s leaving soon" and "he’s too stubborn to leave." Where’s the conflict? Where’s the *teeth*? This feels like the kind of book that gets praised for its "wholesome" vibes but gets buried under the weight of its own sentimentality.

And don’t even get me started on the *timing*. 2026 is supposed to be the year of the "quiet romance," where the focus is on emotional intimacy over explosive drama. But *Save a Horse* doesn’t even *pretend* to be quiet. It’s loud, in the way a siren is loud—annoying, unnecessary, and impossible to ignore. The synopsis is all about the *feelings*, the *connection*, the *sunny days*—but where’s the tension? Where’s the *mess*? Because real relationships aren’t just about shared dinners and horseback rides. They’re about the fights, the misunderstandings, the moments when one person feels like they’re being pulled in two directions. And yet, here we are, with Molly and Gabe gliding through their romance like they’re on a Disney boat ride, hand in hand, no rough waters in sight.

I can’t help but think of *The Notebook* here—because let’s be honest, this is just another love story where the woman is the one who leaves, and the man is the one who stays, and the whole thing is framed as some kind of tragic inevitability. But *The Notebook* at least had the decency to be dramatic. This? This is just… *safe*. And in a genre that’s supposed to be about escapism, safety feels like the opposite of what we’re looking for.

But fine, let’s say the book delivers on the romance. Let’s say the writing is sharp, the characters are layered, and the emotional beats land. Even then, I’d still have a problem with the way the synopsis treats Gabe like some kind of noble, untouchable figure. Single dad? Rugged rancher? Protective? It’s all so neatly packaged, so *perfect*, that it’s almost insulting. Real men aren’t just protective—they’re flawed, complicated, and sometimes downright infuriating. And real women? They don’t just fall into place like puzzle pieces. They *fight* for their place. But I guess that’s not the kind of story we’re getting here.
