**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-17 APL

The synopsis of *We Were Here* arrives like a well-timed but slightly belated invitation to a party you’ve been waiting to crash—only to realize the host forgot to mention the guest list was rigged. The book’s premise is a necessary corrective, a long-overdue audit of rock’s ledger where Black artists have been systematically undercharged for their labor. But here’s the rub: the synopsis itself reads like a press release drafted by a committee of earnest historians who’ve never actually *been* in the room where it happened. It’s the difference between a firsthand account of the *Montreux Fire* and a Wikipedia page on it—same facts, but one still smells like smoke.

Phillips, a musician herself, is clearly positioned to bring something vital to the table: her own lived experience as a Black woman in punk. Yet the synopsis leans so hard into the "we’ve been erased" narrative that it risks becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy. Where’s the *texture*? The *mess*? The way history isn’t just a series of names and dates but a series of *moments*—like Chuck Berry’s guitar licks being stolen by white rockers while Berry himself was still fighting for basic respect, or Jimi Hendrix’s genius being reduced to a few iconic riffs while his political and spiritual depth was buried under layers of whitewashed myth. The synopsis doesn’t *show* this; it just *lists* it, like a grocery list of owed debts.

And then there’s the problem of *alternative music*. The term itself is a slippery, ever-shifting concept, one that’s been co-opted, diluted, and repackaged so many times it’s almost meaningless. But the synopsis treats it like a fixed genre, a monolith, when in reality, it’s a patchwork of scenes, subcultures, and countercultures that often hated each other. The Bad Brains and Living Colour? Sure, they’re icons. But what about the Black punk bands that got lost in the shuffle—like the *Slits*’ influence on early hardcore, or how *Minor Threat*’s DIY ethos was built on the backs of Black musicians who couldn’t afford to play by the same rules? The synopsis doesn’t even *acknowledge* the fractures in the narrative, let alone explore them.

Phillips’ own story is the most compelling part of this project—her coming-of-age in punk, her front-woman role in Big Joanie. That’s the kind of firsthand detail that could make this book *sing*. But the synopsis treats it like an afterthought, a footnote to the larger historical argument. It’s as if she’s saying, "Here’s my story, but really, the *real* story is the one we’ve been missing." But the *real* story isn’t just about what’s missing—it’s about how those absences *shaped* the music, how they created the very spaces that later became "alternative." The synopsis doesn’t grapple with that tension; it just *states* it.

And then there’s the question of *who* this book is for. Is it for the casual listener who’s heard of Hendrix but never considered why his legacy was so carefully curated? Or is it for the hardcore fans who’ve been waiting decades for this kind of reckoning? The synopsis doesn’t narrow its focus enough. It’s too broad, too general, like a museum exhibit that tries to cover too much ground and ends up covering nothing deeply.

But perhaps the biggest flaw is the synopsis’s refusal to *complicate*. It’s all about the "key players" and the "obscured figures," but history isn’t just about the heroes and the villains—it’s about the *systems*. The way Black musicians were forced into specific roles, how their innovations were repackaged as "white" innovations, how the very idea of "alternative" was often a code for "not *too* Black." The synopsis doesn’t dig into that. It just *lists* the names.

Still, if Phillips can deliver on the promise of her own voice—the way a musician’s perspective can cut through the noise of historical revisionism—then this could be something special. But right now, it feels like a book that’s been written by committee, by people who’ve never actually *played* in the scene they’re describing. It’s the difference between a live performance and a studio recording: the synopsis is sterile, polished, and missing the raw energy that would make it *live*.

And now, because I’ve been staring at this too long, I’m distracted by the thought of finding a dry cleaner in Manhattan that doesn’t charge $20 for a pair of trousers. The irony is not lost on me.
