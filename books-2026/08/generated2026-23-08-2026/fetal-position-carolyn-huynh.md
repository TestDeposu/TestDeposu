**Page Count:** Unknown

The radiator has been knocking since three o'clock this morning like a trapped locksmith trying to pick the lock of this miserable radiator valve—god, I hate winter in these divided rooms. Staring at the glow of the monitor while the neighbor’s bass vibrates through the lath and plaster, I am already exhausted by the discourse awaiting Carolyn Huynh’s *Fetal Position*. Speculative fiction loves a good technological panic. And why shouldn’t it? When the real world feels like a poorly coded simulation running out of RAM, writers naturally turn to dystopian blueprints. 

Huynh trades the multi-generational diasporic family dynamics of *The Fortunes of Jaded Women* for something much colder—a near future where the state collapses into a fertility cult run by venture capitalists. 

### The Price of a Mechanical Lullaby

We are promised a biting social critique of corporate complicity, artificial life, and data-driven optimization—themes that sound remarkably like what happens when you let a silicon valley incubator design a uterus. But let us be entirely honest for a second. Or maybe let us not be honest at all, because honesty pays even worse than freelance editing—does anyone actually need another cautionary tale about start-ups exploiting desperate people? Actually, scratch that. We need it precisely because the corporate machine keeps grinding everyone down into grey paste. 

Lena Do enters a world of subsidized security and robotic butlers. A classic bargain with the devil. Except the devil wears Patagonia fleece and has a seed round backed by venture capital. 

The synopsis leans heavily into the *Black Mirror* comparison, which is industry shorthand for: expect a clever premise that twists itself into a moral pretzel by page two hundred. Huynh is dealing with heavy machinery here. Bodily autonomy. The male loneliness epidemic. The terrifying transactional nature of love when love is evaluated by an algorithm. 

And yet—wait, no, I take that back. The premise relies almost entirely on women vanishing while the protagonist plays amateur detective with a robotic butler. That feels dangerously close to a genre trope we have ingested a thousand times over in airport paperbacks. 

### The Algorithm of Despair

The real meat of the book will not be the missing women. It will be the quiet, corrosive horror of Lena realizing she preferred her soul-crushing corporate job to the gilded cage of a tech-bro utopia. Because loneliness is a massive, echoing canyon. Huynh understands this ache intimately. People will sign away their reproductive freedom simply to feel perceived by something—even if that something is a cold-eyed corporation offering a beautiful home and a machine that folds laundry. 

Or maybe the robot butler is the most human character in the book. That would be the cynical twist we deserve. 

Reading about near-futures where birthrates plummet and loneliness is commodified hits a little too close to home when you are sitting in a kitchen that smells perpetually of boiled cabbage, typing frantic critiques to pay for next month’s rent hike. 

The toddler has hidden my favorite fountain pen again.
