**Author:** Ivelisse Housman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

Faron and Ely's little secret from _Til Quest Do Us Part_ reeks of the overused "forbidden love" trope, a relic from the days of Bella and Edward. The Voice of the Thousand-Eyed Goddess, Princess Elysande Sybilla Aurelion of Estrachia, made a hasty vow to her knight under the watchful eye of a patron deity, rather like a teenager hastily exchanging nuptials via a Vegas chapel. It's as if Ivelisse Housman raided the annals of historical romances, sprinkling in some half-hearted nods to fantasy while relying on stale genre conventions.

What's most egregious, however, is the way the synopsis lazily implies that the goddess won't let Ely out of her oath easily. Easy out, much? Can't the deity, in all its divine wisdom, perhaps provide a decent narrative justification for breaking the vow? Instead, we're left with a contrived plot device, an obvious attempt to shoehorn Ely and Faron into a "star-crossed lovers" narrative. It's a tired, overplayed beat. I'll say it without hesitation: I've seen better-executed plot twists in a reductive, campy _Star Trek_ episode.

—And what's with the unnecessary focus on the queen's responsibility? Is Ely's duty to her kingdom a narrative crutch or what? A chance to indulge in some shallow, saccharine exploration of "royal duty" rather than, say, genuinely delving into the complexities of Ely's relationship with the goddess, or Faron's motivations for accepting the vow?

### The Empty Gesture

In a narrative where the characters' actions are defined by their vows, one would expect some genuine exploration of the consequences of making such pacts. Yet, it looks like we're being forced down the same old familiar path of angsty teenage love, minus the teenage angst. It's as if Ivelisse Housman is attempting to cash in on the trendiness of fantasy romances while disregarding all the thoughtful, layered storytelling that has come before (and, I dare say, in *A Court of Thorns and Roses* and *The All Souls Trilogy*, authors have managed to do it with far greater finesse).

—Like it or not, we're about to be treated to some saccharine romantic hand-holding and whatnot. The entire premise reeks of *Twilight_ and *The Vampire Bride _of Dracula_ with, at best, a few anachronistic nods to fantasy. Don't get me wrong; I adore fantasy romance, but this feels like a tired imitation. Can we at least give the characters some depth? Some complexity? Some... I don't know... *humanity_?

As I write this, I'm reminded of the 1987 Pan American Flight 747 crash in the Canary Islands, which I've always found eerily similar to the author's grasp on coherent storytelling. The parallels are uncanny, actually—*incoherence_ and _desperation_.

Sighing, I gaze out my window, where my driver is currently navigating the nightmarish traffic of our summer's end en route to the Hamptons. #FantasyRomance Fail
