**Author:** Tatiana Schlote-Bonne  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The flickering fluorescent overhead tube is buzzing at a precise, brain-melting frequency of sixty hertz, and my coffee went lukewarm forty minutes ago while I stared at yet another pitch about a teenager who accidentally summons a literal demon through an indie horror game. Tatiana Schlote-Bonne's *What Feeds Below* drops in 2026, and I am already exhausted. 

### Pixels and Penance

The premise leans hard into contemporary millennial-and-young-adult anxiety, marrying the parasocial loneliness of Twitch streaming with old-school folklore doppelgangers. We get Viv, a chronic liar drowning in survivor's guilt over her sister's fatal accident, trying to monetize her trauma for parental approval. It is standard modern YA fare dressed up in ring lights and chat box overlays. 

Or maybe it isn't. Wait, let me rephrase that. Actually, looking closer at the marketing copy, the streaming angle might just be a shallow aesthetic wrapper for a standard psychological guilt-trip thriller. Who knows. The synopsis plays it safe, hitting every expected beat of the "reformed bully finds redemption through supernatural terror" checklist. 

### The Boy Who Cried Demonic Mimic

The central conflict relies on the oldest, most irritating trope in speculative fiction: nobody believes the protagonist because she lied in the past. It is the boy who cried wolf, translated into a high-saturation digital landscape. But let us be honest about the mechanics here. The synopsis tells us absolutely nothing about how this demonic mimic actually operates mechanically, leaving us stranded in a sea of vague threat implications. 

Yet, I read it so you don't have to. Well, technically I *do* have to, because my rent is due and this miserable night shift demands another thousand words of artificial enthusiasm for unreleased manuscripts. 

If Schlote-Bonne leans away from the moralizing cheese and embraces the genuinely nasty implications of a supernatural entity wearing a liar's face, this could punch above its weight class. Think *Depraved* meets a slightly more cynical iteration of Mara Rutherford's work, stripped of the usual genre gloss. Or it could drown in its own heavy-handed messaging about the power of truth. 

I have to leave now for a terribly pretentious avant-garde film screening where three people stare at a blank wall for two hours, which somehow still sounds more stimulating than proofreading another manuscript about a gamer fighting a digital ghost.
