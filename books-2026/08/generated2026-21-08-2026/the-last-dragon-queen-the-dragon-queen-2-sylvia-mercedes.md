**Author:** Sylvia Mercedes  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-10 APL

The synopsis for *The Last Dragon Queen* reads like a fever dream someone scribbled down after three espressos and a particularly bad breakup—all the emotional whiplash of *The Song of Achilles* meets the plot armor of *The Lies of Locke Lamora*, but with the narrative discipline of a drunkard trying to recite *The Art of War*. And yet—*yet*—there’s something here that might actually work, if Mercedes can resist the urge to turn this into another *A Court of Thorns and Roses* fanfic where the only thing more predictable than the romance is the villain’s backstory.

Rosie Harpwood’s arc, as outlined, is the kind of tragic heroine setup that makes you wonder if Mercedes is channeling her inner *The Priory of the Orange Tree* but with half the ambition. The idea of a reluctant queen forced into destiny by external forces is so tired it should be a cliché by now—yet here we are, in 2026, still waiting for someone to make it feel fresh. The synopsis leans hard into the "love and loyalty" schtick, which is fine if you’re writing a YA romance where the real conflict is whether the love interest will finally kiss the protagonist in the third act. But when you’re dealing with dragons, phoenix shrines, and a sister who sounds like she was cobbled together from the leftovers of *The Poppy War*’s most unlikable characters, you’d better have more than "passions entwine with flame" to keep things interesting.

Nyxia is the real wild card here—or at least, she’s the character who might actually have some depth if Mercedes isn’t too busy treating her like a plot device with feelings. The synopsis hints at a power struggle between Nyxia and her creator, which is a fascinating dynamic if it’s handled right. But given how often these kinds of stories devolve into "oh no, the villain is secretly the protagonist’s long-lost sibling!" tropes, I’m bracing for the moment when Nyxia’s arc becomes less about her agency and more about whether she’ll "choose the light" at the last second. That is certainly a choice.

The real question is whether Mercedes can avoid the trap of making this feel like a rushed, afterthought to *The Dragon Queen*—because right now, it reads like a book that was written in a hurry to capitalize on whatever trendy "queenly power fantasy" is currently dominating the market. The synopsis doesn’t even bother to sell the stakes beyond "war ignites," which is about as compelling as a *Game of Thrones* episode where the only thing that happens is someone dies dramatically. Where’s the political maneuvering? Where’s the moral ambiguity? Where’s the sense that this isn’t just another story about a girl who becomes a queen because she’s special?

And then there’s the romance—because of course there’s the romance. Valtar, the former enemy turned reluctant love interest, is the kind of character who exists solely to make Rosie’s journey feel more dramatic. But if Mercedes can’t do better than the "enemies-to-lovers" trope that’s been mined to death since *Twilight*, then she’s already lost. The synopsis doesn’t even attempt to make this feel like anything more than a checklist: love, loyalty, fire, destiny. It’s the literary equivalent of a *Shark Tank* pitch that only mentions the product’s features and not its market.

The most damning thing about this synopsis is how little it actually says. Where’s the tension? Where’s the conflict that isn’t just "Nyxia is mad"? Where’s the sense that this isn’t just another book where the magic system is a gimmick and the characters are either love interests or red herrings? The only thing that makes this remotely interesting is the implication that Mercedes might actually pull something off—but given how often these kinds of stories fall flat, I’m not holding my breath.
