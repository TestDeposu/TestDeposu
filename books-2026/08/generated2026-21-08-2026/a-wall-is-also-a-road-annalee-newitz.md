**Author:** Annalee Newitz  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

The first time I saw a slime mold under a microscope, I was twelve and already convinced biology was a cruel joke—until I realized how *alive* it was, how it could solve mazes, remember paths, *choose*. That’s the kind of quiet rebellion *A Wall Is Also a Road* seems to be whispering about, though the synopsis leaves more questions than answers. Because here’s the thing: the moment Gardenpath, our amoeba grad student, lands on that planet and meets Murtis, the brothel worker with "sensor bulbs," the book’s entire premise starts to feel like a science fiction version of *The Princess Bride*—if Inigo Montoya were a slime mold and Westley were a sentient brothel. The problem? The synopsis doesn’t tell us whether this is a cozy, character-driven fable or a hard sci-fi deconstruction of anthropomorphism. And that’s the real tension here: Newitz has a history of blending the cerebral with the visceral (*Autonomous*—which, by the way, I still haven’t forgiven for its treatment of AI as a *person*—but also *The Future of Another Timeline*, which was delightfully messy), so I’m bracing for a story that might as well be a love letter to *Arrival*’s linguistic wonder meets *The City & The City*’s alien urbanism, but with the emotional rawness of *The Three-Body Problem*’s existential dread—if Cixin Liu had written about a grad student’s midlife crisis.

But let’s talk about the *vagueness*—oh, the *vagueness*. The synopsis promises a "thought-provoking and heartwarming" novel, yet it doesn’t clarify whether the planet’s culture is a satire of human colonialism, a celebration of queer alien kinship, or just a really well-written excuse for Gardenpath to abandon their PhD. Is Murtis a subversive figure, a tragic one, or just a really good tour guide? The lack of specificity feels intentional, like Newitz is playing with the reader’s expectations—*see, you think you know what this is, but you don’t*. And that’s fine, because the best sci-fi often thrives on ambiguity. But it also makes me wonder: is this book going to be the kind of thing that leaves you nodding along with the premise, or the kind that makes you *feel* something? Because right now, it’s just a really interesting setup for a story that could go anywhere.

And speaking of anywhere—why does this remind me of *The Good Place*’s fourth season, where the characters keep discovering that their entire moral framework was built on lies? Because Gardenpath’s entire identity as a "scientist" is about to be upended, and the synopsis doesn’t even hint at whether this is a triumphant moment or a devastating one. Is Gardenpath going to become the next Carl Sagan, or are they going to be the kind of academic who gets left behind by their own discoveries? The way the synopsis frames it—*"they're not sure what it is, but they're going to use science to find out"*—suggests a narrative that’s equal parts *Contact*’s wonder and *Annihilation*’s dread, but with the emotional core of a *Station Eleven* novel. And that’s the thing: Newitz has always been better at worldbuilding than she is at character depth, so I’m hoping this isn’t just another book where the aliens are interesting but the humans are cardboard. Because if it is, I’m going to be *so* disappointed.

But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the book is about the gap between what we think we know and what we *actually* experience. And if so, then the synopsis is doing its job perfectly—it’s leaving us with more questions than answers, which is exactly how a great sci-fi novel should start. Because the best stories aren’t about the destination; they’re about the journey of realizing you don’t know where you’re going. And if *A Wall Is Also a Road* delivers on that, then it might just be the kind of book that makes you question everything you thought you understood about science, love, and what it means to be alive.
