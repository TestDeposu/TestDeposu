The humidity hovering over my desk right now is genuinely offensive, a thick, stagnant blanket of heat thanks to an office air conditioning unit that apparently died sometime around the Clinton administration. And yet, I am expected to sit here and wax poetic about a winter wonderland. Emily Stone is back with *Counting Down to You*, and my internal thermostat is sweating just looking at the press release. 

### The Architecture of Grief and Advent Calendars

The premise leans heavily on the contemporary obsession with structured emotional recovery. A dead best friend. A posthumous countdown. It is the literary equivalent of a Hallmark movie directed by someone who read Joan Didion once and decided grief needs a schedule. Delete your ex. Tell the guy you like him. Throw out the scales. It is quirky grief management. Why must every deadpan emotional crisis be solved by an itinerary? I swear, if I have to read one more contemporary romance where trauma is unlocked via a scavenger hunt or a series of index cards left behind by a deceased loved one, I am going to throw my iced coffee directly through the drywall. Give me messy people who just cry in their cars instead of organizing a whimsical, life-altering December ski trip to the Alps. 

### Fake Dating and Frosty Bosses

Enter Leo. The stoic, seriously hot boss. Of course he is stoic. He cannot just be a regular guy with mild emotional availability; he has to be an iceberg waiting for the right woman's stubborn sunshine to melt him. The fake dating trope is doing an Olympic-level sprint here. Co-workers assume they are an item, he plays along to save her from awkwardness, and suddenly they are sharing longing glances over Vin Chaud. Emily Stone made a career out of timing our collective tears—remember *Always, in December*? Exactly. You know the machinery. You know the emotional beats. But I am fiercely rooting against the third-act misunderstanding fueled by undisclosed trauma. Just communicate. Adults should not need an Advent calendar to tell each other the truth. 

This reminds me entirely of that aggressively mid-tier mystery novel I read last month where the protagonist solved a decades-old murder entirely by finding color-coded sticky notes in a dead architect's attic. Who leaves sticky notes? Who has the stationary budget for that level of retroactive plotting? It insults the reader's intelligence. At least Stone usually commits to the emotional carnage, unlike those sanitized YA adaptations Netflix churns out where the heaviest burden any teenager carries is choosing which Ivy League sweater to wear. 

### The Final Verdict on the Frost

Stone writes well enough to make us tolerate the manufactured contrivances of the plot. But I demand emotional realism beneath the alpine aesthetic. If Leo's tragic backstory turns out to be something profoundly stupid like a failed pastry shop in Zurich, I am returning my galley copy to the void. 

Hang on. I hear the unmistakable sound of a body slamming into the drywall from the hallway, followed immediately by a six-year-old shouting about who owns the plastic lightsaber. I have to go break up a full-scale gladiatorial combat match in the living room before someone loses an eye.
