**Author:** Elizabeth Hand  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-13 APL

The dust on my library's first edition of *Orlando* remains undisturbed, yet Elizabeth Hand already threatens to disrupt the shelf-wobbling equilibrium with *Unspeakable Things*. And honestly? We deserve a period-piece thriller that completely rots our brains with obsessive sapphic carnage. 

Hand is trading the claustrophobic dread of *A Haunting on the Hill* for a windswept 1920s moor, dragging the classic Gothic tradition out into the blinding, hedonistic glare of the "bright young things." A working-class girl tutored alongside her social better? Groundbreaking. Or perhaps entirely derivative—until Hand twists the knife. Or wait. Actually, the class dynamic between Olivia and Tamsin feels profoundly tired, a dusty parlor trick pulled straight from the Victorian attic. Let us be entirely serious. We have read this exact British class-warfare trauma porn in every mid-tier literary fiction release since Virginia Woolf put down her pen. 

### The Aesthetics of Ruin

Desire as a weapon. How deliciously mid-century, yet entirely cringe when weaponized by modern marketing copywriters trying to sell books to TikTok. But let us look past the marketing. The synopsis promises an intoxicating collision of societal claustrophobia and bohemian hedonism at Ledges, the elusive estate of Rex de Voil. 

Richly evoking a vibrant time in queer history—or so the blurbs scream—the text balances on a razor's edge between high art and absolute trash. I am living for the mess. Give me the messy queer trauma, you cowards. Hand possesses the stylistic chops to elevate what could easily be a pulpy lesbian murder-romance into something genuinely feral. Yet the synopsis suffers from a maddening vagueness, hiding behind atmospheric adjectives like *propulsive* and *knife-edged* without offering a single concrete narrative anchor beyond an unexplained death. 

### The Price of Freedom

Nothing in Rex's world is without cost. Naturally. Because freedom in the Roaring Twenties always comes wrapped in a silk-stringed noose, financed by dubious capital and fueled by bathtub gin. Hand is clearly interrogating the transactional nature of autonomy for women who dared to exist outside patriarchal parameters. But she's doing it while hiding behind vague plot points that give me zero confidence in the actual mechanics of the third act. 

Expect this to land somewhere between *Rebecca* and Patricia Highsmith on a bender. It is going to be the literary equivalent of eating raw honeycomb—rich, sticky, and deeply sickening if consumed too fast. Or perhaps it will completely bomb, a glossy period piece drowning in its own atmospheric menace. 

The delivery truck carrying my bespoke Italian leather chesterfield is currently idling in the gravel driveway, and the driver looks aggressively incapable of reversing down the lane without crushing my prize hydrangeas.
