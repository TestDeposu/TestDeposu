**Author:** David Rosenfelt  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-13 APL

The synopsis for *Bark Humbug* reads like a holiday special for the genre’s most stubbornly cynical audience—think if *The Thin Man* and *Fargo* had a lovechild, then dressed it in a Santa hat and forced it to solve a murder during a media frenzy. The problem? It’s not just another Christmas mystery—it’s a mystery about Christmas itself, or at least the way it’s weaponized by desperate people and opportunistic journalists. Sharon Lowery’s scheme to lure Andy Carpenter into her brother’s legal mess is less about justice and more about exploiting the season’s sentimental underbelly. The newspaper’s "celebrity and their dogs" spread isn’t just a photo op—it’s a trap, a way to turn Andy’s well-documented disdain for the holiday into leverage. And yet, here he is, already halfway to agreeing because—of course—he can’t resist a dog story, even if it’s wrapped in a murder investigation.

The synopsis leans hard into the trope of the reluctant hero, but where most mysteries make the reluctance feel like a quirk, Rosenfelt’s Andy Carpenter’s disdain is almost a character trait in itself. The idea that he’s *serious* about retiring is funny because we know he won’t be. The football game interruption isn’t just a plot device—it’s a metaphor for how easily Andy’s life gets hijacked by chaos, even when he’s trying to opt out. But the real tension isn’t just in the murder investigation—it’s in the fact that Jon Lowery’s case is so volatile, so *public*, that Andy’s involvement could turn into a circus. The synopsis hints at a web of conspiracies, but it doesn’t say whether those conspiracies are political, personal, or just the kind of media-driven nonsense that makes Andy’s job so infuriating.

Yet—and this is where the synopsis starts to feel like it’s playing it too safe—the mention of "a web of conspiracies" is vague enough to feel like a placeholder for whatever Rosenfelt wants to throw at Andy next. It could be a classic *Andy Carpenter* conspiracy: a cover-up by the political elite, a corrupt law enforcement agency, or even a dog-related scam (because of course). But it could also be something more personal, something that forces Andy to confront his own complicity in the system he’s spent years critiquing. The synopsis doesn’t give us enough to tell if this is going to be a straightforward whodunit or if it’s going to dig into the darker corners of Andy’s own morality. And that’s the problem—it’s too easy to predict the surface-level plot, but the real payoff in Rosenfelt’s books usually comes from how Andy’s personal life collides with the case.

The synopsis also does a decent job of setting up the stakes, but it doesn’t do enough to make us care about the characters beyond Andy and Sharon. Jon Lowery’s desperation is clear, but we don’t get a sense of why he’s running or what he’s willing to do to stay free. Sharon’s dedication is framed as admirable, but it’s also framed as something that could get her brother killed. The synopsis doesn’t ask us to root for either of them—it just sets up the conflict. And that’s fine, because Rosenfelt’s strength is in the tension between Andy’s cynicism and the people who still believe in something, even if it’s just their brother’s innocence.

But then—here’s the pivot—what if the real conspiracy isn’t political or personal, but *literary*? What if Rosenfelt is playing with the expectations of the Christmas mystery genre itself? The synopsis mentions Andy’s "classic disdain for Christmas," but it doesn’t say whether that disdain is going to be the heart of the story or just another layer of irony. If Rosenfelt leans into the idea that the holiday itself is a tool for manipulation—whether by the media, by desperate families, or by the people who profit from the season’s emotional labor—then this could be something special. But if it’s just another case where Andy has to save the day despite himself, then it might feel like a missed opportunity.

The synopsis doesn’t give us enough to tell, but that’s the point. Rosenfelt’s books thrive on ambiguity, on the idea that the real mystery isn’t just who did it, but why Andy keeps getting pulled into these messes. And if *Bark Humbug* is anything like the rest of the series, it’s going to be less about the solution and more about the ride—Andy’s frustration, his reluctant heroics, and the way the world keeps throwing dogs and scandals at him like Christmas presents he didn’t ask for.
