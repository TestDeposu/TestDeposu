A century after Agatha Christie's infamous disappearance, the world's most famous mystery author remains an enigma - and the genre she defined is still replete with unanswered questions. *Eleven Days*, Laura Thompson's forthcoming book, attempts to penetrate the blank space in Christie's life, to shed light on a myth that has tantalizingly remained unsolved.

But what can we really expect from this re-examination of Christie's movements? Thompson's decision to re-enact Christie's journey, from abandoning her car to arriving at a hotel in the north of England, seems like an intriguing approach - but will it yield any real insights? The synopsis is maddeningly vague, and I'm left wondering if Thompson's conversations with people close to Christie, including her daughter Rosalind, will amount to anything more than speculation.

And yet, I'm drawn to Thompson's exploration of the notion of disappearance itself - how it might feel to wander like a ghost through one's own life, to be oneself yet without identity. This is the stuff of which mysteries are made, after all. What sort of person has the capacity to disappear? Many of us may have longed to do so, but actually to go through with it? What courage or daring does it take, what desperation or deviousness?

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The real question is, will *Eleven Days* deliver the final word on the greatest mystery ever created by the greatest writer of mysteries? I have my doubts. Thompson's approach seems too focused on re-enactment, too reliant on speculation - and not enough on the actual facts of Christie's disappearance. Still, I suppose that's the nature of the beast - the truth about Christie's disappearance may never be fully known, and so we're left to pick at the edges of the mystery, to speculate and theorize.

And that's precisely what Thompson seems to be doing - exploring the various theories about Christie's disappearance, from the plausible to the outlandish. I'm reminded of Ruth Rendell's own explorations of the human psyche, her willingness to probe the darker corners of the human experience. Will Thompson's *Eleven Days* be a similarly nuanced exploration of the human condition? I'm not so sure.

But what I do know is that Thompson's book will be a must-read for fans of Christie, and for anyone interested in the art of mystery itself. And who knows? Maybe Thompson will surprise us all, and shed some real light on the mystery that has haunted us for so long.

—But I'm not holding my breath.

The more I think about it, the more I'm convinced that *Eleven Days* will be a fascinating, if ultimately unsatisfying, exploration of Christie's disappearance. It's a bit like trying to grasp a handful of sand - the more you squeeze, the more it slips through your fingers.
