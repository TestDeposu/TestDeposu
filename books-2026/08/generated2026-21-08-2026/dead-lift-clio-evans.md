**Page Count:** Unknown

Bodies are notoriously heavy. Or so the publishing industry insists, anyway. Clio Evans is about to test that physics axiom with *Dead Lift*, a purported serial killer romcom that leans heavily into the dark, cynical humor currently dominating our nocturnal reading lists. And look, I am supposed to write five hundred words on this upcoming release just so the editorial quota is met for the week, staring blankly at a screen while someone's elbow bumps violently against my spine every time this carriage rounds the corner at 14th Street. Why do I do this to myself? 

Let us dissect the premise. A forensic scientist—let us call her Shelby, because the copy insists on it—decides that the legal system is entirely too slow when it comes to avenging a sibling. Groundbreaking? Not particularly. But the tonal whiplash promised here is delicious. We are looking at a collision between the sterile, fluorescent-lit paranoia of *Lights Out* and the blood-soaked, feral charm of *Butcher & Blackbird* by Brynne Weaver. Yet, wait—actually, forget the Brynne Weaver comparison. Romance involving amateur body disposal is becoming its own suffocating subgenre right now, and if Evans does not inject genuine narrative peril into this dynamic, the whole enterprise risks devolving into a sanitized aesthetic exercise for BookTok. 

### Anatomy of a Henchman

The inclusion of the "hot himbo" trope changes the ideological stakes of the narrative entirely. Brad—blond, muscle-bound, and alarmingly eager to assist in homicide—transforms a standard vigilante revenge arc into a workplace comedy with exceptionally high stakes. Power dynamics inverted. The brilliant, cynical woman completely undermined by a man whose primary asset is his raw, unbridled willingness to carry heavy objects. 

Romance readers crave competence porn. This book offers incompetence porn, coated in arterial spray and biceps. 

Every good villain needs a henchman. True. But the structural risk here is tonal collapse. Can an author genuinely balance the grief of a murdered sister with a meet-cute over a corpse? Absolutely. If the prose maintains a razor-sharp edge. The synopsis plays it safe, leaning entirely on the marketing hook rather than giving us a glimpse of the internal architecture of Shelby's mind. We know she understands forensics. We know she lacks upper-body strength. What we do not know—because the blurb keeps its cards clutched tightly to its chest—is whether Evans can sustain the dark comedy across an entire novel without the romance turning toothless. 

We will see in 2026. Until then, I am closing this tab before the person next to me reads my shoulder-surfing commentary on murderous fictional boyfriends.
