Diplomacy has always been a fragile theater of quiet threats and polite poisoning. Yet, looking at the diplomatic corps currently stationed in Geneva, one realizes we have lost our appetite for true performance. We hide behind spreadsheets and subdued sighs. Elliot Martyn’s upcoming *History's Greatest Drinking Games* arrives like a sudden artillery shell through a drawing-room window, reminding us that our ancestors possessed a far more rigorous—and entertaining—method for destroying empires over a few fermented gallons. 

### The Anthropology of Ruin

The premise is deceptively simple. A chronological survey of how humanity has reliably pickled its collective brain across millennia. Martyn promises a grand tour of ancient foolishness, from Greeks chucking unwatered Bordeaux across marble halls to medieval Norsemen weaponizing insults. But let us be entirely serious. This is not high historiography. It reads like the literary equivalent of the Fyre Festival—all grand promises of exotic locations and high-end curation, masking a desperate scramble to keep the ice from melting. 

### The Domestic Archaeology of Bad Decisions

Consider the thematic implications. The publisher’s blurb leans heavily on the comforting cliché that "we've always been idiots." How remarkably comforting for the modern technocrat. We are invited to chuckle at the quaint, primitive absurdity of our forebears, as if the boardrooms of Davos are operated by enlightened philosopher-kings rather than stressed executives playing corporate Russian roulette with venture capital. Martyn wants us to view these drinking rituals as whimsical anomalies rather than the structural foundation of human governance. 

And yet, the structural ambition of the text feels desperately thin. A few anecdotes about competitive wine-throwing do not a cultural history make. It relies entirely on the same tired, mildly amusing trivia one might overhear at a mediocre hotel bar in Berlin while waiting for a delayed flight to Zurich. It possesses all the intellectual weight of a hastily compiled listicle masquerading as a hardcover volume destined for the remainder bins of Heathrow. 

### Architectural Follies

One must question the utility of recreating these historical disasters, as the synopsis cheerfully suggests. Are we truly meant to stage a Viking insult contest in a London pied-à-terre? The carpets are imported silk, for heaven's sake. The sheer logistical nightmare of medieval intoxication translated into contemporary drawing rooms escapes the author entirely. Martyn writes with the breezy detachment of someone who has never had to negotiate the cleanup fees after a minor archduke mistakes an antique credenza for a launching pad. 

The literary landscape is already saturated with this sort of pedestrian anthropology. We have endured enough mildly irreverent histories that treat human suffering as a charming quirk for the commuter train. If I wanted to watch civilization unravel through catastrophic intoxication, I would simply review the architectural blueprints for the penthouse triplex I foolishly acquired in Manhattan before the market corrected itself into oblivion. The plumbing alone is a multi-million-dollar weeping wound, and looking at those structural estimates makes the prospect of ancient Greek projectile drinking look positively methodical by comparison.
