**Author:** Matthew Salesses  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

The synopsis for *To Grieve Is to Carry Another Time* arrives like a poorly sealed letter—promising intimacy but leaving the ink smudged at the edges. Salesses, that ever-so-slightly smug chronicler of craft and wonder, has finally turned his lens inward, but the result feels less like a memoir and more like a *very* expensive journal entry from a man who’s been told grief is a genre he should explore. The title itself is a cliché given the dignity of a funeral home’s mission statement—*grief as a burden to be carried*, as if it weren’t already the weight of a thousand unanswered emails from the dead.

Adoption, Asian American identity, marriage to a Korean woman, fatherhood, widowhood—check, check, check, check, *check*. It’s the literary equivalent of a buffet where every course is labeled “Trauma Lite.” The synopsis doesn’t so much *weave* these identities as it does stack them like dominoes, waiting for the inevitable collapse. Where’s the tension? Where’s the *art*? The synopsis reads like a résumé bullet point: *“Wrote about loss (but not really, just *about* it).”* And yet—there’s a kernel of something here, buried under the self-congratulatory dust of “meditation” and “philosophies.” The question isn’t whether Salesses can write about grief—it’s whether he can write *through* it, not just *about* it.

The real risk—and the real opportunity—lies in the tension between the author’s public persona (the professor of craft, the cheerleader for storytelling as salvation) and the private one (the man who lost his wife to cancer, who now must confront the lie that words alone can heal). The synopsis hints at this, but it’s all too neat: *“new paths, new stories, new philosophies.”* It sounds like a TED Talk, not a memoir. Where’s the mess? Where’s the moment when Salesses realizes he’s been writing about grief as a *subject* rather than as the thing that’s been rewriting him? The synopsis doesn’t dare suggest that the book might fail—because if it does, it’ll just be another data point in Salesses’ career of *successful* failures.

But let’s cut to the chase: this isn’t *The Liars’ Club* or *The Year of Magical Thinking*—it’s more like *The Notebook* if Nicholas Sparks had a panic attack and then wrote a thank-you note to his editor. The synopsis avoids the pitfalls of *too* personal (no explicit details of Cathreen’s illness, no raw hospital scenes) but also avoids the pitfalls of *not* personal enough (no real stakes beyond the abstract “new ways of understanding the world”). It’s the literary equivalent of a LinkedIn post: *“I’ve been through a lot, and now I’m offering you a workshop on resilience.”*

Yet—here’s the thing. Salesses has spent his career arguing that storytelling is a *craft*, not a cure. So if this book is half the sharp, half the self-aware as his nonfiction, it might just be the first memoir that doesn’t pretend grief is a lesson to be learned but a language to be spoken, however poorly. The synopsis doesn’t tell us if he’ll pull it off, but it does tell us he’s trying to write something that isn’t just *about* loss but *in* loss—something that doesn’t just describe the wound but bleeds through the pages.

And that, my friends, is the only thing worth waiting for.
