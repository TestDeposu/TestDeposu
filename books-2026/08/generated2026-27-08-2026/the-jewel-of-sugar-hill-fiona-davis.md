**Author:** Fiona Davis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Fiona Davis is back to rattling the bones of Gotham’s architectural history with *The Jewel of Sugar Hill*. Because nothing quite screams elite summer reading like another dual-timeline exercise involving a dead body in a heritage-listed foyer. We get it. Old houses hold secrets. They whisper in the floorboards. They also require exorbitant property taxes that usually drive the heirs to drink. 

### The Real Estate Portfolio of the Damned

Let us dissect the premise. On one side, we have Eliza Brown in the 1800s, weaponizing Aaron Burr to conquer a high society that clearly possesses better taste than to let her in voluntarily. On the other, 2010 brings us disgraced architect Abby Dorrance—because every protagonist in modern historical-adjacent fiction must carry the heavy cross of familial disgrace—investigating her developer father’s unfortunate gravity-assisted demise at the Morris-Jumel Mansion. 

Groundbreaking. Truly, give the Pulitzer to the typesetter. 

Take notes, everyone. If your fictional father falls off a balcony in a crumbling uptown relic, simply channel your inner disgraced visionary and solve the murder yourself while pondering the ephemeral nature of square footage. 

### Dual-Timeline Fatigue and Other Afflictions

The synopsis leans heavily on the tired trope of two ambitious women, centuries apart, fighting for space on their own terms. It is the literary equivalent of the Fyre Festival. Endless hype, questionable logistics, and a vague sense that we have all been here before. We are promised "scandalous" behavior, yet the synopsis plays it safer than a Vanguard index fund. Give me actual malice. Give me ruin. Instead, we are subjected to the standard-issue historical uplift where pluck conquers all and ambition is treated like a virtue rather than a personality disorder. 

Beatrix von Storch would weep at the sheer predictability of these narrative arcs. 

Davis knows her formula. The faithful will undoubtedly preorder this monstrosity for their sprawling marble kitchen islands, sipping chilled Sancerre while pretending to care about upper Manhattan real estate circa 1765. Yet, one yearns for a bit of narrative danger. Something that doesn't smell quite so strongly of the HarperCollins marketing department. Will Abby save the mansion? Will Eliza secure her mortgage through sheer gall and a founding father? The suspense is entirely negligible. 

Reservations at Le Bernardin wait for no one, and my driver is currently idling downstairs.
