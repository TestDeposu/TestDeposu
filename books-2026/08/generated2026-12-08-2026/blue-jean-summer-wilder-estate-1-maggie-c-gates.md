**Author:** Maggie C. Gates  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Red lockers slamming at 7:52 AM always sound like a cheap gunshot, echoing down cinderblock hallways that smell perpetually of industrial pine cleaner and unwashed gym socks. Kids pass me in the corridor, dragging heavy canvas backpacks loaded with dead weight—chromebooks with shattered screens, crumpled permission slips, and paperback copies of *The Outsiders* that haven't been opened once. 

Which brings us squarely to Maggie C. Gates and her upcoming manuscript *Blue Jean Summer*—or wait, the synopsis mentions both *Blue Jean Summer* and *How to Stay Invisible* in a dizzying identity crisis that proofreaders should have caught months ago. Ah, the glorious chaos of pre-publication metadata where publishers throw random middle-grade titles at a wall and hope a stray buzzword like "high-stakes" sticks to the drywall. 

Look. I watch twelve-year-olds stare blankly at a blank Google Doc for forty minutes straight because a semicolon frightened them. The notion that a kid named Raymond gets abandoned in a trailer park trailer, sets up camp in the school woods with a library survival guide, and befriends a curious coyote stretches the suspension of disbelief tighter than a pair of skinny jeans on a track star after pasta night. 

Gary Paulsen earned his stripes with *Hatchet*. He made the wilderness gritty, terrifying, and utterly devoid of whimsical woodland sidekicks who show up right when the plot needs an emotional pivot. 

And don't even get me started on the absolute absurdity of the district literacy curriculum this year. We spent three weeks analyzing a graphic novel about a sentient piece of toast while actual reading comprehension scores plummet into the Mariana Trench, and now I am supposed to get jazzed about a kid eating berries behind the middle school bleachers because his free-wheeling parents decided parenting was too much of a hassle? Give me a break. Give me Jerry Spinelli or give me silence. 

Wilderness survival with a canine companion and a library book sounds less like a gritty middle-grade existential crisis and more like a rejected pitch for an after-school special directed by someone who has never stepped foot outside of a Brooklyn coffee shop. 

Predictable.

Right after the final bell rings, I have to stand on a damp synthetic turf field for two agonizing hours and explain to ten-year-olds for the third consecutive week that running directly into the path of a speeding soccer ball does not count as defense.
