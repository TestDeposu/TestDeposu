**Author:** K.A. Riley  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Lion and the Impala*—which, by the way, is being marketed as the first installment in *The Resistance Trilogy* despite the title not even *hinting* at resistance—is so painfully reductive it reads like a rejected children’s encyclopedia entry. The premise is a classic "curious cub asks questions" trope, the kind of thing that would’ve made *Watership Down*’s readers roll their eyes in the ‘70s. But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t *tell* us anything about the book’s actual ambition. It doesn’t even *try*. It just lists a series of educational asides—lion diets, herbivore vs. carnivore distinctions—as if the moral were the only thing that matters, which is where the real problem lies.

The moral, of course, is the "grass isn’t greener" cliché, delivered with the subtlety of a sledgehammer. But the synopsis doesn’t *show* how this is subverted or complicated—if it even is. The father’s response is framed as a "delightful exchange," which is either a massive understatement or a sign that the book is so shallow it can’t handle irony. Compare this to something like *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe*, where the moral is buried in the world-building, not hammered into the reader like a lesson plan. Here, the entire synopsis reads like a parent’s summary of a bedtime story they half-remembered. Where’s the tension? Where’s the *resistance*? The title promises a trilogy, but the synopsis doesn’t even acknowledge that the lion cub’s curiosity might lead to something beyond a simple food-chain lesson.

And yet—here’s where I pivot—maybe that’s the point. Maybe *The Resistance Trilogy* isn’t about rebellion at all, but about the *illusion* of rebellion. The lion cub’s fascination with impalas isn’t just about diet; it’s about *choice*, about the fantasy of opting out of predation entirely. That’s a weirdly modern framing for a children’s book—almost like the author is nodding to the way kids today are taught to question systems, only to realize the system is the only thing holding them together. It’s the kind of meta-commentary you’d expect from *The Overstory*’s ecological paranoia, but condensed into a fable. But then again, the synopsis doesn’t *show* that. It just *tells* us the cub thinks impalas have it better, which is the literary equivalent of saying, "Here’s a problem, now solve it yourself."

The real crime here is the vagueness. The synopsis doesn’t even *attempt* to sell the book as more than a morality tale. It doesn’t mention the "resistance" in the title, doesn’t hint at political subtext, doesn’t suggest that the lion cub’s curiosity might be dangerous. It’s as if the author is afraid to commit to anything beyond the surface-level lesson. That’s not how trilogies work. That’s not how *anything* works. Even *Watership Down* had rabbits plotting a revolution—this is just a lion cub asking about snacks.

I’m not crying, you’re crying.

The only way this makes sense is if *The Resistance Trilogy* is a satire of the very idea of resistance, a book that starts with a cub’s naive question and ends with the realization that the system is the only thing keeping anyone alive. That would be bold. That would be *something*. But the synopsis doesn’t give us that. It gives us a checklist of facts, a moral, and a promise of a trilogy that might not even exist beyond this first book. It’s the literary equivalent of a Netflix trailer that only shows the first five minutes of the first episode.

I need to reorganize my towering stacks of reference books before I lose what little patience I have left for this kind of thing.
