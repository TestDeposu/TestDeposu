**Author:** Ally Zetterberg  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-08 APL

The sun bleeds through the blinds like a lazy editor’s red pen—just enough to make the air feel thick with the kind of heat that clings to the spine of a book you’ve already judged before you’ve even cracked it open. *The Dating Game* arrives with the kind of synopsis that smells like a board game store after a particularly chaotic game night: half strategy, half chaos, all wrapped in the kind of moral ambiguity that makes you wonder if the author’s ever actually played *Clue* without laughing. Tilda Smith, our protagonist, is a board game designer—someone who treats life like a turn-based strategy game where the stakes are *your own sanity*. And yet, the synopsis doesn’t tell us whether she’s a genius architect of her own fate or just another woman who’s decided to weaponize her own rules because the dating app algorithm keeps sending her men who look like they’d rather be in a *Married at First Sight* episode than in a real conversation.

The problem with this setup is that it’s *too* neat. Tilda’s life is structured like a game board—friendship game, dating game, end game—because, let’s be honest, that’s the kind of narrative shorthand that sells these days. It’s the same logic as *Gone Girl*’s unreliable narrator or *The Girl on the Train*’s protagonist who’s *definitely* not the villain. But where those books leaned into psychological depth, *The Dating Game* seems to be banking on the idea that a woman who designs games is *automatically* a woman who’ll design her own revenge. And that’s where the synopsis stumbles—because revenge isn’t a game mechanic, it’s a messy, unpredictable thing. The closest we get to nuance is Tilda’s moment of "getting just a bit carried away," which is either a brilliant understatement or a lazy way to imply she’s about to become a noir protagonist. Either way, it’s not enough.

The real question is whether this will be a *Gone Girl*-style twist or a *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo* procedural. The synopsis hints at a cat-and-mouse game—someone watching Tilda, playing their own game—but it doesn’t say whether this is a love triangle, a conspiracy, or just Tilda’s own ego getting the better of her. And that’s the thing about modern thrillers: they either deliver a *sharp* twist or they deliver a *predictable* one. Right now, this feels like it’s trying to be both, which is a recipe for disappointment. Consider *The Silent Patient* or *Sharp Objects*—books where the psychological unraveling is the real game, not the external plot. Here, the synopsis suggests Tilda’s downfall will come from her own rules, but it doesn’t tell us if that’s a tragedy or a triumph. And that’s the kind of ambiguity that either makes a book or breaks it.

The thematic potential is there—modern dating as a minefield, the female rage that’s been simmering in literature since *The Bell Jar* but finally getting its own genre (thanks, *You*—but let’s not pretend this isn’t just another iteration of the "woman scorned" trope). The issue is that the synopsis doesn’t do enough to distinguish itself from the glut of female-led thrillers that have flooded the market since *Gone Girl* proved you could sell a book on the back of a single, well-timed twist. If this is going to be a *Girl on the Train* with a board game twist, it better have more than just a protagonist who treats life like a game. It needs to ask: *What does it mean to play by your own rules when the rules are rigged against you?* And right now, the synopsis doesn’t even hint at that complexity—it just promises chaos.

I am looking respectfully at the clock, because the longer I sit here, the more I realize this book might just be another entry in the "woman loses it and becomes a villain" canon, which is fine if you’re going for *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo*’s dark humor or *Gone Girl*’s razor-sharp satire. But if it’s just going to be a woman who snaps and then gets outsmarted by someone else, well—where’s the edge? Where’s the *twist*? The synopsis leaves me wondering if this is going to be a *Sharp Objects* where the horror is psychological or a *The Girl on the Train* where the horror is just that the protagonist is an idiot. And honestly? That’s not enough to make me wait for September.

The weather’s turning—humid, that lazy August heat that makes you want to sit in a dark room and read something that doesn’t require you to think too hard. But if *The Dating Game* delivers on the promise of a protagonist who’s both the architect of her own downfall and the victim of someone else’s game, then maybe it’ll be worth the wait. Or maybe it’ll just be another book where a woman’s rage is the only thing that makes the story interesting, and that’s a tired trope if there ever was one.

Now, if I don’t salvage that mid-century chair from the sidewalk before the rain hits, I’m going to regret it. And honestly, that’s more of a game I can play.
