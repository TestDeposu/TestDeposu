**Author:** Katie Naymon  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

The synopsis for *In Every Future, It’s Us* arrives like a well-wrapped gift—elegant in its simplicity, with just enough ribbon to tease what’s inside. But the more you unravel it, the more you realize the ribbon itself might be the point. A New Year’s Eve game. Secret predictions. A decade of unspoken rules. It’s the kind of premise that could easily collapse into saccharine nostalgia or a clichéd "will they/won’t they" trope, but the way Naymon frames it—with that final, shattered letter hanging like a ghost between them—suggests she’s playing with something more dangerous than sentimentality. She’s playing with *time*, or at least the illusion of it.

The structure here is a masterclass in how to make a story feel both intimate and expansive. Past and present timelines aren’t just a gimmick; they’re a mirror. Florence and Tom’s friendship, once a fortress of shared secrets, now feels like a house with a single broken window. The game they invented to bind them—five predictions a year, one life-changing decision for the winner—was never just a game. It was a way to control the narrative, to ensure that no matter how life diverged, they’d always have a thread. But threads fray. And when the final letter arrives, it doesn’t just correct a guess; it *ends* one. The synopsis doesn’t say what was written, but the omission is telling. It’s the kind of silence that makes you lean in, like a character in a novel you’re desperate to read.

Yet—and here’s where the synopsis stumbles—there’s a vagueness that feels deliberate, but also lazy. We know Florence is moving away. We know there’s tension. But what’s the *weight* of that tension? Is it the kind that comes from unrequited love, or from two people who’ve outgrown each other? Is Tom’s silence about the letter a refusal to apologize, or a refusal to admit he’s been wrong all along? The synopsis doesn’t say. It’s the difference between a novel that *feels* like a conversation with a friend and one that feels like a menu where you’re only given the prices, not the flavors. Compare this to *The Flatshare* by Beth O’Leary, where the emotional stakes are so vivid you can taste the bitterness of unspoken words. Here, we’re left with the *shape* of the story, not the texture.

But then—*and this is the pivot*—maybe that’s the point. Maybe the beauty of *In Every Future, It’s Us* isn’t in the specifics, but in the *idea* of two people who’ve spent a lifetime predicting each other’s futures, only to realize they’ve never actually *seen* them. The synopsis doesn’t need to spell it out. It just needs to make you *feel* the ache of that realization. And if Naymon pulls it off, this could be the kind of book that lingers like a half-remembered dream—something you can’t quite place your finger on, but can’t stop thinking about.

Literally.

The problem isn’t the premise. It’s the execution. Or rather, the *promise* of execution. Because right now, it’s just a setup. A beautifully crafted setup, sure, but a setup nonetheless. The real question isn’t whether this will be a romance—it’s whether it will be a *good* one. And for that, we’ll need more than just the ghost of a friendship and the hollow echo of a game. We’ll need the kind of emotional precision that makes you forget you’re reading a book and remember you’re living in someone else’s head.

I’m holding out hope it’s the former. But if it’s not, I’ll be the one who has to construct a diorama of the solar system before midnight.
