**Author:** Tanaz Bhathena  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-22 APL

My portfolio manager thinks I should buy more municipal bonds to soothe my insomnia. Instead, I am staring down Tanaz Bhathena’s *Witch Daughter*, a literary excavation of ancient Persia that promises a heavy dose of court intrigue and inevitable doom. Because when my hundred-million-dollar acquisition targets are currently rotting in international customs, nothing cures an existential void quite like a doomed royal dynasty tearing itself apart over prophecies. 

We are promised Tahmineh: temptress, witch, victim, or misunderstood girlboss. Pick your poison. The marketing copy leans hard on the misunderstood angle, but honestly, give me the witch. Give me a woman who actually embraces the storm cloud instead of weeping into her silk pillows about a lack of suitors. The synopsis smells faintly of the kind of sanitized historical revisionism that dominated the early two-thousand-thousands book club circuits—think Khaled Hosseini meets a YA fantasy crossover engineered by committee. Yet, I am strangely intrigued. 

### The Architecture of Inevitable Ruin

Let us look at the mechanics of this tragedy. Princess Tahmineh hears a prophecy, ignores the fine print, marries the warrior Rostam, breeds a son, and then discovers the Universe has a dark sense of humor regarding father-son combat. It has the exact energy of the House of Saud boardroom drama mixed with a Greek tragedy written by an exhausted ghostwriter. 

Wait. Scratch that. The prophecy angle is entirely overplayed. We do not need mystical warnings to make bad marital choices; humans manage that daily without divine intervention. 

Bhathena is stepping out of her contemporary lane for her adult debut here, and I expect teeth. If this turns into a meandering historical romance with soft-focus tent scenes and mild political posturing, I will demand a refund from my literary agent. We need blood on the carpet. We need the kind of visceral, psychological desolation that makes Joe Abercrombie look like a children’s television executive. 

### The Mythology of Martyrdom

Ancient Persia offers a bottomless pit of structural violence, dynastic paranoia, and absolute cruelty. If Bhathena leans into that darkness—if she strips away the poetic gloss advertised by the blurbs—this could salvage my Tuesday evening. The blurbs are entirely unhelpful, naturally. Jodi Picoult comparing a Persian legend to suburban legal drama is roughly equivalent to pairing a 1982 Château Lafite-Rothschild with a gas-station Slim Jim. Unhinged. 

And yet, the core premise of a mother attempting to rewrite cosmic code to save her child carries a grim, inevitable weight. It reminds me of the Titanic deckchair rearrangement at Davos. Futile, expensive, and deeply tragic. 

The heated seats in my SUV are malfunctioning on the lumbar support, which is an absolute tragedy.
