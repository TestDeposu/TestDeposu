**Author:** Lauren Willig  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

The treaty table was always easier to manage when the opposing signatories were simply greedy rather than possessed by ancient, Gilded Age malice. Lauren Willig drops us straight into the limestone mausoleums of Brooklyn in *What Happens at Nightfall*, and honestly, the diplomatic immunity of the deceased is about to be violently revoked. Send help. 

Jess is down on her luck—laid off, untethered, and dragged into a nocturnal excursion among the dead by a well-meaning friend. Typical. But then she finds a strange, ancient key. And a gaggle of terrified boys. And a distractingly competent arborist named Adam who is trying to raise grieving children while wrestling with municipal flora and generational curses. 

### The Mortuary Bureaucracy of Grief

We are looking at a classic structural friction here. On one side, the suffocating weight of domestic widowhood and structural unemployment. On the other, literal demons rising from a century-long slumber because nobody bothered to read the fine print on the Vanderwilt family's real estate deed. It gives me serious vibes of T. Kingfisher meeting a gothic rom-com at a cocktail party where everyone is heavily medicated. Yet, I worry the supernatural elements will get sidelined for endless banter about whether Adam can handle his kids' existential dread while flirting near a headstone. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that entirely. The banter is actually the entire point of the exercise, so scrap my previous worry about narrative focus. 

Willig is leaning hard into the historical-paranormal zeitgeist currently dominating the bestseller lists—think of the cozy yet lethal energy we saw in *The Very Secret Society of Irregular Witches*, scaled up to a sprawling cemetery with a high body count. The juxtaposition of a Gilded Age socialite ghost and a modern corporate layoff victim trying to find spark in a boneyard is brilliant, provided the prose doesn't devolve into toothless whimsy. The stakes must remain lethal, or the demons are just overpriced estate taxes. 

### The Geopolitics of the Afterlife

Adam has more to protect than just the leafy residents of Green-Wood. He has his children, he has his wounded heart, and apparently, he has a demonic pact that stretches across generations. Which brings me to my core grievance with contemporary fiction: why must every man grieving his late wife also double as an amateur exorcist? It is entirely extra. Peak romantic-suspense behavior, but entirely extra. 

The synopsis promises that the line between the living and the dead blurs, forcing Jess to confront a shocking connection to the past. Vague? Excessively so. It reads like a bureaucratic boilerplate draft written by a terrified junior attaché who hasn't read the annex. But Willig usually delivers a tight, witty plot that balances historical texture with genre thrills. If she manages to keep the pacing sharp and prevents the demon-hunting from getting bogged down by domestic melodrama, this will be an absolute banger of a read. 

I must dash now. I am hopelessly late for brunch with my former college roommate who is now a senator, and if I keep him waiting, he will use congressional oversight to audit my tea collection.
