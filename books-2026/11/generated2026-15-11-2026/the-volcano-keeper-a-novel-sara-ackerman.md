The house is finally quiet, save for the groan of the old timber porch cooling in this bitter November air, and I am staring at a synopsis for Sara Ackerman’s *The Volcano Keeper* with the hollow exhaustion of someone who has hauled three hundred bales of damp alfalfa uphill before breakfast. 

### Ash and Memory

Alana Hollinger arrives on Hawai‘i Island in 1984 carrying professional grief and ancestral ghosts. Naturally. Because no protagonist in contemporary fiction can simply study a basalt flow without dragging a century of family trauma across the tectonic plates. She moves into her grandmother’s old house in Volcano Village, dodging tremors while hunting for a vanished relative lost during a wartime secret eruption of Mauna Loa. Respectfully, what? The military hiding a volcanic cataclysm during World War II sounds like the fever dream of a pulp novelist who spent too much time hounding the USGS gift shop, yet here we are, leaning hard into the historical melodrama. 

### The Bureaucracy of Dirt

I have zero patience left for these manufactured overlaps where geological violence conveniently mirrors personal angst. The earth does not care about your grandmother, Alana. Magma rises because of density and pressure, not because a generational curse demands narrative closure. Give me a book about actual soil microbiology, about the agonizing, unromantic calculus of nitrogen fixing in depleted volcanic ash, rather than another brooding neighbor at a military installation hiding guarded truths. Speaking of guarded truths, why is every single children's television program currently obsessed with anthropomorphic rescue vehicles that teach toddlers toxic positivity while my own offspring refuse to put on their heavy wool socks for tomorrow morning's frost? It is an absolute outrage that we subject young minds to flashing plastic sirens when they should be staring at the grey horizon, contemplating the sheer indifference of the late autumn mulch rotting down by the chicken coop. 

Anyway. Ackerman clearly channels the atmospheric dread of Kristin Hannah or Louise Erdrich, though I suspect the geology will take a back seat to lingering gazes over a widowed consultant's fence line. 

I predict this novel will stubbornly refuse to let the mountain simply be a mountain. It will insist on making Kilauea a metaphor for suppressed feminine rage or whatever literary buzzword editorial boards are pushing this season. Give me actual tectonic force over metaphor any day of the week.
