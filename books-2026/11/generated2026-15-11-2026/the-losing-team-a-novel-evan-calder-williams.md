The air smells like cinnamon and damp leaves, the kind of scent that makes you want to linger in the orchard’s quiet corners—where the apples are still tart, the pumpkins still heavy with their own secrets. But Evan Calder Williams’ *The Losing Team* doesn’t offer that kind of comfort. It’s a novel that arrives like a well-worn coat, smelling of whiskey and old money, its pockets lined with counterfeit promises and half-baked schemes. The synopsis alone is a masterclass in controlled ambiguity, the kind that leaves you wondering if you’re being seduced or sold a bill of goods.

Wash Loomis returns from the West, a man who’s seen too much gold and not enough truth, and finds Utica in the grip of its own kind of fever—capitalism with teeth, the kind that doesn’t just chew but swallows whole. The Loomises aren’t just another crime family; they’re a symptom, a grotesque reflection of an era where every hustle is either a survival tactic or a suicide note. Rhoda, Wash’s mother, is the kind of woman who could sell the devil’s own ledger if the price was right—charismatic, ruthless, and utterly convinced she’s the only one running the show. But the synopsis doesn’t tell you whether she’s a villain or a survivor, and that’s the point. Williams seems to be channeling the moral ambiguity of *The Bonfire of the Vanities* meets the raw, unfiltered energy of *The Ballad of Jack and Rose*—but with the kind of dark humor that makes you laugh through your teeth.

The promise of a "kaleidoscopic" novel is both tantalizing and frustrating. Kaleidoscopic implies movement, shifting perspectives, a world that refuses to be pinned down. But the synopsis gives us only the broad strokes: a crime family, a power struggle, a new American era. Where’s the chaos? Where’s the absurdity? The Loomises sound like they could be a dysfunctional family from a *Curb Your Enthusiasm* episode if they weren’t also the architects of their own downfall. And yet, the synopsis doesn’t even hint at the kind of sharp, satirical edge that would make this feel like more than just another historical crime saga. It’s all too easy to imagine this as a novel that could have been written by a younger, less daring version of Don DeLillo—if DeLillo had spent more time in a dive bar than a boardroom.

But then—here’s the pivot. Maybe that’s the whole point. Maybe the vagueness isn’t a flaw but a deliberate choice, a way to let the reader’s imagination fill in the gaps like a half-empty glass at a speakeasy. The synopsis doesn’t need to spell everything out; it just needs to make you *want* to. And it does that, in a way that feels both promising and slightly unsettling. There’s a rhythm here, a cadence that suggests Williams is more interested in the sound of the world than its shape. The Loomises aren’t just characters; they’re a chorus, a cacophony of voices that might just drown each other out before the final act.

Yet—and this is where the bias kicks in—I can’t shake the feeling that this is a novel that’s been sold short by its own hype. The synopsis reads like a press release for a movie poster, all bold text and dramatic pauses. Where’s the grit? Where’s the mess? The Loomises sound like they could be the basis for a brilliant, messy, morally ambiguous epic, but the synopsis doesn’t give us enough to believe that’s what we’re actually getting. It’s like being handed a menu at a restaurant where the chef has only described the ingredients, not the dish.

Still, there’s something undeniably compelling about the idea of a crime family navigating the birth pains of American capitalism. It’s a theme that feels both timeless and urgently relevant, especially in an era where the line between hustle and exploitation is thinner than ever. The synopsis doesn’t tell us how Williams will handle it, but the potential is there—if he’s willing to embrace the chaos, the absurdity, the sheer, unfiltered mess of it all. And if he does, *The Losing Team* could be the kind of novel that lingers like a bad habit, impossible to shake.
