**Author:** Hannah Pettit  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *A Labor of Love Potion* reads like a love letter to the kind of rom-com that either makes you sigh with relief or want to burn the page in protest—depending on whether you’re a sucker for the kind of magic that feels like a sugar rush or the kind of cynicism that demands a side of existential dread. Rosemary Venera, the Wednesday Addams of love potions, is a character who *should* be infuriating—her confidence is the kind that grates like a chalkboard eraser—but the synopsis sells her as a tragicomic figure, which is either a stroke of genius or a lazy shortcut. Because let’s be honest, the real villain here isn’t Bowen Eto, the love therapist who hates her guts (and probably deserves it). The villain is the trope itself: the idea that a woman’s entire identity can be reduced to her ability to seduce someone into loving her, even when that someone is actively hostile. It’s the kind of premise that makes you wonder if the author has ever read *The Love Hypothesis* by Ali Hazelwood and thought, “Hmm, what if we made it about hatred instead?”

The synopsis leans *heavily* into the “enemies-to-lovers” framework, but with a twist—Rosemary isn’t just seducing Bowen; she’s *forced* to, for the sake of her business. That’s supposed to add layers, right? But it also feels like a cop-out. Because if the entire plot hinges on her executing a “failproof Eleven Step Seduction Plan,” then where’s the tension? Where’s the *doubt*? The synopsis doesn’t even hint at whether Rosemary’s plan is foolproof or if she’s just really good at pretending. Is she a master manipulator, or is she just really bad at reading people? The ambiguity is intentional, but it’s also lazy—because the real question is whether this book will ever challenge the idea that love (or hatred, in this case) can be *engineered* like a recipe. And if it doesn’t, then it’s just another entry in the long line of rom-coms that treat emotional labor as a performance art.

The synopsis also does a disservice to Bowen Eto by making him a one-note antagonist. He’s the love therapist who hates Romanticists, which is fine—it’s a fun contrast—but the synopsis doesn’t give him any depth beyond his hatred. Is he a former love potion user who got burned? Is he a purist who believes love should be organic? Or is he just a grumpy man who doesn’t like being told how to feel? The synopsis doesn’t say. And that’s the problem with this kind of setup: it relies on the audience filling in the blanks with their own biases. If you’re expecting a sharp, subversive take on love and magic, you might be disappointed. If you’re just here for the cat-and-mouse game of seduction, then you’re in for a treat. But either way, the synopsis doesn’t do enough to distinguish itself from the dozen other rom-coms that have come before it.

The real question is whether *A Labor of Love Potion* will ever feel like more than a gimmick. The synopsis sells it as a “sparkling, magical rom-com,” but the word “sparkling” feels like a marketing ploy—it’s the kind of language that makes you think of glitter and pastel colors, not the kind of dark, satirical edge that would make this book stand out. And yet, there’s potential here. If the author leans into the absurdity of Rosemary’s situation—if she makes the hatred-to-love potion feel like a farce rather than a solution—then this could be something special. But if it’s just another story about a woman who has to “win” a man’s affection to save her business, then it’s just another entry in the rom-com canon, and we’ve seen this movie before.

Literally.

The only thing that might save this book is if it takes a page from *The Love Hypothesis* and *The Hating Game*—books that balance humor and heart with a sharp, witty edge. But the synopsis doesn’t give us any reason to believe that’s what we’re in for. It’s all surface-level charm and no substance, which is a shame, because there’s a story here that could be so much more than just another love potion romp.
