**Author:** Grace Reilly  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Have Your Cake* is so deliberately vague it’s almost a performance art piece in itself—like a menu where every dish is described as "delicious" and "authentic," but you’re left wondering if the chef actually knows how to season. The idea of a book where women writers, illustrators, and photographers spill their guts over recipes is both *so* 2010s wellness memoir and *so* 2024’s chaotic, fragmented self-help trend—think *The Sun and Her Flowers* meets *Modern Love* if they’d been edited by a sleep-deprived grad student who only reads the comments section of Instagram Reels. The contributors list reads like a who’s-who of mid-career women who’ve either published a YA novel or a parenting blog post that went semi-viral. Claire LaZebnik’s name alone is a red flag—she’s the kind of writer who’d turn a heart-to-heart into a 12-step program, and I can already picture her essay starting with something like, *"I baked my first sourdough during my divorce, and it rose like my self-esteem."*

But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t tell us *why* these women are sharing. Is this a cathartic exercise in collective trauma? A marketing ploy to sell cookbooks? A last-ditch effort to make food writing cool again after a decade of *Salt Fat Acid Heat* fatigue? The lack of specificity is maddening—it’s like being handed a blank recipe card and told to "fill in the blanks with your life." And yet, that’s the point, isn’t it? The book isn’t about structure; it’s about the mess of it all. Which is why it feels less like a literary project and more like a group therapy session where everyone’s brought their own flour and a half-baked idea of what healing looks like.

The contributors list is the real star here, though. Davis Alexander? Kim Allen-Niesen? These are women who’ve spent years crafting narratives about resilience, identity, and the absurdity of adulting—so why not lean into that? But the synopsis doesn’t even hint at the *kind* of struggles we’re in for. Will there be essays about baking as a metaphor for motherhood, like *The Mommy Diaries* but with more buttercream? Or will it be the raw, unfiltered confessions of women who’ve used recipes as a way to sabotage their own diets, like *Big Fat Liar* meets *The Secret Life of Bees*? The vagueness is almost a dare—*"You think you know what this is? Try."*

And then there’s the tone. The synopsis itself is a masterclass in performative warmth—*"a heart-to-heart over a warm slice of coffee cake"*—but it’s also dripping with irony. Because let’s be real: not every woman’s story is a cozy, uplifting one. Some of them are probably about the time they burned a batch of cookies and blamed their partner. Some are about the way food became a weapon in a toxic relationship. Some are just about the time they realized they’d rather eat cake than have a conversation. The synopsis doesn’t acknowledge that tension, which is either genius or a cop-out. It’s the literary equivalent of serving a dessert with no warning that it’s spiked with something unexpected.

My patience left the chat when I realized this book might be exactly what it claims to be: a collection of unfiltered, unpolished moments where the food is just the excuse for the real story. But then I remembered the contributors list again—these are women who’ve spent years refining their craft, who know how to turn a personal essay into a cultural moment. So maybe this is less about the recipes and more about the act of sharing, the way food becomes a language when words fail. It’s the kind of book that would make *NPR’s All Things Considered* do a whole segment on why we eat when we’re sad, but also why we don’t talk about it enough.
