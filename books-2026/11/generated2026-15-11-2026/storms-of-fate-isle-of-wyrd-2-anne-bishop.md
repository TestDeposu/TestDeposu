**Author:** Anne Bishop  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Storms of Fate* reads like a corporate press release for a *Star Trek* episode that got caught in a time warp with a *True Detective* spin-off—all while the writers were half-asleep after a particularly bad *Game of Thrones* binge. The premise is so broad, it’s less a story and more a *Wet Hot American Summer* of cosmic horror and workplace drama, where the only thing more predictable than Joann Baxter’s weight-loss drug mishap is the fact that Lucas Frost will eventually beg Ares de Winter for help, like a man who just realized his kid’s lemonade stand is actually a front for the Illuminati.

Beth Fahey’s arrival on the Isle of Wyrd feels like the moment a *Breaking Bad* character stumbles into a *Stranger Things* episode—except instead of a government conspiracy, we get a *Ghostbusters* plot where the ghosts are just really bad at delivering messages. The idea that a busload of living ghosts carries a cryptic warning from her grandmother is so vague, it might as well be a *Twilight* fanfic where the vampires are just really into yoga. And let’s be honest, if your grandmother’s ghost is the *real* villain here, then *Storms of Fate* is just *The Sixth Sense* with more bureaucracy and less Bruce Willis.

The synopsis also treats Joann Baxter’s weight-loss drug like it’s the *Fountain of Youth*—except instead of turning her into a immortal nymph, it just opens a door to whatever hellish corporate wellness program the Arcana has been hiding. The fact that this drug is "lethal if mishandled" is the kind of plot device that makes you wonder if the author was channeling *The Shining*’s "All work and no play makes Jack a dull boy" energy—except with more spreadsheets and less blood. And when Lucas Frost has to beg his father for help, it’s not just a power move, it’s the literary equivalent of a *Lord of the Rings* fanfic where Gandalf just texted Saruman for a favor.

Thematically, this feels like *The Leftovers* meets *The Office*, where the supernatural is just another HR policy. The Isle of Wyrd isn’t a world—it’s a *Dungeons & Dragons* campaign someone ran at a Starbucks after their third espresso. And the idea that Beth’s fate is "tangled with Wyrd" is so generic, it might as well be a *Harry Potter* plot where the protagonist’s parents were just really into astrology. I’m literally begging for a new plot.

The real tragedy here is that Anne Bishop, who built an empire on the *Others* series, seems to have traded her signature gothic grandeur for whatever *Netflix* algorithm suggested she write next. The synopsis reads like it was ghostwritten by a committee of people who’ve never read *The Dark Tower* but have a deep love for *The X-Files*’ "Monsters of the Week" formula. And when the Sorcerer King’s father is dragged into the mess, it’s not a revelation—it’s the literary equivalent of calling in *The Rock* for a *Die Hard* sequel.

But here’s the thing: if this book delivers even half the tension of a *True Detective* episode where the detective’s wife is a ghost, I’ll eat my hat. Or, more accurately, I’ll drink my lapsang souchong and wonder why I ever thought the supernatural needed another corporate thriller.
