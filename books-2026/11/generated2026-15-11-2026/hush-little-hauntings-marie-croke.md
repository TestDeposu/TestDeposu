**Author:** Marie Croke  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Hush Little Hauntings* is a masterclass in controlled ambiguity—like holding a candle to a locked door and wondering if the flicker is fear or just the wind. The premise is *Model Home* meets *Delicate Condition*, which immediately signals a story that wants to be both a cozy, atmospheric puzzle and a slow-burn horror that peels back layers like a bruise. But here’s the thing: the vagueness isn’t just a stylistic choice; it’s a *strategic* one. The synopsis doesn’t just *hint* at the horror—it *withholds* it, which is either genius or a gambit to make readers feel like they’re uncovering secrets alongside Halie. And yet—

Midwife as haunting midwife? That’s a role so specific it’s practically a character in itself. Halie isn’t just delivering babies; she’s birthing *ghosts*, and not the kind that linger in attics but the kind that *act*—seeking justice, retribution, like some kind of spectral labor union. The idea that these hauntings are miniature replicas of places steeped in pain is *deliciously* eerie, but also… *problematic* in a way that feels intentional. Because if these ghosts are just dolls, then what’s the stakes? But then again, maybe the stakes are exactly that—they’re not dolls, they’re *echoes*, and the house itself is the womb, and Halie is the midwife who’s forgotten how to stop the bleeding.

The synopsis leans hard into the *uncanny*—Isabelle is fragile, incomplete, and Halie suspects a secret. That’s the kind of setup that makes you want to scream, *WHAT IS IT?!*—but the real horror isn’t in the answer, it’s in the *process* of uncovering it. The house changing, corridors lengthening, rooms disappearing—this is the kind of slow-burn dread that works best when it’s *implied*, not spelled out. It’s like *The Babadook* meets *The Haunting of Hill House*, but with the emotional gut-punch of *Delicate Condition*’s grief. The question isn’t just *what* is happening, but *why* Halie is the one who has to figure it out.

But then—

The synopsis doesn’t *tell* you what Midhouse is. Is it a house? A prison? A hospital? A *womb*? The ambiguity is *deliberate*, and that’s where the real tension lies. It’s the kind of story that rewards readers who love to *feel* their way through the dark, like *Mexican Gothic* but with the psychological weight of *The Turn of the Screw*. The fact that Halie is a midwife—someone who’s used to life and death, birth and loss—makes her the perfect protagonist for this kind of story. She’s not a virgin in the woods; she’s a woman who’s already seen too much, and that’s what makes her vulnerable.

Yet—

The biggest question isn’t whether this will work, but *how*. The synopsis is *so* tight it’s almost cruel—like a tease that refuses to let you in. It’s the kind of setup that could either be *brilliant* or *frustrating*, depending on how the book delivers. If it leans too hard into the mystery, it might feel like *The Sixth Sense*’s plot but with worse pacing. If it leans into the horror, it might devolve into *The Conjuring*’s over-reliance on jump scares. But if it walks that tightrope—like *The Haunting of Bly Manor* meets *The Midnight Library*—then it could be something special.

The name-drop of *Liar, Dreamer, Thief* is *perfect* here because it signals a story that’s equal parts *mystery* and *psychological horror*. Maria Dong’s book is all about secrets and deception, and that’s exactly what *Hush Little Hauntings* seems to be promising—just with ghosts instead of liars. The fact that the synopsis doesn’t *tell* you what the hauntings are doing, only that they’re *doing* something, is *chef’s kiss* for fans of *Delicate Condition*’s slow-burn dread.

But then—

The biggest risk isn’t the horror; it’s the *emotional core*. Halie is a midwife, which means she’s already dealing with death and grief. The question is whether the book will let her *feel* it, or if it’ll just use her as a vessel for the horror. If it’s the latter, it’ll feel hollow. If it’s the former, it’ll be *devastating*.

The synopsis is *so* good it’s almost *too* good—like a puzzle box that won’t let you open it. It’s the kind of setup that makes you want to *demand* answers, but also *fear* them. And that’s exactly what makes it work.
