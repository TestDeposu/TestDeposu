**Author:** Ausma Zehanat Khan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Lines We Cross* reads like a checklist for every third-rate thriller that’s been regurgitated by a committee of overworked screenwriters and underpaid activists. It’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if the author’s editor was high on the idea of "diverse representation" while also trying to outdo the latest *True Detective* season—because, let’s be honest, this is just *True Detective* with more mosques and a Muslim detective who’s basically the female version of Rust Cohle, except with a side hustle in feminist solidarity.

The premise—girls disappearing in a Colorado town, a body dumped in a mosque, a corrupt cop, a "bond" between three women of color—is so tired it’s almost *refreshing* in its banality. It’s the kind of plot that would make *The Wire*’s David Simon roll his eyes so hard he’d dislocate his shoulder. Where’s the grit? Where’s the *substance*? Instead, we get a neat little package of "issues"—racism, Islamophobia, police corruption—all neatly tied up with a bow of "female solidarity," as if that’s some kind of literary breakthrough. Newsflash: *The Handmaid’s Tale* already did that in 1985. What’s new here is the fact that the "moral" is so obvious it’s practically screaming from the pages, like a bad YA novel’s conscience.

The character dynamics are even more laughable. Detective Inaya Rahman is clearly meant to be the "relatable" Muslim woman who’s both brilliant and emotionally complex—because nothing says "depth" like a detective who’s also a "victim of systemic oppression." Meanwhile, Lieutenant Waqas Seif is the classic "love interest who’s also the villain," a trope so overused it’s now just a placeholder for "male character who exists to test the female lead’s emotional resilience." And then there’s the trio of women—Areesha, Catalina, Inaya—who are supposed to represent some kind of "unbreakable bond," but honestly, it sounds like a bad *Friends* reunion where everyone’s just waiting for the next crisis to bond over. Where’s the *real* tension? Where’s the *psychological* depth? Instead, we get a checklist of "issues" that feel more like a college thesis than a novel.

The thematic execution is even worse. The synopsis doesn’t just *mention* racism and Islamophobia—it *celebrates* them, like they’re some kind of literary achievement. But where’s the *nuance*? Where’s the *ambiguity*? This isn’t *Beloved*—it’s *The Help* if *The Help* had been written by a committee of people who thought "diversity" was just another buzzword to slap on a book jacket. The way the synopsis frames the murders as some kind of "exploration of faith and prejudice" is almost insulting, like it’s treating these crimes as a *case study* rather than the horrific, real-world tragedies they are. And the idea that a detective’s personal relationships with other women of color will somehow "expose the conspiracy"—well, that’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if the author’s ever actually read a real crime novel, like *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo* or *Gone Girl*, where the real tension comes from *people*, not *ideas*.

The only thing that might save this from being a complete snoozefest is if Ausma Zehanat Khan actually *subverts* the expectations set by the synopsis. If she manages to make Inaya Rahman more than just a "strong female character" who’s also a "victim of systemic oppression," or if she turns Waqas Seif into something more than a "love interest who’s also the villain," then maybe—*maybe*—this could be something worth reading. But right now, it just feels like another entry in the "diverse detective solves crimes while also being a feminist icon" genre, which is a genre that’s already been exhausted by now.

And don’t even get me started on the fact that this is the *third* book in a series. If the first two were as shallow as this synopsis suggests, then I can only imagine how much worse the writing gets with each subsequent installment. It’s like watching a bad reality TV show where the cast is just waiting for the next crisis to happen so they can all bond over it.

But hey, at least it’s not *The Da Vinci Code*. That’s something, I guess.
