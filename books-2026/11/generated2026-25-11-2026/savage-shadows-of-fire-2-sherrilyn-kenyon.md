**Author:** Sherrilyn Kenyon  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The tractor is leaking gear oil again, carving a slow, iridescent rainbow into the gravel by the pump house while the squash beetles stage a full-scale amphibious assault on the lower garden beds, and yet the publishing world expects me to sit here and untangle whatever marketing department mashed up Sherrilyn Kenyon and Alexandra Ivy into this five-in-one paranormal omnibus. Look at the copy. *Beyond the Darkness*. *Devoured by Darkness*. *Darkness Eternal*. *Taken by Darkness*. *Bound by Darkness*. One can only assume the collective noun for this genre is a black hole of originality where werewolves, half-demons, and Victorian marquesses fight over pureblood females and magical witches like raccoons in a grease trap. There is a profound irony in reading about fictional predators hunting for survival against extinction while actual topsoil erodes into the creek because industrial agribusiness treats the earth like a disposable paper plate. But sure, let us ignore the collapse of regional biodiversity and obsess over whether Salvatore Giuliani can sink his teeth into Harley without triggering a mandatory mate-rejection trope. 

Why must every single immortal alpha male possess a savage need that obliterates all other instincts? It is lazy prose wearing leather pants. Give me Barbara Kingsolver or Wendell Berry any day over this assembly-line urban fantasy where every heroine is simultaneously fiercely independent and entirely incapable of resisting a brooding vampire who traps her in the underworld. 

Exhausting.

The sheer logistical nightmare of keeping track of five distinct novellas—each recycling the exact same tired vocabulary of eternal lust, captive damsels, and prophesied bloodlines—makes hauling forty-pound bags of organic pelletized lime up the south ridge look like a spa vacation. Authors like Ilona Andrews manage to build sprawling, messy, lived-in ecosystems where the world-building actually matters to the plot, whereas this blurb reads like it was generated by an algorithm fed exclusively on grocery store checkout romance novels and damp basement mold. And what is with the authorship attribution mix-up in the official text? Attributing a box set to Alexandra Ivy while the title header screams Sherrilyn Kenyon is the kind of administrative carelessness that would get you fired from a regional seed-sorting co-op by noon on a Tuesday. 

At least the squash beetles have the decency to be direct about wanting to consume everything in sight rather than hiding behind flowery prose about dark realms and elite warriors. 

I really must call the broker to review my life insurance policy, which is a joyless task.
