**Author:** Kalie Cassidy  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

November air has a way of stripping the pretense from a room, leaving only the biting, skeletal reality of what remains. *The Long Night Looms* arrives with the heavy, portentous weight of a leaden sky, promising a return to the gothic sensibilities that Cassidy popularized with *In the Veins of the Drowning*. One wonders, quite frankly, if we have truly exhausted the "isolated village with dark secrets" trope, or if the reading public possesses the masochistic stamina of an audience watching a botched production of *Tosca* at the Met.

### The Bureaucracy of Fear

Brina Lund functions as our anchor—a Preserver, a title dripping with the kind of self-important pastoralism that usually precedes a catastrophic narrative stumble. Then there is Dr. Magnus Larson, the government man. The synopsis practically vibrates with the cliché of the rationalist clashing with the mystic. It is the literary equivalent of the Enron collapse; all systems look functional on paper, yet the internal rot is inevitable. Why must we always pair the skeptical academic with the local oracle? It feels as stale as the damp earth in a neglected cellar. Are we meant to find their impending ideological skirmish "compelling," or simply a necessary friction to propel the plot toward a conclusion that will surely involve a revelation about the state’s corruption?

### Folklore as a Political Liability

The premise of children returning "missing pieces of themselves" is the only element here with genuine teeth. It hints at an ontological horror—a loss of selfhood that is far more terrifying than the standard creature-feature nonsense. Yet, I fear Cassidy will prioritize the tedious "arrogant doctor learns a lesson" arc over the genuinely unsettling implications of the village’s stolen, hollowed-out youth. If the darkness is merely a metaphor for government malfeasance, consider me underwhelmed. We have seen this specific marriage of state surveillance and folk-horror before, and rarely does it survive the transition into a satisfying third act. It reminds me of the utter disintegration of the 2008 financial markets—a grand, complex structure masking a complete lack of substance.

Cassidy clearly aims for the throat, attempting to modernize the brooding atmospheric dread of Shirley Jackson or perhaps the jagged, clinical precision found in newer works by Silvia Moreno-Garcia. But will it hold? The synopsis is frustratingly vague, obscuring the actual mechanism of the threat behind a veil of "long-buried secrets." It is a cowardly way to solicit pre-orders. Either commit to the ghost, or commit to the conspiracy. Trying to occupy the space between them is a recipe for a fractured, incoherent experience. 

I expect a frantic, over-explained finale that trades mystery for shock value. Still, the aesthetic promise is there, hanging like frost on a windowpane. I shall leave the wire transfer for the orphanage in Zurich to be authorized now; millions of francs are far easier to reconcile than the inevitable disappointment of a predictable plot twist.
