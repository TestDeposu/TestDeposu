**Author:** E.V. Olsen  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The marketing team behind *Wicked Serpent* clearly spent their entire budget on bold font and high-contrast color palettes, but looking at this copy, one has to wonder if they accidentally pasted the *Dark Rise* synopsis into the submission portal. We are promised a dark hockey romance, yet the text screams of nineteenth-century London, ancient Stewards, and the return of a Dark King. It is a spectacular blunder—a clerical error that leaves the reader hovering between the smell of a locker room and the soot of a Victorian chimney. I am here for a gritty, sweat-drenched romp on the ice, not a magic-addled romp through a generic fantasy landscape.

### A Failure of Identity

The synopsis leans heavily on the tired crutch of the chosen one. Sixteen-year-old dock boy? Check. Secret destiny involving an order of protectors? Double check. It’s an exercise in extreme derivative writing. We are told to expect moral ambiguity, yet the framing is as binary as a light switch. Light versus Dark. Heroes versus villains. It is quaint, really—the kind of earnest, wide-eyed storytelling that hasn't held any real teeth since the early 2000s. If the author truly intends to deliver a "dark" romance, they are going to need more than just a brooding, beautiful antagonist with a predestined grudge. A grudge is not a personality trait. 

Actually, I might be too harsh. The industry is currently starving for that specific brand of catastrophic, ruinous attraction found in K.J. Charles’s work or the more unhinged moments of *Captive Prince*. If the manuscript can pivot away from the high-fantasy posturing and focus on the psychological disintegration of these leads, there is a pulse here. Perhaps the magical trappings are just a metaphor for the inherent violence of the rink? A bizarre, messy metaphor, but a metaphor nonetheless.

### The Problem with Expectations

The copy insists this will sit comfortably next to *Shadow and Bone*, but the target demographic for a dark hockey romance—presumably readers who enjoy the frenetic energy of A.J. Truman or the specific, abrasive chemistry found in *Heated Rivalry*—couldn't care less about nineteenth-century London. They want stakes that manifest in body checks and power plays, not in some forgotten war between metaphysical factions. And honestly, the "shocking twist" promised at the end of the text is likely a betrayal that any halfway competent editor could spot from fifty pages away. Tropes are only as good as the prose elevating them. If this is just another iteration of the "I hate you but your hair is nice" dynamic, the shelving in bookstores will be the least of its concerns. It will vanish into the bargain bin by mid-2026. My morning tarot reading specifically predicted an evening of profound annoyance, and it seems the cards have managed to manifest a book description that is as intellectually insulting as a lukewarm espresso.
