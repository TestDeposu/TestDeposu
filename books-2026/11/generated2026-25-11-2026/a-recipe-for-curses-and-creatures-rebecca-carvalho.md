**Author:** Rebecca Carvalho  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *A Recipe for Curses and Creatures*—no, wait, *A Recipe for Curses and Creatures*? That’s not the title. That’s the title of a *very* specific, very niche, very *not* this book. This is *something else entirely*, and yet here we are, staring at a synopsis that reads like a corporate whitepaper for a *LinkedIn Learning* course on AI, not a novel. But let’s play along, because the sheer audacity of this—this *monstrous* misdirection—is almost worth the price of admission.

The problem isn’t that the book is *bad*. The problem is that it’s *not a book at all*. It’s a *manual*. A *how-to*. A *self-help guide for people who think they’re smarter than they are*. And yet—here’s the pivot—maybe that’s the point. Maybe the entire premise is a satire of the way we’ve turned *everything* into a curriculum, even the apocalypse. Because if you squint hard enough, this isn’t a book about AI. It’s a book about *us*, about how we’ve reduced wonder to spreadsheets, magic to algorithms, and the unknown to a series of checkboxes in a *Figma* prototype.

The synopsis is *perfect* in its banality. It’s the kind of thing you’d find in a *Wired* article from 2018, not in a novel. There’s no tension, no mystery—just a *roadmap*. "You will learn about ethics!"—oh, how *bold*. As if ethics were a module, not a *wound* in the fabric of progress. And the way it lists *NLP* and *computer vision* like they’re ingredients in a soufflé—*soufflé*—is either genius or the most exhausting thing you’ve ever read. It’s the literary equivalent of a *TED Talk* with a *PowerPoint* that’s also a *manifesto*.

But then—*and this is where the genius lies*—what if this is *exactly* the book we need? What if the cure for our obsession with AI is to *drown* in it, to let it consume us so thoroughly that we forget we’re being consumed? The synopsis doesn’t promise a story. It promises a *transformation*. And in a world where *transformation* is just another keyword in a *job description*, that’s *terrifyingly* compelling.

Yet—*here’s the contradiction*—I can’t help but wonder if the real sin here isn’t the book itself, but the *audience*. Who is this for? The *enthusiast*? The *student*? The *professional*? Or is it for the *person who thinks they’re an enthusiast* but is really just someone who Googled "how to build an AI" after watching *The Social Dilemma*? The synopsis doesn’t care. It doesn’t *need* to. It’s written for the *idea* of the reader, not the actual one.

And that’s where the book—if it exists—will either *soar* or *implode*. Because if it’s *really* about AI, then it’s a *traitor* to the form. Novels don’t *teach*. They *haunt*. They *disturb*. They make you feel like you’ve been *watched* by something that wasn’t there before. But this? This is *instructional*. It’s the *user manual* for a world we’ve already built. And in a way, that’s *more* terrifying than any *skynet* plotline.

The heated seats in my SUV are malfunctioning, which is a tragedy because I was just about to drive to a bookstore and demand they return my money.
