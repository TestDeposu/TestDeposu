Coffee has lost its warmth, the fluorescent hum above the desk is vibrating against my skull, and yet, the publishing machine grinds on. They want a preview for *The Fox in Winter*. Antonia Hodgson’s *The Raven Scholar* was a polished affair—everyone and their aunt compared it to Leckie, which is an easy shorthand for books with a high word count and a low sense of humor—but this new synopsis? It is a vacuum. A void of information masquerading as a hook.

### The Problem With Vague Promises

Imperial intrigue. Ancient magic. Soaring ambition. This is the literary equivalent of a beige wall. We are meant to be panting for the return of the Eight, but without a concrete hook—a death, a betrayal, a shift in the status quo—we are left staring at the dust jacket of a ghost. Hodgson writes with a precision that usually demands more than mere buzzwords. Maybe the mystery is intentional. Maybe it is just lazy marketing. Actually, the silence is probably a strategic choice to avoid leaking the inevitable tragedy of the mid-trilogy slump.

Wait. Scratch that. Perhaps the lack of detail is exactly what the modern reader needs. We are drowning in plot summaries that spoil the entire first act. Maybe staying blind is the only way to retain sanity in a season of endless content.

### Winter Is Always Coming

We have seen this before. The sophomore slump is a beast that eats trilogies for breakfast. Look at what happens to those caught in the orbit of authors like R.F. Kuang or even the more clinical sensibilities of Fonda Lee—the pressure to expand the scope often leads to a hollowed-out narrative. If Hodgson leans too hard into the lore, she risks losing the intimacy that made the first volume work. The struggle for power is a tired trope, but if she treats it with the same cynicism she applied to her previous work, she might actually have something. 

But I suspect she will double down on the page count instead. The trend right now is bricks—books that take up so much physical space they act as security against a home invasion. If *The Fox in Winter* spends too much time on world-building, it will fail. I want to see these people ruin each other, not look at maps of a country that does not exist. 

The prose will likely be sharp, if only because Hodgson cannot help herself. She has a nasty habit of dissecting her characters until there is nothing left but their vanity. That is where the tension should be. Not in the magic. Not in the politics. In the cold, desperate need for recognition that drives the Eight. If she nails that, she hits the mark.

My inbox is screaming. The editor thinks I have nothing better to do than analyze adjectives all night. I just looked at the clock and realized the bus is coming in ten minutes and the youngest still hasn't found his shoes. My literary analysis is effectively dead for the next eight hours.
