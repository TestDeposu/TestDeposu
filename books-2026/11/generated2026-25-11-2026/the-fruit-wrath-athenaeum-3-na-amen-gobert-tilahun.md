**Author:** Na'amen Gobert Tilahun  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Corpiliu looms like a moth-eaten curtain, dusty and threatening to collapse under the sheer weight of its own metaphysical pretension. *The Fruit*—or *The Tree*, as the frantic promotional materials seem unable to decide—promises a collision of San Francisco’s banal concrete and the nebulous shifting sands of divine ancestry. Yet, looking at the synopsis, one feels the familiar, curdling sensation of a narrative stretching itself too thin. It is the literary equivalent of the 1997 disaster at the La Fenice opera house; expensive, structurally unsound, and doomed to leave us all clutching our pearls in the dark.

### The Geography of Disjointed Souls

Lil is wandering among the untrustworthy, stripped of her agency. Erik is haunted by ghosts, a trope so weary it practically demands a sedative. Why must the protagonists of urban fantasy always suffer from such pedestrian identity crises? The burden of divine blood is a heavy mantle, I concede, but does it truly require another trek through the woods of "sanity-questioning visions"? If I wanted to watch a man lose his mind while staring at his own navel, I would simply attend a cocktail party at the consulate. The plot concerns itself with a darkness that devours cities—a vague, hungry metaphor that suggests Tilahun might be running low on specific villainous motivations. 

### Secrets Kept in Bad Faith

We are told that long-held secrets will be unearthed. Fine. But secrets require a foundation of established truth, and this series feels like it is built on sand. Tilahun seems obsessed with the "taboos" of history, a narrative device usually employed when an author cannot be bothered to actually construct a coherent lore. It is a cheat. A shortcut. If you hide your world-building behind the veil of a "forbidden truth," do not be surprised when your audience finds the resulting revelation to be nothing more than a hollow prop. It reminds me sharply of the later, bloated chapters of N.K. Jemisin’s lesser works—all texture, no spine.

### A Requiem for the Coherent

There is a distinct lack of rhythm in this preview. It promises a war on two fronts, an escalation that feels less like a narrative necessity and more like a desperate attempt to keep the reader from noticing the stagnation in character development. One yearns for the sharp, biting economy of a Le Guin or the brutal, efficient prose of a mature fantasy scribe. Instead, we are left with this. A promise of "strange visions" that will likely lead to yet more circular dialogue regarding heritage and duty. 

I find my patience evaporating faster than the water in a neglected fountain. The prose in the synopsis is clunky, lacking that essential cadence that separates high literature from the dross piled up in airport kiosks. I suspect this sequel will collapse under the weight of its own world-building, leaving behind nothing but scorched pages and disappointed expectations. 

I must conclude this drudgery. My late aunt has bequeathed me a collection of heirloom jewelry, and the appraisal—a tedious, glittering affair—cannot wait any longer.
