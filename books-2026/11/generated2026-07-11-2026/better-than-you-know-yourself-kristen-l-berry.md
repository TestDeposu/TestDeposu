Another soggy plastic bag just blew past my face and wedged itself neatly against a rusted chain-link fence—poetic, really, considering the kind of suburban misery I am forced to analyze today. Look. I am sitting here watching a discarded Styrofoam cup dissolve in the gutter, wondering how many bills I have to churn out before I can afford to drink actual scotch instead of the stuff that comes in a plastic jug. And yet, I am contractually obligated to pretend I care about a fictional artisan nut butter empire. God save us all from upper-middle-class entrepreneurial fiction.

### The Artisan Trap and Other Capitalist Nightmares

Kristen L. Berry is back with *Better Than You Know Yourself*, following her previous suburban hit *We Don’t Talk About Carol*. Because apparently, women cannot simply have hobbies anymore; they must launch artisanal condiment brands that inevitably lead to homicide. Jia and Imani built a thriving nut butter business over thirteen years of thick-and-thin bonding. But wait. One is broke and dreaming of roasting garlic as a chef again, while the other is sleeping with their cutthroat investor named Ravi. A classic archetype. The wealthy financier who exists solely to get murdered on a kitchen island. 

Why must every domestic suspense novel require a dead financier on a quartz countertop? It is getting derivative. Liane Moriarty has run this exact playbook into the ground, and yet here we are, watching middle-aged female friendship crumble over corporate buyouts and illicit pantry hanky-panky. The synopsis leans heavily into the domestic thriller bingo card. Divorce? Check. Toddler debt? Check. A secret affair with a man who definitely wears tailored vests and says things like "synergize"? Double check. 

### The Aesthetics of Culinary Manslaughter

Let us examine the structural mechanics here. The synopsis promises a public scandal, a cold shoulder from the BFF, and—my personal favorite—MORE PEOPLE DYING IN THEIR CIRCLE. Yes. Apparently, Ravi was just the appetizer. Soon the entire suburban cul-de-sac is going down like dominos because of artisanal nut spreads. It is absurd. Yet I have to hit a specific word count, so I will pontificate on the duality of peanut allergies and betrayal. 

The thematic core relies entirely on the fragility of female partnerships under late-stage capitalist pressure. Jia is crumbling under the weight of toddlerhood and unpaid bills, trapped in a business she apparently never wanted. Imani is riding high on success and illicit bedroom escapades. Then Ravi dies. And suddenly, it is *Big Little Lies* meets a Brooklyn farmers market. Do I believe these women will stand together? Absolutely. Do I care? Only insofar as I need to finish this draft before my phone dies in the damp chill of this stupid alley. 

My local independent bookstore has run out of my favorite literary magazine.
