The chill of November is already setting in hard against the windowpanes, carrying that familiar, heavy scent of wet mulch and decaying foliage from the yard. It makes one want to curl up with something sharp. Something that doesn't just whisper platitudes about market forces, but actually demands to know why the system keeps breaking its own promises. Sonari Glinton’s upcoming *Blackonomics* arrives on the horizon with a very specific kind of quiet audacity, aiming to dismantle the polished lies we tell ourselves about supply, demand, and supposedly meritocratic boardrooms. Newsflash: the free market has never been entirely free, and pretending otherwise is just lazy economics. 

Look, the premise itself is hardly revolutionary—we have watched activists and scholars make these exact structural arguments for decades, echoing the vital, unflinching frameworks laid out by Mehrsa Baradaran in *The Color of Money* or Heather McGhee in *The Sum of Us*. But Glinton brings a veteran journalist’s ear to the noise. He trades dry academic charting for the messy reality of corporate maneuvering. He drags us straight into the automotive plants of Detroit and the sanitized suites of Fortune 500 companies, exposing the systemic friction that throttles true potential. Yet, let us be entirely honest here—or at least let me walk back my previous praise for a second—the synopsis relies terribly heavily on corporate redemption arcs as proof of progress. Does McDonald’s finally waking up to the buying power of Black consumers really signal a moral awakening, or is it just another cynical ledger entry disguised as inclusion? Sometimes I worry this book might lean too optimistically into the idea that capitalism can reform its own bigotry if we just show the executives the math. 

Markets are human systems, bruised and narrow-minded. They punish exclusion, yes, but they also reward exploitation with terrifying efficiency. 

Markets. 

### The Ledger of Omissions

The most compelling promise here lies in the data surrounding Black women business owners being the smartest bets on Wall Street, a reality that Wall Street’s myopic gatekeepers consistently fail to leverage. Glinton threatens to upend the stale tropes that keep capital concentrated in the hands of the unimaginative. And if he can balance the bitter despair of corporate exclusion with the stubborn, brilliant hope of those who build wealth anyway, this might actually be worth the ink. But I am inherently suspicious of any business title promising that enlightenment will simply make executives "get off their butts" to change society. Corporations do not have a conscience; they have quarters. 

The wind is howling louder now against the glass, and I really need to go out there and clean out the gutters before the freezing rain hits and ruins the foundation completely.
