**Page Count:** Unknown

The parchment spine of a forthcoming December publication sits upon the mahogany writing desk, untouched, heavy with the manufactured gravity of the mass-market paperback. Yet another thriller resting its entire weight on the rotting corpse of a nineties grunge idol—how dreadfully derivative. One expects the corpse to smell of patchouli and expired contract law. Kylie Scott clearly fancies herself the heir to the Penny Jordan throne, though she writes with the subtle psychological architecture of a supermarket tabloid. 

### The Gothic Real Estate Portfolio of Grief

Observe the mandatory billionaire—or in this specific case, the hermit guitarist—sequestrated behind iron gates because he watched his friend die. A gothic fortress. How utterly pedestrian. But I suppose we must applaud the commitment to architectural melodrama as the leaves outside the tall French windows turn a brittle, rotting brown against the November chill. Andrew Brooks lives in squalor and brooding glares, which is supposed to pass for emotional depth. 

Or perhaps it is just lazy character design. No, wait—let me retract that entirely. Perhaps Scott is intentionally leaning into the sublime absurdity of the rock-and-roll gothic, crafting a high-camp pastiche that elevates the material far beyond mere genre schlock. 

### The Progeny Problem and Other Epistemological Nightmares

A secret child discovered via biography? Groundbreaking. One can practically hear the editorial boardroom cheering at the sheer predictability of the trope. Ruby Donnelly appears to be yet another vessel designed for the reader to project their latent fantasies of sudden, violent displacement into a gilded cage. She goes from an ordinary life to dodging paparazzi outside a decaying mansion, and we are expected to buy the immediate chemistry with a man who smells like damp wool and unaddressed trauma. It is giving absolute aesthetic whiplash, fr fr. The juxtaposition of high-concept investigative mystery—who shot Christopher Durand?—with the inevitable shirtless kitchen-counter reckoning feels profoundly unserious. 

And yet, I find myself ordering a courier to fetch the slipcased advance proofs anyway. Perhaps because the autumn wind against the leaded glass makes a proper thriller almost tolerable. Or perhaps simply because the legal department insists I read every word of the option agreement before the studio shreds whatever dignity the manuscript has left.
