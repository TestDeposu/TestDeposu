A plastic grocery bag just whipped past my ankle like a phantom rat, driven by the miserable draft howling down this concrete corridor while I sit here pounding out another word-count-padding literary hit-piece just so my rent gets paid this month. God forbid I ever get to read something that doesn't smell like a corporate marketing focus group. 

Look at Jenni Howell's *Teach Me to Prey*. We are staring down the barrel of yet another monetized trauma-bond repackaged as YA romantic fiction, and honestly? The literary industrial complex needs to be put out to pasture. 

### The Commodification of the Gothic Boyfriend

The premise demands a certain suspension of disbelief, yet it leans so hard into the bad-boy-demon trope that it risks collapsing under the weight of its own eyeliner. Tara Corbane trades lover-hearts to a supernatural entity just to keep her sister alive. Classic bargain-with-the-dark-forces gambit. But then she meets Rian Connach, a vengeance-seeking real estate heir who refuses to play the victim. 

### Dismantling the Monster

And here is where the ideological battleground gets deliciously messy. Is Tara an autonomous agent of her own damnation, or is she just a glorified snack-delivery system for a toxic shadow-daddy? The synopsis tries to sell us a feminist subversion of the predator-prey dichotomy—oh look, she's tearing out hearts with her bare hands, how empowering—while simultaneously stuffing her into a tired cat-and-mouse dynamic with a boy who won't stop hunting her. Make it make sense. It is giving absolute unhinged emotional damage, but let us be real: we are all going to pre-order it anyway because the YA dark fantasy pipeline has us all in a chokehold. 

Howell previously gave us *Boys with Sharp Teeth*, establishing a clear brand of tooth-and-claw romantic fatalism that sits somewhere between Holly Black's ruthless fae courts and Jennifer L. Armentrout's aggressively horny gods. *Teach Me to Prey* promises more of the same genre-blending sadomasochistic folklore. Yet, the real question is whether the narrative will actually let Tara burn the patriarchy—and the demon—to the ground, or if she will inevitably end up crying into a brooding supernatural chest while the plot resolves itself through the sheer power of heteronormative yearning. 

I am dead tired of female protagonists whose agency is entirely contingent upon the men—or demons—they are sworn to destroy. Give me a blood-soaked girl who chooses violence without needing an ideological excuse or a tragic backstory involving a dying sibling. Give me narrative nihilism with actual teeth. 

Whatever. I am done analyzing this promotional text. I am going to go put on a Coltrane vinyl and forget this book exists until it inevitably pops up on my feed again next December.
