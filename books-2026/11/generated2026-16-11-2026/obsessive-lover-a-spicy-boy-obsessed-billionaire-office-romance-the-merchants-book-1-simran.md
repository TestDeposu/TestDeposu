**Author:** Simran .  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The VAULT Festival’s 2023 anthology isn’t a book—it’s a *misunderstanding*, a glitch in the system where the most urgent stories of the moment get repackaged as something else entirely. But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t *describe* these plays—it *erases* them. It reduces *Honour-Bound* to a single, hollow question mark about "family and anti-Blackness," as if the real work of Zahra Jassi’s solo performance could be distilled into a soundbite for a festival program. And yet—there’s a kind of poetic justice in that. The plays themselves are already fighting against erasure, aren’t they?

*Fanboy* is the closest thing to a love letter in this collection, but it’s a love letter to the wrong thing. Joe Sellman-Leava’s nerd isn’t just obsessed with *Star Wars*—he’s *haunted* by it, like a man who’s spent too long in the dark trying to remember how the light used to feel. There’s a quiet desperation there, the kind of longing that makes you wonder if nostalgia isn’t just a way of saying, *"I don’t know how to live in the present."* It’s the same energy as *The End of the F***ing World* meets *The Big Bang Theory* if the latter had been written by someone who’d just lost their last job to AI. The problem? The synopsis doesn’t capture the *physicality* of it—the way a man who’s spent his life quoting dialogue might suddenly realize he’s been quoting *himself* all along.

Then there’s *Five Years with the White Man*, which sounds like it should be a historical epic, but it’s actually a *satire*—a razor-sharp, almost absurdist take on the myth of the "great Black writer" who never gets his due. Eloka Obi and Saul Boyer’s ABC Merriman-Labor isn’t just a character; he’s a *concept*, a walking contradiction, the kind of figure who exists only in the gaps of history. The synopsis calls it a "startling account," but it’s more than that—it’s a *rebuke*. The way it frames Merriman-Labor’s journey as both "defiant" and "forgotten" is the kind of double-edged praise that makes you think of *The Color Purple*’s Celie, but with the cynicism of *The Wire*’s Stringer Bell. The question isn’t just why he was forgotten—it’s why we keep forgetting to ask.

But the real masterstroke here is *How We Begin*, which is either the most relatable or the most infuriating play in the bunch, depending on how you feel about monogamy. Elisabeth Lewerenz’s Helen and Diana are the kind of characters who’d make *Normal People*’s Connell and Marianne look like they were raised by monks. The synopsis reduces their dynamic to *"Diana’s got a boyfriend,"* as if that’s the punchline. But the *real* punchline is that love isn’t the problem—it’s the *system*. The way these two women navigate their desires, their careers, their *flats*—it’s all so painfully modern, like if *Her* and *The Social Network* had a baby and then left it in a shared Airbnb. The synopsis doesn’t get that. It doesn’t get the *weight* of it.

And then there’s *I F****d You in My Spaceship*, which is either the most ambitious or the most self-indulgent play in the collection, depending on whether you believe in alien abduction as metaphor. Louis Emmitt-Stern’s couples inviting strangers into their homes—it’s the kind of premise that could go anywhere, but the synopsis treats it like a joke. *"Sex and relationships"*—as if that’s not the entire *point* of modern drama. The real question is whether this is a play about *invasion* or *intimacy*, and the synopsis doesn’t even *try* to answer it. It’s like reviewing *Blade Runner* by saying, *"A man runs from robots."*

The problem with this anthology isn’t that it’s vague—it’s that it’s *too specific*. It’s the difference between a movie poster that says *"A man walks into a bar"* and one that says *"A man walks into a bar, orders a whiskey, and realizes the bartender is his long-lost brother who’s been dead for twenty years."* The VAULT Festival’s synopsis is the first kind. The plays themselves are the second.

But here’s the thing—none of this matters. Because the real question isn’t what these plays *say*. It’s what they *do*. And that’s something you can’t tell from a synopsis.

I need to repair the ribbon on my vintage Underwood typewriter.
