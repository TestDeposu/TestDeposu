**Author:** Monique Hazel  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Bare branches scrape against my grimy, double-paned window while a gray November sleet turns the overpriced espresso puddles on the concrete outside into a dismal brown slush. Exactly the kind of stagnant afternoon to sit here and pull apart yet another derivative piece of publishing house machinery. Let us talk about *The Dreamwitch*. Monique Hazel arrives in 2026 with an imaginary debut that reads like an algorithm fed every YA gothic bestseller published since the twilight years of the Obama administration. 

### The Real Estate of Rotting Manors

We are subjected once again to an ancestral estate that apparently defies thermodynamics. Wolford Manor swelters when it should freeze, because nothing says peak gothic atmosphere quite like a localized heatwave inside a drafty fictional mansion. The gardens rot instead of grow. Groundbreaking. We get a poor, struggling dreamwitch named Matiah—because characters in these books are never comfortably middle-class; they are always one missed rent payment away from selling their souls or mending lace—who wanders into this meteorological disaster zone to fix the rich kid’s nightmares. Naturally, there is a brooding bodyguard named Atlas. Atlas. Because calling him Greg or Kevin would ruin the heavy-handed, crystal-encrusted decadence of the mood-board. 

Wait. Let me correct myself on the setting—it is not actually a manor, the synopsis calls it a manor, but honestly, it functions merely as a glorified Victorian petting zoo for tortured archetypes. 

### The Weaponization of Clichés

And what would a paranormal romance be without the mandatory emotional obstacle course? Atlas has a devastating history with dreamwitches. Of course he does. Everyone in these books carries trauma like a designer handbag. They hate each other, except they don't, because the synopsis guarantees they cannot resist their growing attraction. The huntsmen—who sound like a rejected heavy metal band from 2012—are hunting them. Or maybe they are hunting the dreamwitches. The plot details are delightfully fuzzy because why bother inventing a functional political system when you can just throw ominous nouns at the page? Huntsmen. Nightmares. Ghosts of the past. It is a linguistic yard sale. 

Critics like Lyndall Clipstone and Kalie Reid are already blurbing this thing with words like gossamer, candlelit, and decadent, which usually translates to: *the prose is so thick with adjectives you will need a shovel to clear a path to the dialogue.* But perhaps that is the point. We want to be swept away to rotting autumnal gardens because the actual real-world November outside my window smells of diesel exhaust and municipal failure. 

### The Ultimate Danger

The synopsis warns us that love is the most dangerous thing of all. Truly terrifying. Forget the dimensional rifts and the ghostly entities breaking the barrier between reality and the dreamscape—the real threat is catching feelings for a guy named Atlas who probably smells like ozone and bad poetry. Hazel is leaning hard into the romantic horror trend, cashing the checks that Erin Craig and Hannah Whitten signed years ago. Yet the prose promises to be lush. Indulgent. I can already taste the candle wax. 

The radiator in my pre-war apartment is clanking violently again, threatening to explode into steam and ruin what little peace I have left before dark.
