**Author:** Henry Hoke  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The First Girl in Hell* is a glittering, jagged thing—like a broken mirror reflecting a 1940s Hollywood backlot, but with the edges still sharp enough to cut. It’s the kind of prose that makes you want to light a cigarette and lean in closer, even though you know the smoke’s gonna mess with your lungs. Henry Hoke’s follow-up to *Open Throat* isn’t just a book; it’s a middle finger to the idea that revenge stories need to be *earned* in some neat, cinematic way. No, this is a novel that smells like whiskey and old perfume, where the hell isn’t a place you go—it’s a state of mind you *become*.

Lucy—Lucinda, if you’re feeling fancy—isn’t just a blacklisted actress. She’s a woman who’s been *erased*, and the only thing left is the fire in her chest. The synopsis doesn’t tell you much about *how* she got here, but it doesn’t need to. The way it’s written, you can already hear the crackle of a radio playing static, the kind of static that sounds like laughter. The "Doom Book" is a brilliant invention—less a plot device, more a cultural artifact. It’s the kind of thing that would’ve made the tabloids *scream*, the kind of thing that would’ve had people whispering in church about the moral decay of the age. And yet, Lucy’s not interested in morality. She’s interested in *Maisie*, and the man who took her from her—this "Little Prick," who’s basically the literary equivalent of a Hollywood studio exec with a ledger and a god complex.

The real genius here is how Hoke frames Lucy’s descent. She’s not a tragic figure. She’s a *force*. The line—"Good girls want to marry the boy next door. Bad girls want to be the first girl in hell."—is pure Hoke, equal parts snark and existential dare. It’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if he’s channeling *The Devil’s Party* by Dennis Cooper or if he’s just too good at making you feel like you’re reading a lost pulp novel from the 1940s, written by someone who’s been drinking too much and watching too much noir. The fact that Maisie is both her best friend *and* her love interest? That’s not just a trope—it’s a *landmine*. The synopsis doesn’t say how Lucy plans to get her back, but you can already smell the gunpowder.

And then there’s the setting—the Nevada desert, a divorce ranch, the summer of 1940. It’s not just a backdrop; it’s a character. The heat, the dust, the way the air feels like it’s been sucked out of a room—it’s all there, even if it’s not spelled out. The synopsis doesn’t give you a lot of details, but that’s the point. It’s the kind of book that *feels* like it’s happening in real time, like you’re sitting in a diner with Lucy, listening to her talk about how she’s gonna burn it all down. The fact that she’s already in hell? That’s not a spoiler. That’s the setup.

The only thing missing from the synopsis is the *texture*. The way the pages would feel if you were holding them—thick, maybe, like the kind of book that smells like old money and cheap perfume. The way the sentences would *hit* you, like a right hook to the gut. But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe Hoke’s letting you imagine it. Maybe he’s leaving just enough unsaid to make you *want* to read it.

I can already tell this book’s gonna be a mess. In the best way possible. It’s the kind of thing that would make *The Secret History* look like a Sunday school story. Lucy’s not just a revenge fantasy; she’s a *myth*. And the fact that she’s gonna do whatever it takes to get her girl back? That’s not just a plot point. That’s a *philosophy*.

But here’s the thing—this synopsis is *too* vague. It’s like looking at a painting and only seeing the outline. You can tell it’s gonna be good, but you can’t tell if it’s gonna be *great*. Is this gonna be a dark comedy? A gothic horror? A love letter to old Hollywood? The synopsis doesn’t say. And that’s okay. Sometimes the best books are the ones that leave you wanting more.

Yet—there’s a risk here. The risk that it’ll all feel too *theatrical*. That the revenge plot will get bogged down in its own grandeur. That the fantasy elements will start to feel like they’re trying too hard to be *literary*. But if Hoke’s anything like he was with *Open Throat*, he’ll pull it off. He’ll make you care about Lucy. He’ll make you *hate* her. And by the time you’re done, you’ll be wondering if you’ve been to hell or if you’ve just been reading a really good book.

The upstairs neighbor just started playing something loud and discordant. Probably the cello. I can’t focus. But I can tell you this—if *The First Girl in Hell* lives up to its synopsis, it’s gonna be the kind of book that makes you question everything you thought you knew about revenge, about love, about hell itself.
