**Author:** Sally Hepworth  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Look, I have zero patience left today for another domestic thriller masquerading as witty satire about upper-middle-class misery. Sally Hepworth wants us to chuckle over joint credit cards and Netflix accounts while people presumably push each other off balconies at some overpriced luxury resort called The Beverly. And frankly, my coffee is stone cold, the baby has been screaming since dawn over a blue plastic spoon, and I am supposed to pretend I am thrilled about an anticipated 2026 release based on three paragraphs of glossy marketing copy that reads like an Liane Moriarty reject pile crossed with *The White Lotus* on sedatives. Give me a break. We get it. Rich people are messy. Marriage is hard. But do we really need a clever pun about marriages dying alongside the room service? The synopsis leans heavily into this breezy, dark-comedy tone—complete with feral guests clutching Louis Vuitton bags and actual weapons—before slamming on the brakes to tell us Liv Carrington’s husband might not just be cheating, but actively lethal. 

Actually, scratch that tonal whiplash. 

Maybe the juxtaposition of snappy one-liners about asset-hiding and sudden homicidal dread is precisely where Hepworth shines, or maybe it is just an exhausting gimmick masking a thin plot about a woman standing in a lobby staring at a creepy stranger named Tilda. 

### Checking In For Maximum Cynicism

The thematic underpinnings here rely entirely on the weaponization of domestic intimacy—a trope currently clogging every bestseller list from *The Last Mrs. Parrish* to whatever domestic suspense novel Reese Witherspoon is currently optioning for a streaming miniseries. Yet, what makes my eye twitch is the sheer predictability of the character dynamics. We have the oblivious wife. We have the cheating or murderous spouse lurking off-page. And, naturally, a cryptic female confidante who knows more than she should, because apparently no one in these books can make a friend organically without a shroud of impending doom. 

Look, I care about fictional friendships the way normal people care about their actual social circles—meaning I invest emotionally, and when authors reduce complex female bonds to mere plot devices for exposition dumps in hotel corridors, I lose my mind. 

Hard pass. 

Or perhaps I will read it anyway just to see if Tilda is actually a ghost, which would at least explain the unnerving intensity. 

Wait. The toddler has just hidden my favorite fountain pen inside the piano again.
