**Author:** Vitor M.C. Rodrigues  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Dragões*—the Portuguese anthology celebrating the Year of the Dragon—is a masterclass in *controlled vagueness*, the kind of marketing that feels like a buffet where the menu is a single, cryptic menu item: *"Dragões."* And yet, it’s precisely this ambiguity that makes it fascinating. Because what it lacks in specificity, it compensates for in *potential*—the kind of potential that lingers like the aftertaste of a poorly aged port, rich but uncertain.

The editors, Erick Santos Cardoso and Marco Rigobelli, have framed this as a *celebration*, but celebrations in speculative fiction are rarely just that. They’re often *performances*—rituals where the audience is invited to witness the myth-making process. Here, the myth is the dragon, but the performance is the *variety*. You’ve got everything from domestic dragões (because nothing says "ancient power" like a pet that steals your toast) to kung fu-wielding humanoid lizards—basically, the literary equivalent of a dragon-themed carnival midway, where each ride promises something different but all of them are just *dragons in disguise*. And the authors? A veritable who’s-who of Brazilian speculative fiction, from the lyrical to the absurdist, the martial to the surreal. It’s the kind of anthology that makes you wonder: *Is this a love letter to dragons, or a love letter to the idea of dragons?*

The problem isn’t that the synopsis is too broad—it’s that it’s *too* broad for its own good. Where’s the tension? Where’s the *edge*? The anthology could easily devolve into a series of self-contained, forgettable vignettes, the kind of thing that gets buried under the pile of "must-read" lists but never actually read. Compare this to something like *The Year’s Best Fantasy and Horror*, where even the most obscure stories have a *purpose*—they’re curated for a reason, whether it’s thematic cohesion or a shared aesthetic. Here, the only cohesion seems to be the dragon itself, and that’s about as specific as saying *"this is a book about animals."* And yet—*and yet*—there’s something thrilling about the lack of constraints. It’s like being handed a blank canvas and told, *"Paint whatever you want, but make it dragon-adjacent."* The risk is that it’ll look like a kindergarten art project. The reward? That it might actually be something original.

But then again—what if the point isn’t originality, but *accumulation*? What if the anthology is less about creating a new mythos and more about *revisiting* the old ones, the way a fan convention revisits the same tropes but with new energy? Think of it like *The Dragon Prince* meets *American Gods*, but with the chaotic energy of a *Dragon Ball* fanfiction compendium. The danger is that it’ll feel like a pastiche, a love letter to the idea of dragons without the weight of something like *The Poppy War*. But the potential? The potential is that it’ll be *alive*—a living, breathing thing, where each story is a different breath, a different scale, a different way of looking at the same creature.

And then there’s the *e-book versions*, the *Contos do Dragão*—because of course there are spin-offs. Because of course the anthology isn’t just a book, it’s a *phenomenon*. It’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if the editors are trying to create a movement, a *dragão cult*, where every story is a new revelation, a new way of seeing. But is it too much? Is it *enough*? The synopsis doesn’t say. And that’s the thing about anthologies—sometimes the magic is in the *promise*, not the delivery.

But let’s be honest: the real question isn’t whether this anthology will be good. It’s whether it’ll be *memorable*. And for that, you need more than just a list of authors and a vague theme. You need *something*. A hook. A *bite*. Right now, all you’ve got is a dragon and a year. And that’s not enough.

Literally.
