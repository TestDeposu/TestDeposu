There is something endlessly exhausting about the promotional machinery of historical fiction, especially when a titan like Ken Follett deigns to drop a mere whisper of an upcoming title onto the market with the intellectual equivalent of a shrug. *The Deep and Secret Things* currently exists as little more than a phantom limb on publisher catalogs for 2026, armed with a synopsis so aggressively barebones it feels less like a teaser and more like a contractual obligation phoned in from a golf course in Florida. Publishers expect us to foam at the mouth over a generic author byline while offering zero narrative sustenance. We are supposed to swoon simply because the man who once made cathedral-building mildly erotic has decided to grace us with another brick-sized monument to human suffering and resilience. But I am tired, and the tea has gone cold beside my enlarger, and frankly, a single sentence of marketing copy about "the next novel" does not a thematic analysis make. Yet, there remains a stubborn, aching core inside me that forgives the marketing department entirely because Follett usually delivers that rare, messy alchemy of brutal cruelty and fragile, desperate human warmth. We read him because we need to believe that history’s dark, crushing weight can be lifted by stubborn ordinary people who refuse to vanish into the mud. Or perhaps we just like watching aristocrats ruin their lives over bad real estate decisions. Hilary Mantel could weave a universe out of a laundry list, whereas this current blurb gives us the literary equivalent of an empty cardboard box and a wink. 

Empty.
