**Author:** T. Kingfisher  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The subway car is packed with the kind of people who still believe in the magic of *The Magic School Bus*—the ones who clap when the bus turns into a whale and sigh dramatically when Ms. Frizzle gets stuck in a volcano. Meanwhile, I’m here, clutching my phone like it’s the only thing keeping me from dissolving into the crowd, because *A Spoonful of Trouble* isn’t just another princess story. It’s a middle-grade rebellion disguised as a fairy tale, and that’s either genius or a dangerous precedent, depending on how you feel about the genre right now.

The synopsis sells this as a *Ella Enchanted* meets *Impossible Creatures* hybrid, which is fine—those books at least had the decency to make their princesses *actually* interesting. But here’s the thing: the problem isn’t that Tatter isn’t pretty or patient. The problem is that the entire premise hinges on the idea that a princess’s worth is measured by her ability to perform femininity like a well-rehearsed ballet routine. And yet—*and yet*—the book’s entire hook is that Tatter is *not* that princess. She’s the one who’s going to break the mold, or at least stab a monster with a spoon. That’s the kind of subversion that makes me want to cheer, but also makes me wonder: is this just another iteration of the "ugly duckling" trope, or is it something sharper?

Because let’s talk about the *Trouble*—this Yuletide monster invasion that happens every three years. It’s a cursed tradition, which means it’s either a metaphor for generational trauma or just a really convenient plot device. The fact that Tatter’s solution involves a goddess, a goat, and an enchanted spoon tells me this isn’t going to be a straightforward adventure. It’s going to be a mess of folklore and misfit magic, the kind of thing that would make Neil Gaiman nod approvingly while simultaneously facepalming at the idea of a goat being the MVP. And the goat—oh, the goat is going to be *everything*. Is it a trickster? A reluctant ally? A sentient, judgmental farm animal with opinions on royal succession? I hope so, because if this book doesn’t lean into the absurdity of its own premise, it’s going to feel like a watered-down *Howl’s Moving Castle* for kids who haven’t yet learned to appreciate subtext.

But here’s where the synopsis fails: it doesn’t tell us *why* the Trouble exists. Is it a punishment? A test? A leftover from some forgotten war? The vagueness is maddening, like when a kids’ cartoon introduces a villain with a single line of dialogue and then expects us to care. *Avatar: The Last Airbender* could’ve gotten away with it in 2005, but now? Now we want *context*. We want the weight of history behind the monsters, the reason they’re here, the way they’ve shaped the kingdom’s rules. Because if this is just another "princess saves the day" story with a twist, it’s not going to stand out. But if it’s something deeper—if the Trouble is tied to the kingdom’s oppression, if the monsters are a manifestation of the royal family’s own cruelty—then this could be something special. The synopsis doesn’t say, and that’s a shame, because the potential is there.

And then there’s the sister. The "lovely" one. The one who *is* good at being a princess. She’s not even named yet, which is either a deliberate choice or a sign that the book will treat her as an afterthought. But here’s the thing: in stories like this, the "lovely" sister is usually the one who gets the happy ending, the one who marries well and lives happily ever after. And Tatter? She’s the one who gets to be the hero. So is this a story about sisterhood, or is it a story about the cost of conformity? The synopsis doesn’t say, but I have a feeling it’s going to be the latter. And if it is, then *A Spoonful of Trouble* is going to be the kind of book that makes me want to scream at the screen—because why does it always have to be the ugly one who saves the day? Why can’t the pretty one be the one who’s *actually* interesting?

But enough about the synopsis. Let’s talk about the real issue here: the way middle-grade fantasy is being treated like it’s some kind of safe space for adults who want to pretend they’re still kids. *The Last Cuentista* was brilliant because it was *about* being a kid, not *for* kids. It didn’t dumb things down; it didn’t make the stakes feel small. And yet, here we are, with another princess story that’s going to be marketed as "for girls who don’t like princesses," which is like selling a vegan burger as "for people who don’t like meat" but then putting bacon in it anyway. It’s a betrayal of the genre, and I’m not sure I can forgive it.

But I’ll forgive it if the book delivers. If Tatter’s rebellion isn’t just about breaking the mold but about *why* she’s breaking it. If the monsters aren’t just obstacles but symbols. If the spoon isn’t just a MacGuffin but a metaphor. And if, for once, the "ugly" princess isn’t just a stand-in for the "unlovable" one but a full, complicated character who doesn’t need to be fixed. Because that’s what makes a good story—not the tropes, but the way they’re subverted. And if *A Spoonful of Trouble* does that, then I’ll be the first to admit it’s worth the hype.

But if it doesn’t? Well, I’m going to take my Porsche out for a spin and listen to some actual music. The kind that doesn’t pretend to be for kids.
