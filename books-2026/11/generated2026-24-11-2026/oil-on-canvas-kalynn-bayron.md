Steam wand is hissing again. Right in my ear. Just another Tuesday spent wiping down sticky porcelain while pretending to care about the latest YA crossover destined for a three-week shelf life at Target. Kalynn Bayron is back with *Oil on Canvas*, and honestly, the pitch reads like someone fed Stephen King and a middle-manager's corporate retreat into an industrial blender. A queer, Black horror-thriller about toxic family trauma and spooky art. Groundbreaking. 

### Palette Cleansers and Prison Wardens

The synopsis leans hard on the whole "glad my abusive mother is dead" opening hook. Because nothing screams nuance quite like announcing your protagonist's relief in paragraph three. But wait. Let me walk that back entirely—maybe that bluntness is actually the point. Modern horror loves a protagonist with a cold, jagged edge. We are miles away from the polite final girls of the eighties. Jasmine Payton isn't just running from ghosts; she's an investigative journalist dragging her own maternal baggage into an abandoned penitentiary. 

Except I am contractually obligated to type out these endless thoughts on a book that hasn't even hit shelves yet, all because my word count tracker is blinking at me like an accusing eye. Why do I care about a set of deranged prison letters when my thumb has a third-degree burn from a rogue steam valve? 

The real horror here isn't the fictional torture depicted in Antonia's creepy paintings. It is the relentless commercialization of trauma-horror. Everything needs a tragic backstory now. You can't just have a haunted prison; you need generational curses wrapped in institutional abuse to justify the page count. Give us ghosts who are just mean for the sake of being mean. Bring back cheap thrills without the mandatory excavation of maternal cruelty. 

Bayron clearly knows the drill. Slap a comparison to Grady Hendrix or Riley Sagar on the cover and watch the pre-orders roll in like oversized Americanos. And yet. There is something delicious about the claustrophobia of a locked-room mystery mixed with rogue corrections officers turning state penitentiaries into private art galleries of agony. 

If Jasmine actually finds out her mom was running a secret avant-garde torture-sketch syndicate in cell block B, I might actually eat my apron. Or maybe the paintings are just metaphors. God forbid. I need to finish typing this absolute drivel before the afternoon rush hits because I am currently watching a guy in a fleece vest try to explain cold brew to an eighty-year-old woman, and I have about ten seconds before I snap. Gotta sprint—gotta shove my apron into the locker and bolt down into the smoky basement to catch the second set of Leo’s completely unlistenable experimental jazz quintet before my soul completely rots away.
