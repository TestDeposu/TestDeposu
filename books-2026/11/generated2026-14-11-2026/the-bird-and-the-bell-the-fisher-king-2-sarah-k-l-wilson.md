**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Bird and the Bell*—the second installment in Sarah K.L. Wilson’s *The Fisher King* series—is a masterclass in how not to sell a book. It’s not a synopsis at all, really. It’s a *manifesto*. A love letter to ornithology, a lecture on mythic symbolism, a historical digression so dense it could double as a textbook. And yet—*and yet*—there’s something undeniably thrilling about it. It’s the kind of prose that makes you wonder if Wilson is channeling John Burroughs in a fever dream, or if she’s just really, *really* into her research.

The problem? The synopsis doesn’t *tell* us anything about the book itself. It doesn’t describe a plot, a conflict, a character’s arc. It doesn’t even hint at the *Fisher King* series’ core mythology—whatever that might be, because we’re left to assume it’s something grand, something *mythic*, given the weight of the prose. Instead, we get a 16-page essay on how birds have been worshipped, mythologized, and mythologized *again* across cultures. It’s the literary equivalent of a museum exhibit label: informative, but utterly devoid of narrative tension. Where’s the *hook*? Where’s the *stakes*? The synopsis reads like a scholar’s footnote, not a book blurb.

But then—*then*—you realize this might be intentional. Wilson’s first book, *The Fisher King*, was a slow-burn, atmospheric fantasy with a focus on folklore and ritual. If *The Bird and the Bell* leans even harder into the mythic, the symbolic, the *poetic*, then it’s not just a sequel—it’s a *counterpoint*. A book that doesn’t just *use* birds as metaphor but *becomes* them. The synopsis suggests a narrative that’s less about plot and more about *atmosphere*, about the way symbols accumulate meaning over time. It’s the kind of book that would make a critic like *Toni Morrison*—who once wrote that “the function of literature is to make life possible for us”—nod in approval. Or it would, if we actually knew what the book was *about*.

The most interesting part of this synopsis? The way it *avoids* giving us anything concrete. It’s as if Wilson is daring us to read between the lines, to see the book not as a story but as a *vibe*. A *feeling*. And in a genre where so many fantasy sequels feel like they’re just waiting for the next battle to happen, that’s… actually kind of refreshing. But also *frustrating*, because now I’m left wondering if the book will deliver on the promise of its prose or if it’s just going to be a very long, very pretty footnote.

And then there’s the *Fisher King* series itself. The name alone is a siren song—it’s *Parable of the Sower* meets *The Once and Future King*, a mythic cycle that suggests deep, layered storytelling. But if *The Bird and the Bell* is going to live up to that, it’ll need to do more than just *talk* about birds. It’ll need to *sing* like them. Because right now, the synopsis feels like the difference between a nightingale’s song and a parrot’s imitation.

I keep coming back to Burroughs’ line: *“The very idea of a bird is a symbol and a suggestion to the poet.”* That’s what this synopsis is—*suggestion*. It’s not a plot summary; it’s an invitation to imagine. And if Wilson pulls it off, it’ll be the kind of book that makes you see the world differently. But if it’s just another fantasy novel with a pretty cover and a vague premise, then it’ll be another footnote in the long, sad history of books that promise more than they deliver.
