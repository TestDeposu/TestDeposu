November rain is currently slicking the asphalt outside this drafty kitchenette where three of us share a single toaster and a mountain of unpaid utility bills, which makes Gus Moreno’s upcoming *If You Think You're Lonely Now* hit with the kind of crushing, visceral resonance only a millennial drowning in mortgage anxiety can truly appreciate. Paul Tremblay claims it gave him nightmares—color me shook, because the mere prospect of owning a dilapidated three-flat in Chicago sounds like an absolute psyop designed to bankrupt your youth. 

Is the pre-release hype justified? Barely. But Moreno proved with *This Thing Between Us* that he understands the existential dread of domestic spaces going rogue, trading traditional jump scares for the creeping horror of late-stage capitalism and structural rot. 

### The Architecture of Domestic Ruin

Let us talk about the real estate. Buying a fixer-upper is supposed to be the ultimate escape from the 9-to-5 soul-crushing grind, an architectural emancipation proclamation. Instead, Gabe and Espo buy an abandoned box of trauma that literally feeds on their past. Brilliant. Because what is a haunted house anyway if not a physical manifestation of the crushing debt and generational garbage we are forced to inherit? 

You get the eccentric trifecta of domestic claustrophobia: strange dreams, oddities tucked behind drywall, and phantom music from an empty attic. Classic tropes, sure. Yet Moreno pivots away from tired specters to weaponize the ultimate horror—family. 

### Familial Parasites and Hollow Foundations

Enter the actual monsters of the narrative: a spiraling brother fresh off a divorce and an estranged, recovering addict father. Now every single floor is occupied by blood relatives who bring their own atmospheric pressure changes into a crumbling Chicago tenement. This is where the synopsis gets deliciously messy. The book leans hard into the psychic weight of filial duty, treating love and addiction like foundational cracks that compromise the entire structure. 

We are looking at a masterclass in domestic claustrophobia—vibes immaculate, dread absolute. Rachel Harrison calls it the great Millennial horror novel, which is high praise coming from someone who also charts the messy intersections of trauma and ghosts. Yet one has to wonder if the supernatural element is even necessary when familial obligation is already this terrifying. Why worry about a ghost in the attic when your brother is actively unraveling your sanity in the unit below? 

The text promises an uncompromising deep dive into what we inherit. Debt. Grief. Addiction. Love. All wrapped in a Chicago autumn that undoubtedly mirrors the damp, biting chill currently seeping through these old window frames and freezing my toes to the linoleum floor. If Moreno manages to balance the supernatural hauntings with the brutal, soul-crushing weight of familial codependency without losing the plot, we are in for a generational genre masterpiece. Or it will just be two hundred pages of people arguing about plumbing while an attic plays cursed jazz. Either way, sign me up. 

Now if you'll excuse me, I need to go dig between the sofa cushions for twenty-five cents so I can dry my socks down the street before the laundromat closes.
