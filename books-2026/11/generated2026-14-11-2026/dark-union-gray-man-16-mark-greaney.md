**Author:** Mark Greaney  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The way *Dark Union* frames its premise—Court Gentry glimpsing his dead brother at a funeral, only to be dragged into a conspiracy that feels like it was cobbled together from a *True Detective* season finale and a *John le Carré* novel—already tells you everything you need to know about its ambitions. It’s the kind of setup that makes you wonder if the author is trying to out-*The Night Country* *The Night Country*, or if they’ve just been staring at a *Breaking Bad* DVD too long and decided to weaponize grief. The problem isn’t that the idea is bold—it’s that it’s *too* bold, like a pumpkin spice latte that’s been microwaved for 12 minutes. You can taste the desperation in the synopsis, the way it’s trying to sell you on the idea that a man haunted by his brother’s death is now also a man who’s going to *unravel the state itself*. But here’s the thing: Court Gentry isn’t a man who *unravels things*. He’s a man who *survives them*, and that’s the difference between a thriller that feels like a punch to the gut and one that feels like a poorly timed *Law & Order* rerun.

The real question is whether Greaney can make this work without resorting to the kind of *X-Files*-style supernatural hand-waving that’s become the default for authors who can’t decide if they’re writing a crime novel or a *Stranger Things* episode. The synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at how the ghostly encounter is going to be handled—is it a hallucination? A government experiment? A literal ghost?—which means we’re left with the same problem we always have with these books: the author is betting on the reader’s willingness to suspend disbelief, but not on their ability to *enjoy* the suspension. It’s like watching a kid’s cartoon where the characters suddenly start talking in full sentences and then immediately revert to grunting—you know it’s a mistake, but you can’t look away because the rest of the show is still *technically* entertaining.

And let’s talk about the *state* for a second. The synopsis drops the phrase “the dark state will do anything to keep its secrets” like it’s a line from a *24* episode, not a *Gray Man* novel. But here’s the thing: the *Gray Man* series has always been more about Court’s personal vendettas than about grand, systemic conspiracies. The state has been a backdrop, not a villain—until now, apparently. It’s like if *The Sopranos* suddenly decided Tony was going to take down the entire FBI. You can see the wheels turning in the synopsis, the way it’s trying to pivot from character-driven tension to something bigger, but it’s still missing the one thing that would make it work: a *reason* why the state would care about Court’s brother’s death in the first place. Without that, it’s just a man chasing ghosts, and we’ve seen that movie before—it’s called *The Sixth Sense*, and it’s been out of theaters for 25 years.

But fine, let’s assume Greaney pulls it off. Let’s assume he makes the ghost encounter *matter*, that he turns Court’s grief into something more than just a plot device. Then what? Then we’re left with the question of whether *Dark Union* can live up to the series’ reputation for *style*. The *Gray Man* books have always been praised for their prose—Greaney’s ability to make a gunfight read like a ballet, to make a chase scene feel like a dance with death. But the synopsis here is all *substance*, no *sizzle*. There’s no sense of Court’s voice, no hint of the sharp, dark humor that’s made the series stand out. It’s like reading a menu where every dish is described as “meat” and “flavorful.” You know it’s going to be edible, but you’re not sure if it’s going to be *good*.

And speaking of good—why does every book about a man grieving his brother have to be so *dramatic*? It’s like the publishing industry has decided that if you want to sell a book, you have to make the protagonist’s pain sound like it’s being broadcast on a siren. But grief isn’t a *plot point*—it’s a *character trait*, and if you’re going to use it as fuel for your story, you’d better make sure it’s not just *there* to make the reader feel something, but to *change* the character. Otherwise, you’re just writing a *very* long *The Notebook* fanfic, and we’ve all seen how that turns out.

But enough about grief. Let me tell you about the real tragedy of modern publishing: the way authors keep trying to outdo each other with increasingly ridiculous premises. It’s like a game of one-upmanship where the prize is “I wrote the most confusing book about a man who’s haunted by his dead brother.” And yet, here we are, waiting for *Dark Union* like it’s the next *Gone Girl*, when really, it’s just another entry in a series that’s been coasting on its own momentum for years. The question isn’t whether it’ll be good—it’s whether it’ll be *enough*. And that’s the real kicker, isn’t it? Because in a world where every book is trying to be the next *best seller*, the only thing that matters is whether it’s *better* than the last one. And so far, *Dark Union* hasn’t given us any reason to think it will be.
