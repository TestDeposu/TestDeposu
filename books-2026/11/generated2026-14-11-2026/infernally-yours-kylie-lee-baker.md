**Author:** Kylie Lee Baker  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The damp November air presses against the greasy windowpane of this booth while the bass from the jukebox rattles my teeth. Someone just spilled half a pint of bitter stout across the sticky wood near my elbow. God, my chest hurts. Not from a physical strike—though I spent three hours watching heavyweight sparring tape before dragging myself out into the freezing dusk—just a hollow, aching pressure behind the ribs that won't quit. Anyway. We are looking at *Infernally Yours* by Kylie Lee Baker, an upcoming YA drop scheduled for 2026. The premise involves a teenage girl named Sakura Smith whose estranged grandmother kicks the bucket and leaves behind a family transformation. Everyone turns into yokai. Classic folklore upgrade. 

Now. Let's assume for a second this actually works. 

The synopsis tells us Grandma Kojima rigged the estate. The cousins need to marry the Prince of Hell—Benjiro, a guy supposedly sweet and kind, despite his royal subterranean real estate—to regain human skin and grab the cash. Sakura wants the money desperately. Naturally, she falls for the target. It is a reality-TV-style deathmatch disguised as young adult romance. 

Wait. Let me back up. Actually, forget the reality TV comparison. It trivializes the stakes. Baker usually writes sharp, atmospheric horror-fantasy—look at *The Keeper of Night* for proof that she understands how to weaponize cultural mythology without making it feel like a Wikipedia gloss. But a romantic comedy centered on a hell-prince and sentient hair? The tone is doing some heavy lifting here. We have a protagonist dealing with an extra mouth on the back of her head while simultaneously trying to secure a demonic marriage license. The physical comedy writes itself. But can a writer known for dark historical fantasy successfully pivot to chaotic supernatural farce without losing the emotional core? 

Actually, no. The emotional core is probably the whole point. 

The thematic undercurrent here is nakedly transactional family greed wrapped in mythological body horror. Yokai transformations are rarely pretty in traditional lore; they are usually punishments for spiritual corruption or uncontrolled greed. So turning the greedy relatives into literal monsters because of an inheritance dispute? Brilliant. It is poetic justice writ large. The metaphor is so heavy it could bench three plates. Yet the synopsis promises a romance with the mark. The psychological tension between exploiting a nice infernal prince for generational wealth and genuinely falling for his sweet demeanor is standard YA boilerplate—except the girl's hair is sentient. That detail alone saves it from feeling entirely derivative of every other court-intrigue fantasy clogging the bestseller lists right now. 

Let's talk about the combat dynamics of this premise, because nobody else is going to. In any good bout, footwork is everything. Sakura is fighting her wealthy, entitled cousins for ring control. But her opponent isn't just the family—it is her own biology. A sentient mane and a parasitic mouth mean she is fighting a two-front war against her own anatomy while trying to land a submission hold on the inheritance. That is terrible cardio management. No fighter can go three rounds when their own skull is actively rebelling against them. 

The market is bloated with these high-concept rom-com fantasy hybrids. Think Stephanie Garber meets Japanese folklore after three espressos. Baker has the chops to pull off the world-building, but the romantic arc risks getting bogged down by the sheer absurdity of the premise. Will the prince have any agency, or is he just a shiny trophy with horns? The synopsis calls him sweet and kind, which usually translates to a walking red flag or a painfully flat love interest designed to trigger reader empathy without posing any actual narrative danger. 

I need to stare at a wall for an hour. I am going to spend the evening reading Sylvia Plath by candlelight.
