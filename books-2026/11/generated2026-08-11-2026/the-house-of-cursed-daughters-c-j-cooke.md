**Author:** C.J. Cooke  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Another historical-temporal triptych crosses the desk, smelling vaguely of market research and recycled tropes. *The House of Cursed Daughters* demands an immense reservoir of charity, a commodity currently out of stock. Three epochs, three heroines, and a generational hex. How entirely avant-garde. One can practically hear the focus groups cheering in unison. 

### Chronological Clichés and Other Maladroit Schemes

C.J. Cooke insists on shuffling the deck through centuries—France in 1312, London in 1865, and Brittany in 2026. The temporal architecture feels less like a deliberate artistic choice and more like a desperate bid to stave off readerly slumber. Yet, the 1865 Bedlam sequence actually holds a sliver of genuine atmospheric promise. The asylum corridors wrapping around poor Lois like white serpents—that is decent imagery. Then again, the synopsis immediately ruins its own fleeting poetic merit by leaning into the most banal, tired trope in speculative fiction: the mysterious phantom child asking who the mother is. Groundbreaking. Truly, the ghosts of Victorian melodrama refuse to stay buried, much to my eternal dismay. 

Wait. Let me rescind that mild praise entirely. Upon second glance, the entire Victorian madhouse motif is as derivative as a sophomore's Sylvia Plath pastiche. 

### The Commodification of Woe

Female power forging bright pathways through darkness. Oh, spare me. The marketing copy reads like a LinkedIn post for a coven of wellness influencers. We are promised an exploration of matrilineal resilience, which usually translates to three hundred pages of crying into artisanal porridge while an ancient ghost knocks politely on a French oak door. Emma preparing for her wedding in a rented castle while wrestling with a surly sixteen-year-old named Ivy is so aggressively contemporary it hurts my teeth. It is the literary equivalent of a beige living room. If this book attempts to serve me one more heavy-handed metaphor about generational trauma—that absolute buzzword of the chronically unread—I shall scream. 

We are expected to marvel at the sheer audacity of connecting Queen Isabella to a modern artist with wedding jitters. But the conceptual glue holding these eras together looks painfully thin from the vantage point of this synopsis. It gives off major Danielle Steel meets *The Once and Future Woe* energy, cooked up purely for the book-tok algorithm. Mid-tier gothic nonsense wrapped in a velvet aesthetic. 

My private flight to London has been delayed by an hour.
