**Author:** Ryan Pote  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Serpent’s Eye* is a masterclass in the kind of *adventure-lite* baiting that makes you want to both groan and grab a map. On one hand, it’s the kind of setup that would make Cussler himself nod approvingly—golden maps, lost cities, the kind of existential dread wrapped in treasure-hunting that feels like *Indiana Jones* if it were written by a guy who’s also written *The Da Vinci Code* but with more gold. Yet—there’s something *off* about the way it leans into the "threat to mankind" angle. It’s the kind of vague, *Armageddon-lite* threat that’s been overused since *The Da Vinci Code* made it cool to whisper about ancient conspiracies while sipping espresso.

The Fargos, of course, are the perfect protagonists for this kind of thing—husband-and-wife treasure hunters who exist primarily to be the audience’s emotional stand-ins. Sam and Remi are the *Narnia* of adventure fiction: reliable, competent, and just *slightly* too wholesome to feel entirely real. But here’s the thing—when the synopsis drops the "lost Incan city" line, it’s not just another *Raiders* knockoff. It’s a *cultural appropriation* of the kind that makes you wonder if the author’s ever actually been to South America beyond the airport. Incas weren’t just a backdrop for treasure hunts; they were a civilization with *real* secrets, *real* politics, and *real* consequences for desecration. And yet, here we are, treating their ruins like a video game level.

The synopsis plays it safe by keeping the threat abstract—"a secret that could threaten all mankind." That’s the kind of line that makes you think of *The Da Vinci Code*’s "secret society" nonsense, but with the added bonus of being *way* less clever. It’s the literary equivalent of a buffet where the only option is "mystery with a side of doom." And yet—there’s a certain *charm* to it. The way the Fargos are framed as *almost* competent, *almost* heroic, but ultimately just a couple of rich folks chasing shiny things. It’s the kind of story that would make *Pirates of the Caribbean* fans weep with nostalgia, if not for the actual plot, then for the *vibe*.

But then—*lexical whiplash*—there’s the way the synopsis *almost* hints at something darker. The theft of the ship’s log and letters? That’s not just a MacGuffin; that’s a *narrative device* that could, if handled right, feel like the opening of *The Name of the Rose*, but with more gold and less philosophy. The fact that it’s not explored here is either a sign of lazy writing or a deliberate tease—because let’s be honest, the real thrill isn’t the treasure, it’s the *hunt*. And if the Fargos stumble into something *actually* dangerous, something that forces them to question their own motives, then maybe—just maybe—this could be more than just another *treasure map* fantasy.

Yet—*the pivot*—I’m already second-guessing myself. Because what if the whole point is the *illusion* of danger? What if the "threat to mankind" is just a red herring, a way to sell more books while the Fargos go on another joyride through exotic locations? That’s the kind of cynicism that would make *Douglas Adams* roll in his grave, but also the kind of cynicism that makes *adventure fiction* so endlessly entertaining. The real question isn’t whether this book will deliver on its promises—it’s whether it’ll deliver *better* than the promises.

And that’s where the scotch comes in. Because if *The Serpent’s Eye* is anything like the rest of the series, it’ll be a solid, if unremarkable, ride. But if it’s anything like *The Lost City of Z* or *The Shadow of the Wind*, then it’ll be something special. And honestly? I’d rather take my chances with the latter.
