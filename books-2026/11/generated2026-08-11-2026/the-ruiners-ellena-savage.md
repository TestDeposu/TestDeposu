**Author:** Ellena Savage  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

November damp clings to the windowpanes outside, smelling of wet mulch and the kind of stubborn perennial roots that refuse to die quietly before the first hard freeze. Speaking of things that refuse to die quietly, I am looking at the publishing slate for late next year, specifically Ellena Savage’s upcoming novel, *The Ruiners*, which arrives when the snow will presumably have already ruined whatever is left of my gutters. The synopsis describes a pair of twenty-somethings—Pip, a Melbourne waitress grieving a sudden inheritance, and Sasha, some insufferable scholar of Balkan fiction—who sink fifty grand into a crumbling ruin on an economically devastated Greek island. Because nothing says youthful romance quite like buying structurally unsound masonry in a climate-ravaged tax desert. 

### Mortars and Broken Promises

The publisher wants us to read this as a sharp, cerebral eco-thriller about global capitalism and its ongoing theater of decay. Yet the synopsis reads like a standard-issue expatriate cautionary tale written by someone who has never had to pry asbestos tile off a subfloor with frozen fingers while a toddler screams about a lost red mitten in the mud outside. I am tired of young people fleeing their mundane economic dread in the West only to discover—gasp—that the Global South is not their personal bohemian playground or a scenic backdrop for their interpersonal betrayals. Did nobody teach these kids how to check a foundation? Or how to build a sandbox that doesn't wash away in the first November cloudburst? The children down the street are currently trying to reconstruct a ruined Lego bridge out of wet autumn leaves, and honestly, their structural ambition makes these fictional millennials look positively bankrupt. I remain entirely unconvinced.

Predictable.

One expects the ghosts of past colonial mistakes to emerge right on cue, probably delivered via some weathered local fisherman who shakes his head knowingly while sweeping glass off a veranda. Think Lauren Groff crossed with the bleakest social realism of Rachel Cusk, but filtered through the lens of a generation that treats property acquisition as a personality trait. 

I have to go unclog the lawnmower deck because the grass was too wet.
