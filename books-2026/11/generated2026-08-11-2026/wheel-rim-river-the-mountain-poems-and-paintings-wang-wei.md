The chill pressing against the windowpane right now carries that specific, damp melancholy that only late November can muster. And so, reading about an eighth-century autumn walk through Chinese mountains feels oddly appropriate. *Wheel-Rim River: The Mountain Poems and Paintings* arrives via publisher machinery with a quiet promise, positioning itself as a physical artifact of communion between Wang Wei and P’ei Ti. Twenty sites. Two friends. A vanished scroll that somehow spawned centuries of artistic echo-chamber replication. David Hinton translates, commentary flows, and we are told to expect an awakening. 

### Cartography of the Lost Scroll

Or are we? The synopsis plays it terribly safe, leaning heavily on the mystique of the East without offering much editorial footing about how these forty full-color reproductions will actually hold up under modern scrutiny—though I suspect most buyers will simply frame the pages and call it a day, absolute cringe move. But wait, perhaps the material demands this reverent ambiguity. After all, when dealing with Ch’an Buddhist insight translated for contemporary drawing-rooms, clarity is often the first casualty. 

### The Aesthetics of Wandering

Poetry as painting. Painting as poetry. It is a tired conceit traded by dusty ambassadors and retired ministers who collect jade cabbages to fill the empty hours between state funerals. Yet, Hinton usually cuts through the academic fluff with a sharp blade. The aesthetic framing here attempts to turn geography into enlightenment, rendering twenty coordinate points on a map into a spiritual ledger. We are promised a constellation of interrelated words and images. One wonders if the actual printing quality will justify the price tag, or if this is merely another coffee-table ornament for people who prefer their philosophical transcendence pre-packaged and sanitized.
