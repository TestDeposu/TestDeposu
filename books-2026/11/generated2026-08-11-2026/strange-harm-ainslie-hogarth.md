**Author:** Ainslie Hogarth  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Another domestic thriller about a missing child and the media circus that follows. Groundbreaking. One can practically hear the advance checks clearing across Brooklyn while reading the pitch. Ainslie Hogarth clearly wants to bottle that jagged, unhinged lightning of *Motherthing* again, but trading visceral domestic grotesquery for a true-crime satire feels like a lateral move into the overcrowded pool of Shirley Jackson-meets-Netflix-adaptation pastiches. 

### The Mid-Century Media Rot

The premise trades on our collective, rotting obsession with missing white women and the true-crime industrial complex. Fine. It is a fat target. Yet the synopsis leans so heavily into the manufactured media tropes—the weeping father, the poised mother, the parasitic podcaster—that I worry the pacing will stall out in a swamp of meta-commentary. Or perhaps the pacing will actually fly, clipping along at a neat, breathless clip that ignores the structural bloat typical of modern suspense. Actually, scratch that. Hogarth has a nasty, feral style that usually rescues her from her own high-concept traps. The narrative engine here relies entirely on whether the middle section drags under the weight of fifteen years of narrative stagnation, which it inevitably will because true crime as a genre is structurally repetitive by design.

### Folklore Versus Forensic Cynicism

Gold River provides a neat little claustrophobic sandbox. A haunted wood. A local myth named Druda. A grieving family fracturing along the fault lines of public perception. Calvin believes in the fairy-tale monster pulling his daughter into another world; Annette performs grief for the cameras like a pageant queen. And yet, I am utterly exhausted by the folkloric pivot in contemporary fiction. Every single literary horror novel right now feels obligated to use local folklore as a cheap metaphor for repressed trauma or grief. Give me a straight procedural over another fairy-tale metaphor any day of the week. Wait. Let me retract that entirely. Straight procedurals are dreadfully boring. We need the folkloric nonsense, or else what are we even doing here? The Druda spirit angle is precisely the kind of weird, slippery surrealism Hogarth excels at, provided she doesn't pull her punches in the final act. If this book doesn't end in complete, hallucinatory disaster, I will want my nonexistent money back. 

This will either be a sharp, venomous scalpel of a novel or a bloated mess that collapses under the weight of its own cleverness. My money is on the latter, but I have been wrong before, usually when cocktails were involved. 

Now, if you will excuse me, I have to go scream at a florist about the catastrophic lack of imported French ranunculus for the upcoming launch party.
