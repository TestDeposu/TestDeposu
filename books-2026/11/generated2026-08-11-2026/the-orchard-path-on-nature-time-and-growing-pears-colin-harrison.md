**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Another manuscript promising an antidote to modernity. How terribly pedestrian. One can scarcely open a parcel these days without tumbling into a pile of solemn prose about grown men fleeing civilization for the dirt. Colin Harrison has evidently decided to follow the well-trodden mud of Henry David Thoreau and every trust-fund homesteader who ever romanticized a hoe. Seven neglected acres in Suffolk County. No electricity. No running water. Just a man, an abandoned potato farm, and the desperate urge to escape the relentless ping of a smartphone. 

### The Cult of the Overdue Pear

We are told, through the predictable machinery of publishing synopses, that this upcoming volume braids together the history of pear varieties with personal salvation. Because nothing cures existential dread quite like horticulture. The blurb insists upon the charm of wood ladders, bees, and raccoons—as if nature curated her wildlife exclusively for the aesthetic pleasure of stressed urbanites. It reads less like an original observation and more like a rejected pitch for a quarterly supplement in a lifestyle magazine meant for the Hamptons weekend set. We are promised an ode to patience. We are promised the profound silence of a life stripped of deadlines. One expects the prose to smell faintly of expensive linen and artisanal compost. 

### Notes on the Boredom of the Soil

The market is entirely saturated with this agrarian posturing. It is the literary equivalent of buying pre-distressed designer jeans at triple the cost. Whenever the culture at large begins to fray at the seams—perhaps during the next inevitable collapse of a major financial institution or another catastrophic auction house scandal—the publishing houses rush to flood the mahogany tables with pastoral retreats. Harrison bought an overgrown plot thirty years ago, and we are only now being subjected to the fruit of his quietude. Or rather, the fruit of his marketing team's timing. 

Expect the prose to lean heavily on words like *measured* and *lush*. Expect long, agonizing passages about the precise angle of sunlight hitting a branch while the author ponders his own mortality over a glass of dry cider. It is designed entirely for readers who want to feel intellectual while contemplating a piece of fruit. There is no grit here, only the sanitized, romanticized version of manual labor performed by someone who can presumably leave whenever the plumbing breaks permanently. 

Give me Edith Wharton or give me nothing. At least her characters destroyed each other in drawing rooms with proper lighting and functioning silver service, rather than whining about raccoons in the brush. 

The toddler has hidden my favorite fountain pen again.
