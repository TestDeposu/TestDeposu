They are selling us *The Secret Garden* again. As if we haven't spent the last decade cowering behind scented candles while the world burns down to structural ash. Tessa Hadley has apparently decided that what humanity desperately requires in the year 2026 is another polite little stroll through the domestic parlor. Jane Austen. Henry James. Elizabeth Bowen. The usual pantheon of dead Europeans who wrote extensively about drawing-room etiquette while empires collapsed outside their windows. 

Look. The official marketing copy practically purports to cure seasonal depression with a Ladybird book and a damp cup of Earl Grey. "Life has been made vivid by reading," it whispers. How quaint. Some of us require a few hundred pages of flayed skin, geopolitical betrayal, and dark sorcery just to feel the faint, dull throb of a pulse beneath our ribs. But no. We are supposed to swoon over sailboats in *Swallows* and *Amazons*. We are supposed to find profound spiritual awakening in the familiar furnishings of a British middle-class routine. 

### Realism as an Escape Hatch

What infuriates me about this entire enterprise is the sheer, unadulterated cowardice of literary realism when applied as a balm. Hadley wants to convince us that fiction makes life "richer, deeper, and more fully imagined." Give me a break. It is the literary equivalent of the Fyre Festival catering tent—all aestheticized promises and zero nutritional value. Alice Munro and Colm Tóibín are brilliant, yes. But deploying them to validate the safety of the ordinary world feels like hiring an executioner to knit you a sweater. 

### The Audit of the Soul

And what about the actual text? Oh, right. Nobody has read it yet. We are staring blankly at a synopsis that relies entirely on the word "luminous" to do heavy lifting. Luminous. The exact adjective used by publishing houses when they want to charge forty dollars for three hundred pages of people talking quietly about who gets to sit by the window. It is the literary equivalent of a Boeing 737 door plug flying off at thirty thousand feet, except instead of decompression, you just get fifty pages of Henry James subtext. 

Give me the darkest, most depraved grimdark fantasy on the market over this soft-focus nostalgia trip any day of the week. At least Joe Abercrombie has the decency to admit that we are all living in a latrine. Hadley wants us to peer through the windowpane and admire the curtains. 

Somebody needs to tell the public relations department that the collective nervous system of the species is currently fried. We are living through the modern economic equivalent of the Titanic string quartet playing while the hull buckles, and we are being offered an essay collection on the enduring joy of reading *Jane Eyre*. 

I would love to offer a balanced critique. But I simply cannot muster the charity. Between managing three different portfolios that are currently bleeding faster than a Victorian consumptive and trying to find a decent dry cleaner in Manhattan who won't completely ruin a bespoke cashmere overcoat with basic chemical negligence, my tolerance for literary comfort food is hovering around absolute zero.
