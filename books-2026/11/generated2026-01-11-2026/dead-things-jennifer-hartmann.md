**Author:** Jennifer Hartmann  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Staring at yet another pitch built entirely on recycled procedural jargon makes my left eye twitch with a violence usually reserved for cartoon characters. Jennifer Hartmann returns with *Dead Things*, and honestly, the corpse cataloging tropes are already gathering dust on my desk before the manuscript even arrives. Let us be entirely real here. The pitch relies on the oldest trick in the dark romance playbook: take a sterile, clinical environment—in this case, a forensic table surrounded by seven dead bodies—and throw the heroine directly into the muddy arms of the fugitive mentor she used to idolize. 

The synopsis screams BookTok optimization. We have the pristine forensic assistant—because of course she counts wounds and dissects silence—forced into the woods with a man accused of murder. It is *Still Beating* meets an episode of *CSI* written by someone who has never stepped foot in a morgue, yet somehow understands the exact aerodynamic velocity of a lingering glance over a cadaver. 

### The Aesthetics of Compulsory Captivity

Let us talk about the wilderness dynamic. Because running into the trees with a suspected serial killer is apparently the hottest new form of team-building exercise. The thematic core here hinges entirely on proximity-driven delusion. The lines blur. They always blur. You start the book calling him a monster, and by chapter twelve, you are sharing granola bars in a damp cave while debating the finer philosophical points of forensic pathology. 

Actually, wait. Let me walk that back. Maybe the woods setting offers a genuinely claustrophobic claustrophobia—if the author actually leans into the psychological rot rather than rushing straight to the breathless bodice-ripping by chapter three. Hartmann has a knack for emotional bludgeoning, and I suppose I respect an author who refuses to subtlety her way through a trauma bond. 

### The Predictable Trajectory of Obsession

The premise demands a suspension of disbelief thicker than cheap pancake makeup. Seven bodies land on the table, every single trail points to the brilliant mentor, and yet he is somehow the only one who can set her free? Groundbreaking. Truly, nobody has ever written a dark romance where the captor is simultaneously the ultimate protector. Penelope Douglas and Haunting Adeline fans are going to devour this like starving wolves, while I sit here wondering if anyone in contemporary fiction is allowed to simply call the police. 

Yet the prose might save it. Hartmann knows how to milk a angsty, morally bankrupt dynamic for all it is worth. If the dialogue avoids sounding like an outdated fanfiction and leans into the sheer, ugly desperation of two people clinging to each other at the end of the world, this might actually survive the crushing weight of its own synopsis. Or it will collapse under the sheer exhaustion of the predator-protector trope. Time will tell. 

Right now, my inbox is mocking me with forty-seven more queries about mafia accountants and shadow daddies, and I am entirely out of patience for men who live in the woods and swear they did not commit the murders. I am going to go brew a cup of lapsang souchong and ponder my existential dread.
