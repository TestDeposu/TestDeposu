**Author:** Natasha Preston  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

The snow’s already falling in the synopsis—*literally*—and it’s not just atmospheric. It’s a metaphor for how this thing’s gonna read: thick, suffocating, the kind of whiteout that makes you question whether you’re still standing. Natasha Preston’s *The Lodge* arrives with the same kind of *snowed-in* precision as *The Guest List* but with the psychological frostbite of *Sharp Objects*—if Gillian Flynn and Ruth Ware had a lovechild trapped in a ski resort’s HVAC system.

The premise is *classic* locked-room thriller territory, but the execution risks drowning in its own *cozy-catastrophe* irony. Hallie and her friends—*the usual suspects*—think they’re trading their WFH hellscape for a week of powdery escapism. Instead, they get a *very* specific kind of hellscape: one where the only escape is through the blizzard, and the only company is a rotating cast of lodge denizens who might as well be characters in a *Twilight Zone* episode if they weren’t so *painfully* human. The synopsis leans hard into the *isolation-as-trope* bit, but where’s the subversion? Where’s the moment where the reader realizes the real horror isn’t the killer—it’s the *loneliness*? Because right now, it feels like Preston’s just repackaging the same old *stranded women vs. the man in the basement* formula, only with skis instead of a chainsaw.

The *strangers* angle is *deliciously* vague—friendly young couple? Two brothers? A mysterious worker?—which is either a masterstroke of tension or a lazy way to avoid fleshing out actual characters. Preston’s strength has always been in her *unreliable narrators*, but here, the synopsis gives us *zero* indication of who’s lying, who’s hiding, or who’s just *really* bad at poker faces. It’s like reading a *Choose Your Own Adventure* book where the only choice is whether to trust the guy who smells like pine cleaner or the one who keeps adjusting his scarf. And don’t even get me started on the *bodies piling up*—because if this follows the trend of Preston’s work, the real horror won’t be the violence, but the *bureaucracy* of it. Who calls the cops when you’re trapped in a lodge with no signal? Who’s the first to suggest *maybe* the victim *deserved it*? That’s where the *meat* should be, not in some generic "killer in plain sight" reveal that could’ve been pulled from a *Hallmark Horror* script.

Thematically, this feels like a *snowed-in* version of *The Silent Patient*—where the real trauma isn’t the murder, but the *unspoken*. The lodge itself is a character, sure, but it’s the kind of character that’s *always* been there, like the *haunted house* trope but with better insulation. The blizzard is just the *excuse*—the real horror is the *people*. And yet, the synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at what makes this lodge different from any other ski resort where someone’s bound to go missing. Is it the *architecture*? The *history*? The *way the snow muffles the screams*? Or is it just that, like *The Guest List*, the real villain is *the expectation* that this is supposed to be fun?

I’ll say this for Preston: she knows how to sell the *atmosphere*. The snow, the isolation, the *slow burn* of paranoia—it’s all there, dripping with the kind of *creepy-cute* tension that makes you want to book a flight to a cabin you’ll never actually stay in. But where’s the *twist*? Where’s the *moment* that makes you slap your knee and go, *"Ohhh, she’s playing with us"*? Right now, it feels like Preston’s just *setting up* the same old *locked-room* game, and we’ve all seen this movie before—*The Strangers*, *The Cabin in the Woods*, *that one time I got snowed in with my ex and his new girlfriend*. The synopsis reads like a *checklist* of thriller tropes, not a *promise* of something fresh.

And yet—*and yet*—there’s something *deliciously* unreliable about this whole thing. The way the synopsis *hints* at a killer in plain sight but never *shows* us who it is. The way it *dangles* the idea of a psychological unraveling but doesn’t *commit* to any one character’s descent. It’s like reading a *mystery novel* where the author keeps whispering, *"But what if it’s not who you think?"*—and then never delivering. Maybe that’s the point. Maybe *The Lodge* is supposed to be *that* book: the one where the reader’s own paranoia does the work for them.
