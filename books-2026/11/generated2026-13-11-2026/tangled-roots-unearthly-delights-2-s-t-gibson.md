**Author:** S.T. Gibson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

God save us from another hollow exercise in transactional hedonism marketed as high literature. S.T. Gibson returns with *Tangled Roots*, dropping us right back into the exhaustingly decadent world of *Savage Blooms*. My inbox at the firm pinged with sixty unread messages before nine this morning, yet my mind somehow drifts to this—a plot about rescuing some poor chap named Adam from the faery underworld through the sheer force of scheming and seduction. The premise reads like a lesser Milan Kundera novel rewritten by a TikTok algorithm obsessed with velvet corsets and minor key cello covers. 

### The Currency of Boredom

Notice the central conflict of the faery court according to the synopsis: entertain the king, or suffer his boredom. How devastatingly pedestrian. We are meant to tremble before an immortal sovereign whose primary existential threat is a lack of stimulating evening programming. It gives off strong Fyre Festival VIP tent energy. One pictures the court designer frantically rushing to secure an avant-garde ice sculpture while the nobility mutters into their champagne flutes. Eileen, Finley, and Nicola are supposedly plunging into a centuries-old power struggle, yet the text reduces their grand geopolitical maneuvering down to a desperate hustle to survive a monarch with the attention span of an agitated executive producer at a network pitch meeting. 

### Hedonism Without Stakes

Pleasure and pain elevated to art. That is the marketing hook. But art requires discipline, irony, and distance. What we get instead is a parade of cheap temptations where every dark desire comes with a convenient receipt. Characters are supposedly torn between heroism and having their wildest dreams fulfilled. Yet true seduction demands ambiguity. When a faery king dangles immediate gratification, the internal conflict feels about as profound as choosing between a third negroni or calling an Uber XL. Gibson leans heavily into the eroticism of transactional power, which is fine if you enjoy your romance novels structured like a boutique consulting contract. But spare me the existential angst. 

The synopsis leaves us swimming in a puddle of vague architectural nouns and emotional platitudes. Craigmar's curse sounds less like a deeply embedded mythic tragedy and more like a restrictive lease agreement in a brutalist Kensington apartment block. And yet, the readership will devour it. They will line up around the block, clutching their special-edition sprayed edges, eager to consume another hundred pages of glistening prose about people who cannot keep their hands—or their psychological projections—to themselves. 

My tailor is waiting to fit me for the upcoming awards ceremony.
