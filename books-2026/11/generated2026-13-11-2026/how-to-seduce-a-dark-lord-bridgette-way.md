The morning chill rattles the glass panes of the conservatory while a dying November gale strips the last stubborn copper leaves from the weeping willow outside. Frost bites the borders of the herbaceous beds. Yet I am huddled here by the hearth, sipping a vintage tawny port and nursing a profound, uncharacteristic tenderness in my chest. Yes, a tender heart. A dangerous mutation for one whose literary diet usually consists of blood-soaked tragedies and bleak modernist despair. Bridgette Way’s impending *How to Seduce a Dark Lord* arrives on the horizon like a rogue heatwave, threatening to melt my frostbitten misanthropy with what the marketing copy breathlessly terms a queer paranormal rom-com debut. God help us all. 

### The Necromantic Art of Husbandry

Consider the premise. A necromancer prodigy—blessed with the moniker New York’s Most Darling Devil—decides that the crushing bureaucratic burden of villainy is vastly inferior to securing a lucrative spot as a trophy husband to a terrifying shadow-monarch. It is a mood. A supreme, unabashed vibe. We are slouching away from the earnest, sweat-soaked moral agonizing of traditional fantasy and leaning hard into the decadent luxury of marrying rich and letting someone else handle the architectural terror of the Northeast. Kieran Prentiss possesses my entire heart. He is simultaneously a practitioner of the dark arts and an absolute rat-boy chasing his bag. 

Way is clearly channeling the biting, champagne-soaked banter of Oscar Wilde if Wilde spent his Tuesday nights plotting necromantic PR campaigns over artisanal martinis. We are miles away from the grueling, Tolkien-esque slogs that dominate the current market. Give me camp. Give me undead PR spin. 

### The Mechanics of Lying to Your Sugar Daddy

Yet the synopsis hints at structural peril. An assassination plot. A competition gone awry. And—most dreadfully—feelings. Blech. Can we simply let gorgeous, morally bankrupt people scheme in peace without forcing them into the agonizing crucible of emotional vulnerability? The tension between calculated deception and genuine affection is older than petroglyphs. It is the narrative equivalent of eating cotton candy for dinner; delightful initially, followed shortly by a violent gastric reckoning. 

If Way manages to thread this needle without devolving into saccharine YA-adjacent moralizing—if she preserves the feral, glittering malice of a true dark comedy—this might actually redeem the dismal landscape of contemporary genre fiction. Think *Red, White & Royal Blue* meets a necromantic coup d'état, but written with actual syntactic grace. Or perhaps it will be an absolute dumpster fire of missed opportunities and tepid prose. One must maintain guarded optimism, even as the wind howls like a banshee against the conservatory door and the embers turn to ash. 

I must draw this to a swift close. The stonemason is waiting in the drawing room, and I really must review the architectural blueprints for my new beach house before the frost hardens into permanent permafrost.
