The mahogany paneling in this room smells faintly of decaying velvet and terribly expensive gin, perfectly matching the mood of a literary symposium that has already overstayed its welcome by at least three Chiantis. 

### The Chronological Arrogance of Academia

We are told A.F. Steadman is pivoting from middle-grade heroics to adult fantasy with *TimeLess*, bringing along a protagonist named Artemis who graduated top of her Cambridge class of assassins. Because nothing screams Ivy League rigor quite like a lethal GPA. 

And yet, the synopsis reads like a feverish pitch meeting at an agency desperate to cash in on the dark academia craze before the trend goes the way of Elizabeth Holmes' turtlenecks. 

### Chrono-Espionage and the Aesthetic of Ruthlessness

The world-building relies on a crew—navigator, armorer, historian, medic—which feels suspiciously like a heist movie trapped inside a physics textbook. But what strikes me, sitting here pretending to care about the keynote speaker's PowerPoint, is the delicious absurdity of an assassin who discards lovers like used espresso pods while harboring a terrible secret. 

Such dramatic heavy lifting. Artemis is apparently ruthless, yet she suffers from the classic literary affliction of making terrible tactical choices the second an enigmatic man in a trench coat enters the frame. 

She encounters a time vandal. She breaks all the rules and lets him live. 

Groundbreaking. Truly, the Oppenheimer of chronal warfare. 

### The Pathology of Obsession

The central tension hinges on why this man is everywhere she is and why she cannot bring herself to execute him. One suspects the answer involves smolder, cheekbones, and zero emotional availability. 

The prose promises a high-stakes spy thriller where the future is fragile, but the emotional architecture feels distinctly domestic. You spend your formative years at Cambridge mastering the lethal arts, only to develop main-character syndrome the moment an antagonist exhibits mild eye-contact. 

Call it chronological Stockholm syndrome. Call it mid-twenties burnout brought on by too many supervision sessions in damp stone libraries. 

Either way, the marketing leans heavily into the kinetic energy of cat-and-mouse games. Let us hope the actual sentences possess more grit than the boilerplate jacket copy, which sounds suspiciously manufactured by an algorithm trained exclusively on Sarah J. Maas press releases and rejected Bond scripts. 

The oysters at lunch were entirely subpar, ruining my creative flow.
