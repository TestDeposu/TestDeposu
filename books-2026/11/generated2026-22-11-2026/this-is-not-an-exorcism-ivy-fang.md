Watching these parents scramble toward the school gates is a study in frantic mediocrity. The desperate reach for a fleeting sense of parental adequacy is almost as pitiable as the premise of Ivy Fang’s debut, *This Is Not an Exorcism*. One wonders if the literary market has finally run out of original sin, or if we are merely doomed to recycle the "con artist with a heart of gold" archetype until the heat death of the universe. 

### The Aesthetics of Fraud

Laurel Meng, our supposed protagonist, purports to exorcise the spirits of the dead while merely managing the neuroses of the bored and gullible. It is a cynical maneuver, certainly. Yet, there is a pedestrian quality to the setup that reeks of modern commercial desperation. Fang clearly hopes to lean into the macabre, but the synopsis reads like a script rejected by a mid-tier streaming service seeking to capitalize on the *Grady Hendrix* flavor of the month. Ghosts as comedic foils? A bed and breakfast housing secrets? It feels less like a narrative and more like an episode of *The Real Housewives of Beverly Hills* staged at a colonial-era cemetery. It is all so terribly gauche.

### The Problem with Poppy Creek

The author promises us a "visceral" experience, a term so abused in publishing circles it has lost all remaining utility. I suspect we are in for a slog through the standard tropes of Asian American diasporic trauma, commodified for a public that enjoys their ghost stories with a side of sociological critique. If Taika—the ghost, of course—becomes the inevitable love interest or the pithy companion, the novel will likely collapse under the weight of its own attempt to be both irreverent and profound. It reminds me of the disastrous 1989 restoration of the Sistine Chapel frescoes: far too much scrubbing, far too little reverence for the original intent, and ultimately, a garish smear where something subtle should have been. 

The synopsis leaves far too much to chance. Where is the architecture of the haunting? Are we meant to fear the bones of the earth, or simply the lack of originality in the prose? I find the reliance on "boundless ignorance" as a plot device for the clients to be a lazy shortcut for an author who perhaps cannot fathom how to write a truly cunning antagonist. 

If this book possesses any real teeth, it will need to move past the tired trope of the "reluctant hero uncovering the truth." We have seen this performance before, usually delivered with a smug wink at the reader that suggests the writer is far cleverer than their audience. I shall require something significantly stronger than the watered-down drivel of a YA-adjacent haunting to keep my interest. My sommelier has finally secured that rare batch of Pappy Van Winkle 23, and I have no intention of lingering on this pedestrian nonsense any longer than the school bell allows.
