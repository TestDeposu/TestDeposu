**Author:** Rachel Reid  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Unrivaled* is so deliberately vague it reads like a hockey puck being passed between two players who’ve already decided the game’s outcome—except the crowd hasn’t gotten to see the play yet. We’re told Ilya and Shane are out, married, *finally* playing on the same team, and yet the real tension isn’t their love story (which we’ve already witnessed in *Heated Rivalry* and its sequels) but the backlash from a segment of the hockey world that’s still clinging to the idea that love between two men on the ice is a *distraction*—as if a player’s personal life should ever be a liability, not a narrative. The synopsis leans into the *Game Changers* formula: high-stakes professional rivalry, slow-burn romance, and the inevitable cultural pushback that makes the love story feel like a rebellion. But where’s the edge? Where’s the *Twilight* of hockey romances—where the real drama isn’t the couple but the world they’re fighting to change?

And then there’s the *Top Shelf* podcast and the #TakeBackHockey movement, which sounds like a perfect storm of online outrage and sports media’s love affair with controversy. Reid’s done this before—see *The Long Game*, where the couple had to navigate public scrutiny—but this time, the stakes feel higher because the synopsis doesn’t bother to specify *how* the backlash manifests. Is it a smear campaign? A threat to their careers? A fan boycott? The ambiguity is frustrating, like watching a highlight reel where the most exciting plays are cut away mid-action. It’s the same problem with *The Shots You Take*—the synopsis promises tension but delivers only the setup, leaving the reader to wonder if Reid’s saving her best material for the adaptation.

But let’s talk about the *real* missed opportunity here: the synopsis doesn’t even *mention* the team dynamic. Are Ilya and Shane’s teammates divided? Is there a coach who’s secretly thrilled for them but can’t admit it? Is there a rival player who sees their relationship as a weakness? Reid’s built her career on the idea that hockey is more than just a game—it’s a microcosm of society, and the *Game Changers* series thrives on that tension. Yet here, the synopsis treats the hockey world like a monolith, as if the only opposition comes from a single podcast and a hashtag. Where’s the *grit* of *Tough Guy*? Where’s the *pressure* of *Role Model*? This feels like the synopsis for a romance novel where the only conflict is a disapproving mother—except in this case, the mother is the internet.

And don’t even get me started on the *Top Shelf* podcast. It’s the kind of contrived villain that makes you wonder if Reid’s team is writing these things in a shared doc. A hockey podcast leading a movement? That’s the kind of thing that would get canceled in real life, but in Reid’s world, it’s just another obstacle for Ilya and Shane to overcome. It’s like if *The Bachelor* had a villain who was just… a group chat. The synopsis doesn’t even bother to give the movement a name that suggests depth—#TakeBackHockey is generic, like a protest sign someone Googled. Where’s the *#NoGayHockey* energy? Where’s the *#HockeyIsForStraightGuys* hashtag that would make this feel like a real cultural battle? The synopsis is so sanitized it might as well be a Disney movie about two boys who like each other but have to prove it’s okay.

Yet—here’s the thing—I *do* love that Reid’s leaning into the idea that love is still a rebellion. The synopsis makes it clear that Ilya and Shane’s relationship is no longer a secret, but the world isn’t ready for them yet. That’s the kind of tension that could make this book feel like a modern *The Bridges of Madison County*—if Reid actually committed to the metaphor. But instead, we’re left with a synopsis that’s so broad it could apply to any romance where the couple faces minor backlash. It’s like if *The Notebook* had a synopsis that just said, “Two people fall in love, but some people don’t like it.”

I can’t wait to see how Reid handles this. If she leans into the cultural battle, she could make *Unrivaled* feel like a *The Handmaid’s Tale* for hockey fans—where the real enemy isn’t just one podcast but the entire system that’s built to silence love. But if she stays in the safe zone, this might just be another book where the couple gets their happy ending and the world moves on, like *The Shots You Take* but with more skates.

Literally.
