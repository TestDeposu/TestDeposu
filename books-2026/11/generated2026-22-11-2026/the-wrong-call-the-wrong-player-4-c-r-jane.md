**Author:** C.R. Jane  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Wrong Call*—or rather, the *Press Start to Play* anthology it’s somehow masquerading as—is a masterclass in *not* telling you anything. It’s like holding up a controller and saying, "Press any button," while the manual is written in a language you don’t speak. The line *"You are standing in a room filled with books"* is either the most poetic or the most vague opening sentence since *The Shining*’s elevator pitch. Is this a choose-your-own-adventure? A meta-commentary on gaming culture? A collection of stories where the "game" is life itself? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just *hints*—like a loading screen that never resolves into a menu.

The roster alone is a flex. Ernest Cline’s foreword is the cherry on top of a sundae that already includes *Ready Player One*’s co-conspirators, *The Expanse*’s Marc Laidlaw, and *The Locked Tomb*’s Seanan McGuire. This isn’t just a crossover—it’s a *collaboration*. But here’s the rub: the synopsis doesn’t explain *how* these stories interact. Are they all connected? Is this a *Dungeons & Dragons* campaign where the players are the authors? Or is it just a party where everyone brought their own game and no one bothered to set the rules? The lack of clarity is almost *deliberate*—like a game designer leaving the difficulty setting on "insane" and calling it "immersive."

The "inventory" section is the most interesting part, though. Keys. A cell phone. A wallet. This isn’t a *Skyrim* loot list—it’s a *Groundhog Day* prop list. What does it mean to be "standing in a room" with these objects? Are you a player stuck in a glitch? A critic analyzing the mechanics of storytelling? The synopsis doesn’t say. But the way it frames it—*"What would you like to do?"*—suggests this isn’t just a book. It’s an *experience*. And that’s either genius or a gimmick.

The theatrical persona instruction is the most revealing part of the whole thing. "You are a broke university student demanding scientific accuracy in sci-fi." That’s not a character—it’s a *lens*. The entire anthology is being viewed through the cynicism of someone who’s spent too long in a library arguing with grad students about whether *Blade Runner*’s rain is physically plausible. And yet—here’s the pivot—the synopsis doesn’t *actually* give us any stories to critique. It just sets up the *vibe*. It’s like being handed a *D&D* character sheet without the adventure.

The real question is: is this anthology a *game* or a *book*? The synopsis oscillates between the two like a *Metroid* boss that respawns every time you die. If it’s a book, it’s a missed opportunity—because the synopsis doesn’t do the work of *selling* the stories. If it’s a game, it’s a gimmick—because the "rules" are so vague they might as well be written in smoke. Either way, the lack of specificity is *annoying*. It’s like a *Tetris* game where the blocks keep changing shape mid-drop.

But then again—maybe that’s the point. Maybe the entire anthology is about the *failure* of clarity. The way games promise immersion but deliver glitches. The way sci-fi promises worlds but delivers notes. The way anthologies promise cohesion but deliver a mishmash of voices. The synopsis doesn’t say. And that, perhaps, is the most *Press Start to Play*-esque thing about it.
