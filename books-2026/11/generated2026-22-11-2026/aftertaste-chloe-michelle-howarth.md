**Author:** Chloe Michelle Howarth  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Aftertaste* is so deliberately vague it feels like a love letter written in lipstick on a foggy mirror—beautiful in its ambiguity, but already fading before you can read it. There’s no concrete timeline beyond the 2010 recession, no clear emotional arc beyond "illusions shatter," and the class divide is reduced to a single, almost throwaway line: "privileged, wealthy via birth." It’s the kind of synopsis that makes you wonder if the author is testing whether readers will fill in the blanks with their own fantasies or resentments. Because let’s be honest—this isn’t just a story about two women; it’s a story about the *idea* of two women, the way their relationship becomes a Rorschach test for the reader’s own biases about queerness, domesticity, and survival.

The prose is already being marketed as "lush, searing," which is either a promise of literary excess or a warning that we’re about to be drowned in metaphor. Howarth’s previous work—*Sunburn* and *Heap Earth Upon It*—suggests a writer who thrives on atmospheric density, the kind of prose that clings to the page like cigarette smoke in a dimly lit room. But here, the synopsis does little more than sketch the contours of a familiar trope: the wealthy queer woman and the working-class lover, the idyllic summer that turns to winter, the recession as the ultimate third wheel. It’s the kind of setup that could easily devolve into melodrama if not handled with precision. The question isn’t whether the book will be good—it’s whether it will *earn* its lushness. Because right now, it feels like a promise rather than a delivery.

The synopsis also does a masterful job of avoiding any real emotional stakes beyond the obvious. "The small cruelties, the estrangements, the desire simmering underneath it all"—this could apply to any long-term relationship, queer or otherwise. The recession is framed as the catalyst, but what about the *before*? The honeymoon phase, the power dynamics, the way Aly’s privilege isn’t just about money but about the unspoken rules of her world? The synopsis gives us a car ride, a plush bed, a move back to Meadhbh’s hometown—all of which sound like the setup for a rom-com if not for the recession looming like a storm cloud. But where’s the tension? Where’s the moment when Aly’s wealth isn’t just a backdrop but an active force, shaping decisions, erasing boundaries, or forcing Meadhbh to choose between comfort and autonomy?

This is the kind of book that could easily become a vehicle for clichés—"the working-class heroine who falls for the rich girl," "the recession as the ultimate villain," "the love that conquers all (or doesn’t)." But if Howarth is anything like she was in *Heap Earth Upon It*, where the real tension came from the way language itself could be both a weapon and a refuge, then *Aftertaste* might subvert expectations. The synopsis doesn’t give us enough to say whether it will be a character study, a social commentary, or a love letter to the idea of home. But it does give us enough to know that it won’t be shallow.

The most interesting part of this synopsis is its refusal to commit to a clear narrative arc. It’s not a tragedy, not a triumph, not even a clear-cut romance—it’s a *state of being*. The recession isn’t just an external force; it’s a metaphor for the way relationships unravel, the way illusions crumble, the way two people who were once inseparable suddenly find themselves on opposite sides of a chasm they didn’t see until it was too late. It’s the kind of story that could feel like a modern retelling of *The Great Gatsby*, if Gatsby were a queer woman and the valley of ashes was a London flat that suddenly can’t afford to heat.

But here’s the thing—this synopsis is so sparse that it’s almost *too* perfect. It’s the literary equivalent of a teaser trailer that doesn’t give away the plot but leaves you wondering if you’ll even like the movie. It’s the kind of writing that makes you want to read the book just to see how much Howarth will make you feel. And that’s either a sign of genius or a sign that the author is playing a very dangerous game.
