**Page Count:** Unknown

The L train shudders over rusted iron while November’s icy damp seeps straight through my wool coat—just fantastic. And yet, here I am, glaring at the latest promotional copy for Tricia Levenseller’s *How Rage Burns*. Another dark romantasy dropped onto my radar, promising violence, proximity tropes, and enough edge to cut glass. But let us be entirely serious for a moment. Does anyone actually read these things for the plot, or are we all just collectively waiting to see how violently these fictional aristocrats manage to weaponize their unresolved mommy issues? 

### A Symphony of Severed Appendages

The premise is gloriously unhinged. We have a protagonist named Ydra—because of course her name sounds like a final boss in an Elden Ring expansion—who commands armies while running an elaborate foster-front out of her harem, only to casually decide she needs to hunt down and literally kidnap a husband across enemy lines. Peak Sarah J. Maas-adjacent energy, mixed with the unhinged bureaucratic efficiency of Rebecca Yarros. But then we get to the prince. Canus of Brutus. A man so utterly devoid of self-preservation that a literal stalker leaves him a bouquet of severed fingers as a courting gift—yes, you read that correctly, a floral arrangement of human digits—and his primary reaction is mild annoyance because he has a war to attend to. Make it make sense. The juxtaposition of gothic horror aesthetics with high-fantasy military campaigns is doing heavy lifting here. It is peak brain-rot literature, and honestly? We ate it up. 

### The Commodification of Carnage

Leaning hard into the edgelord market, the synopsis explicitly warns us about graphic sexual content and darker themes, which is industry-speak for *the author watched one too many dark romance TikTok edits and decided to cash out*. Yet, the thematic spine here actually looks genuinely fascinating beneath the gloss. We are staring down an interrogation of institutionalized power versus bodily autonomy. Ydra rejects docile men—she wants friction, she wants resistance, she wants a partner who mirrors her own lethal competence. Canus, meanwhile, is fighting a literal shadow-monster inside his own soul that somehow reacts hungrily to her presence. It is heavy-handed psychosexual scaffolding, sure, but it works. It is toxic waste, and I am diving in headfirst. Call it character growth. Call it psychological desperation on my daily commute. Either way, Levenseller knows her audience is feral for men who need serious psychiatric intervention and women who can bench-press them. Prediction? Half the fandom is going to miss the entire sociopolitical commentary on militarism because they are too busy losing their minds over a tent scene. 

The train brakes screech with enough friction to shatter enamel, and the persistent draft coming from my poorly insulated window is unbearable.
