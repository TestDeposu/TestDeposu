**Author:** Katherine Center  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Dust motes dance in the fractured light filtering through these broken floorboards, settling onto the hem of my coat like the forgotten years I’m trying to excavate from this hollow shell of a building. It feels like standing inside a skeleton. I suppose *The Lovebirds*—Katherine Center’s next foray into the commercially polished heartache we’ve come to expect—will aim for that same spectral quality, though likely with better lighting and significantly fewer structural hazards. The premise, if we can call this thin slice of digital deception a premise, hinges on that tired, creaking floorboard of a trope: the long-distance masquerade. Addie Mitchell, a woman tethered to the whims of a middle-school tormentor, decides to rewrite her reality by impersonating her boss in correspondence with a man who seemingly has nothing better to do than write to a former flame from thousands of miles away. Is it charming? It’s arguably fraudulent. Center has carved a niche for herself—think Emily Henry’s suburban cousins or a less frantic Sarah Adams—but there is a persistent, gnawing vagueness in the setup that bothers me. Why does the PhD in Antarctica settle for emails? Why is the deception the primary engine of the narrative rather than the emotional vacuum that presumably necessitates it?

Absurd.

Why did my daughter insist on binge-watching that abysmal show about the talking rescue puppies for three straight years? The repetition of those chirpy, synthetic voices still vibrates in the back of my skull like a low-grade migraine whenever I try to focus on anything of actual substance. Those dogs had more personality-driven conflict in a single five-minute segment than most contemporary protagonists possess in three hundred pages, and frankly, I resent the parallel; if I’m forced to witness a grown woman fumble through a digital catfishing scandal, I expect a level of emotional stakes that aren’t just placeholders for a happy ending that feels inevitably manufactured.

Catfishing as a meet-cute is the literary equivalent of serving instant coffee at a gala—it’s functional, but it leaves a bitter, artificial aftertaste that lingers long after you’ve turned the page. The thematic weight here rests on the fragile tension between the curated digital self and the messy, physical reality—a classic anxiety for the current decade. Center clearly wants to explore if intimacy can survive the shock of a revelation. Yet, I suspect the book will prioritize the banter, that familiar, rhythmic ping-pong of dialogue that serves as the genre’s primary sedative. If the synopsis is any indication, the plot is a house of cards waiting for the Antarctic PhD to board a plane to Texas. It’s a clean setup. Perhaps too clean. The real question isn't whether he’ll fly the coop, but whether we, the readers, are meant to care about the truth at all, or just the performance of it.

I must translate a particularly stubborn piece of French existential poetry.
