**Page Count:** Unknown

Staring out the frosted window at these pathetic, rotting November leaves plastered against the glass, I am plagued by a severe, bone-deep nostalgia for an era when marketing copy didn't rely on unhinged cinematic math to sell a novel. We are supposed to swoon over the intellectual collision of *Crazy Rich Asians* and *K-Pop Demon Hunters*—as if billionaires and spectral vengeance are novel commodities. But Monika Kim won a Bram Stoker, so perhaps we extend a tiny shred of grace before rolling our eyes into the back of our skulls. *What Hungers in the Dark* dares to bridge 2022 Seoul with 1891 Hanseong, dragging the putrid baggage of patriarchal legacy straight through the sepia-toned corridors of dynastic wealth. 

### Gilt-Edged Horrors and Dynastic Crapola

And what do we actually know about the plot? Absolutely nothing, because modern synopses are allergic to substance. A brother vanishes. A billionaire dynasty hides secrets behind a manicured facade. A queer romance in the nineteenth century triggers a bloody hereditary curse because fathers would rather mutate their sons into literal monsters than let them love freely. You can't make this stuff up. It is peak Gothic melodrama—give me all the rotting flesh and repressed longing, no cap. We are looking at a text that desperately wants to dissect how patriarchal entitlement devours its own children, wrapping feminist fury in an opulent, blood-soaked Hanbok. 

Yet, I am exhausted by the constant insistence that historical suffering needs a glossy, high-concept elevator pitch to matter. Why must 1891 Korea be dressed up like a prestige television pilot just to get modern readers to care about queer persecution and generational trauma? Monika Kim is too sharp a writer for this cheap packaging, channeling the icy claustrophobia of Shirley Jackson mixed with the atmospheric bite of Patricia Park, yet the publisher insists on selling it like a beach read for girlbosses who love a good corporate assassination. Make it make sense. 

### The Aesthetic of Suffocation

The real meat of this text—whenever it finally lands on my desk—won't be the billionaire gloss. It will be the silence of nineteenth-century Hanseong. The way patriarchal power structures demand absolute obedience until the blood starts leaking through the floorboards. Charlotte Chang running around modern Seoul looking for her missing brother is fine, but the historical half of the narrative is where the actual teeth are. Blood, obsession, and legacy. The holy trinity of gothic fiction that never misses when executed with actual structural rigor. I just pray the dual timeline doesn't devolve into a messy, exposition-heavy info-dump. Give us atmosphere. Give us the suffocating weight of a dynasty that refuses to die. 

Time to drag myself away from this window and pack three lunches that will inevitably return uneaten.
