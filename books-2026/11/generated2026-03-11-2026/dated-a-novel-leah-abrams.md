**Author:** Leah Abrams  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Look, I swear to the gods of quantum mechanics that if I read one more contemporary debut about a twenty-something political staffer whose biggest existential crisis involves a rogue grandparent on a dating app, I am going to drop out of my thermodynamics lectures and permanently move into the university library basement. *Dated: A Novel* by Leah Abrams is slated for 2026, and already the literary commentariat is losing its collective mind over this plot. Sarah Thankam Mathews calls it sharp. Jeremy Gordon calls it tender. Yet nobody is asking the fundamental scientific question here—how on earth does a senior citizen secretly hijack a granddaughter's JDate algorithm without tripping any basic biometric authentication protocols? 

Wait. Let me rephrase that. Actually, do not let me rephrase anything, because the total lack of algorithmic plausibility is precisely what makes contemporary fiction so wildly unhinged from reality. 

### The Entropy of Municipal Politics and Grandmothers

The synopsis tries to sell us a high-stakes collision between Obama-era idealism, a morally bankrupt mayoral shift, and an older woman obsessed with Jewish continuity. Sophie wants to write soaring rhetoric for City Hall. Irene wants grandchildren wrapped in legitimate matrimonial contracts. And Zaid appears like a human physics anomaly, colliding with Sophie on the exact trajectory of a protest against her own boss. It is a neat little narrative centrifuge. But let us be entirely serious for a second. Municipal politics does not function like an Aaron Sorkin fever dream. You do not just stare dramatically at a handsome protestor and question your entire career trajectory while a barcode scanner beeps in the background. Real politics smells like stale bodega coffee and sulfur, and nobody ever pauses to deliver a witty zinger about the Middle East while dodging tear gas. 

Except Abrams is writing a comedy of manners, not a peer-reviewed paper on civic corruption. And maybe that is fine. Maybe I am just projecting my sheer, unadulterated rage about the third law of thermodynamics onto a harmless romantic comedy. 

### The Ghost in the Dating Machine

The real engine of this book—the part the blurbs are drooling over—is the catfishing grandma. Irene logging onto JDate and acting as a rogue matchmaking proxy for her overworked granddaughter is a hilarious premise. It is also an absolute invasion of privacy that would result in an immediate restraining order in any universe governed by laws of physics or common decency. But families are messy, and the synopsis promises that a bar mitzvah meltdown will air all the dirty laundry. Classic trope execution. Think Nora Ephron meets a slightly more caffeinated version of *Fleabag*, minus the structural integrity. 

And yet, I cannot shake the dread that the prose will rely too heavily on quirky generational clashes rather than actual character gravity. Actually, scratch that—Abrams probably writes dialogue better than half the people currently tenure-track at this wretched institution. The blurbs insist the pages turn themselves. I just hope the science checks out, or at least that the jokes land hard enough to distract me from my looming student loan default. 

My local bodega has completely run out of my preferred brand of cheap cigarettes, and honestly, that is the most devastating plot twist of my entire week.
