Tony Messenger wants us to stare directly into the smoldering ruins of Ferguson for ten more years. Because apparently, watching a crumbling civic infrastructure fail in real time is the ultimate weekend entertainment when the yacht needs dry-docking and the vintage Scotch tastes like battery acid. *The Ferguson Decade* arrives with the heavy, unearned self-importance of a Davos panel on urban renewal. The synopsis promises a grand post-mortem on structural racism, bureaucratic inertia, and the glorious exhaustion of hope. 

Give me dragons. Give me throat-ripping grimdark brutality where characters at least have the decency to die for a fictional throne rather than drown in municipal zoning laws. 

### The Architecture of Despair

St. Louis becomes a character here, supposedly. A city caught between the ghosts of 2014 and the sheer, exhausting fatigue of a populace told that everything has changed while precisely nothing has moved an inch. Messenger spent a decade talking to everyone from cops to school board members. Riveting stuff. Nothing screams high-altitude literary triumph quite like reading about the agonizingly slow death of a municipal ordinance. It reads like the transcripts of a bankruptcy hearing for a failing private island resort. 

Except, perhaps, it is vital. Or maybe it is just the literary equivalent of the Costa Concordia captain steering too close to the rocks just to see if the band still plays. 

Actually, strike that entirely. The municipal angle is the only thing keeping this from being another forgettable sermon packaged as journalism. I take back the boredom. Bureaucracy is the true horror genre—far more terrifying than any severed limb in Abercrombie's *The Blade Itself*. 

### The Perpetual Motion of Stagnation

The synopsis leans heavily on the exhaustion. Activists, lawmakers, average citizens—all grinding their teeth against the great American machine. Then the political pendulum swings, a cultural backlash hits, and we are back to square one. The residents offer soundbites like, “Everything has changed, and nothing has changed.” Groundbreaking sociology. We needed a Pulitzer winner to tell us that gravity still pulls downward after a decade of talking points. 

One can almost smell the stale coffee in the newsroom. One can almost feel the editors trimming the nuance to fit a public with the attention span of a panicked hedge fund manager checking his portfolio during a flash crash. 

Messenger is relying entirely on the gravity of his subject matter to do the heavy lifting. The synopsis reads like a syllabus for a class you desperately tried to drop during freshman orientation. Yet we will buy it. We will place it on the mahogany coffee table next to the art books nobody opens, pretending we care deeply about municipal reform between bites of caviar. 

I need to go check the guest wing. A juice box exploded on the ceiling.
