The wind carries the scent of wet cardboard and something older—rust, maybe, or the faintest trace of something that once was a cigarette, now just a ghost of ash. It’s the kind of weather that makes you wonder if the city itself is exhaling, tired of holding its breath. Down here, between the dumpsters and the flickering streetlamp, the air feels like a bad translation—too many words, none of them quite right.

The synopsis for *Dead Matters*—no, wait, that’s not the right one. The one we’re talking about is the kind of book that arrives like a bad joke you didn’t ask for but can’t look away from. It’s the kind of reporting that makes you think, *Oh god, they’re doing it again*—the kind of book that promises to dissect a presidency like a cadaver, only to leave you with the sickening realization that the real horror isn’t the body on the slab, but the fact that the surgeons were in on the cover-up. Phil Rucker and Carol Leonnig, those Pulitzer-winning vultures, have done it again—this time with a scalpel instead of a quill. The problem? The synopsis reads like a press release for a true-crime documentary, not a book. Where’s the *nuance*? Where’s the *art*? It’s all here in broad strokes: the COVID denial, the military fantasies, the Capitol riot—check, check, check. But where’s the *why*? The *how*? The kind of reporting that makes you feel like you’re reading *All the President’s Men* meets *The Social Network*, but with the emotional range of a DMV form.

And yet—here’s the pivot—there’s something undeniably compelling about the way it’s framed. The synopsis doesn’t just list events; it *performs* them. It’s the literary equivalent of a car crash you can’t look away from. The way it describes Trump’s "longing to deploy the military" isn’t just a fact—it’s a *gesture*. A performance. The kind of thing that would make Norman Mailer nod approvingly, or at least mutter something about the "mythic dimensions" of American politics. But where’s the *style*? Where’s the prose that makes you feel like you’re not just reading about history, but *experiencing* it? The synopsis reads like a Wikipedia edit history—efficient, but soulless. It’s the difference between a forensic report and a *true crime novel*. And let’s be honest, we’ve had enough forensic reports. What we need is the kind of writing that makes you feel like you’re sitting in the Oval Office with a whiskey in hand, watching the whole thing unravel—like *The Power Broker* meets *The Big Short*, but with the emotional punch of a Mike Leigh screenplay.

The real question is: will this book have the *weight* of *The Wager* or the *urgency* of *The Butcher’s Crossing*? Or will it be the kind of book that gets buried under the weight of its own footnotes, like a library book checked out by a grad student who never returns it? The synopsis doesn’t tell us. It doesn’t even *try*. It’s the literary equivalent of a Tinder profile that just says "I’m a doctor" instead of "I’m a doctor who once operated on a man’s leg while he was still wearing his pants." We get the *what*, but not the *how*—and that’s the problem.

And then there’s the *January 6* section. Oh, the *January 6* section. The synopsis drops it like a bombshell, but it’s already been done to death. We’ve had *January 6* from every angle—*The Plot to Destroy Democracy*, *The Riot*, *The Big Lie*. What’s left to say? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just *implies*—and that’s the problem. Implication is fine, but this isn’t *In Cold Blood*. This isn’t *The Stranger*. This is a book that wants to be *The Jinx*, but with the narrative drive of a *Law & Order* episode. It’s the difference between a scalpel and a butter knife.

But here’s the thing: the synopsis is *perfect* for its audience. It’s the kind of book that will sell like crazy to people who want to feel like they’re in the know, like they’re part of the inner circle. It’s the kind of book that will make you feel like you’re holding the keys to the kingdom, even if all you’re really getting is a tour of the basement. And in a world where people would rather watch a 10-hour documentary than read a 500-page book, that’s not nothing.

Yet—here’s the contradiction—there’s a part of me that *wants* this book to be better. I want it to be the kind of book that makes you sit up at 3 AM, the kind that haunts you like a bad dream. I want it to be the *All the King’s Men* of the Trump era, the *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* of American democracy. But the synopsis doesn’t give me that. It gives me a *checklist*. And checklists don’t make for great literature.

The wind picks up again, and with it comes the sound of a plastic bag tearing. Somewhere, a dog is barking. The city is alive, but it’s not *this* book’s problem. Its problem is that it’s already been written—just not yet published. And when it is, it’ll either be the kind of book that gets taught in schools, or the kind that gets forgotten in the back of a used bookstore. Either way, it’s a gamble. And gambles are what keep us up at night.

My tailor’s waiting to fit me for the awards ceremony. The one where they hand out the prizes for books that actually *matter*. Not the ones that just *exist*.
