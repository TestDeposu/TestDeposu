Quiet down. The collective sigh of the publishing industry needs to stop drowning out my espresso machine. We are looking at Tala Albanna’s *I Pray You Will Be Safe*, an epistolary tightrope walk scheduled for late next year. A twenty-year-old in Gaza City and a twenty-four-year-old in Sderot—orchestrated by a journalist in early 2024—writing letters across a chasm that most people only dare to scream over. And yet, I find myself violently protective of these two real-life women. Why must every human tragedy be packaged into an early-adopter hardcover for the Frankfurt Book Fair?

### The Geography of Epistolary Tourism

There is a distinct, nauseating flavor to how the Western literary market consumes real-time survival. It gives off strong RMS Titanic second-class seating energy. We buy the book to feel better about our own geographic insulation. The synopsis leans hard into the "improbable friendship" trope, as if humanity is a charmingly stubborn rom-com obstacle rather than a pile of concrete and ash. 

Or perhaps I am entirely wrong. Perhaps the letters refuse redemption. 

Actually, strike that. I am completely misreading the publisher's intent. The synopsis guarantees no neat conclusions, only the "dawning realization" of alternate timelines. 

Tala has never spoken to an Israeli. Michelle has never spoken to someone from Gaza. They write of faith, families, and stolen humanity. But wait. Let us be entirely serious for a moment. There is not enough caffeine in the world for this. We are turning a live-streamed apocalypse into a two-person pen-pal special. David Treuer blurbed it as a "double diary that injects humanity where we all need it most." Which is precisely my issue. Why does humanity need to be *injected* by a thirty-dollar trade paperback? 

### The Weight of Unwritten Pages

We do not know the page count yet. A dangerous void. Will this be a slender pamphlet of jagged insights or a bloated compendium designed to sit untouched on oak coffee tables next to architectural digests of Brutalist buildings? The marketing explicitly invokes *Being Jewish After the Destruction of Gaza* and *One Day, Everyone Will Have Always Been Against This*. We are building a brand-new genre here. Call it "Pre-Emptive Historical Grief." 

Let us hope Albanna and Amzalak retain their teeth. Epistolary writing between opposing forces risks sliding into a sickeningly polite performance of mutual understanding for an audience that wants to weep and move on to the dessert course. I demand anger. I demand friction. If these letters do not burn the page, the entire enterprise is just a comfort blanket for liberal hand-wringers. 

The business class lounge was far too crowded today, ruining my concentration.
