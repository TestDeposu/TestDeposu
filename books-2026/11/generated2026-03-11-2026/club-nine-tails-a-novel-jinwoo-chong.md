**Author:** Jinwoo Chong  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

November drafts smell distinctly of expensive pipe tobacco and cold French press coffee lingering on a mahogany desk. Jinwoo Chong returns with *Club Nine Tails*, a title that sounds less like a novel and more like the leaked financial statements from the Theranos liquidation. We are expected to care about a twenty-one-year-old named Connor—because apparently, the literary world hasn't exhausted the excruciating trials of the terribly rich. Let’s unpack this absolute trainwreck. 

The premise relies entirely on an absurd inheritance mechanism known cryptically as "the egg." An egg. One might as well hinge a modern tragedy on a missing Fabergé duck. Connor’s pedigree reads like a failed pitch meeting for a basic prestige drama. Tony-winning mother. Billboard-plastered attorney father who conveniently flees after committing white-collar theft on an industrial scale. And what does our spoiled protagonist do when the penthouse carpets are seized? He crawls back to an estranged uncle in Hell’s Kitchen who runs a gay bar featuring questionable interior design choices. Subtlety is dead. Long live the metaphor.

Chong attempts a picaresque revival by shoving these two mismatched relatives into a beat-up Nissan Cube for a cross-country sprint to Los Angeles. Dollywood gets a cameo because why not add a splash of technicolor irony to a family implosion? This isn't literature. It’s an extended episode of *Succession* directed by someone who only understands human misery through TikTok essays. The synopsis leans heavily on emotional shorthand—bone-deep connections, suffocating futures, motel parking lots. We are promised a clash between a past-haunted uncle and a future-dimmed nephew. Yet, the narrative trajectory feels as predictable as a flight delay at JFK during a nor'easter. 

One suspects the manuscript was assembled using a blender, a stack of back-issues of *Out* magazine, and an algorithm tuned to the exact frequencies of millennial despair. The thematic exploration of familial duty versus self-preservation demands sharp, biting prose. Instead, we get a roadmap of clichés. Financial ruin meets neon signifiers. Let us be entirely serious for a fleeting moment. A cross-country road trip in a Nissan Cube with a crotchety relative is not a vehicle for profound emotional catharsis. It is a purgatory reserved for junior account executives attending a mandatory team-building retreat in Bakersfield. 

Will the money save the mother at Rikers? Does anyone genuinely care? The synopsis treats structural economic corruption as mere set dressing for a quirky bonding exercise. Give me Edith Wharton or give me silence. We do not need another contemporary novel masquerading as gritty realism while behaving like a glossy streaming pilot looking for a production budget. 

Now, if you will excuse me, I must suffer through selecting the appalling floral arrangements for my upcoming book launch.
