**Author:** Talia Hibbert  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Last Thorn* reads like a fever dream of *The Hating Game* if it had been written by a committee of overcaffeinated TikTokers who’d never read a single page of *Pride and Prejudice* but were convinced they’d invented romance. There’s a certain *soup kitchen chic* to the premise—Chloe Brown, a chronically ill, plus-size tech nerd with a *very* specific list of rebellious directives, recruits her brooding, tattooed neighbor, Red Morgan, to be her guide to sin. And yet—where’s the *actual* tension? Where’s the *substance* beneath the glittering veneer of “drunken nights out” and “meaningless sex”?

The problem isn’t that the premise is unoriginal—it’s that it’s *too* original in the worst way. The synopsis leans into the *aesthetic* of rebellion without ever grappling with the *psychology* of it. Chloe’s directives read like a *Black Mirror* episode where the protagonist’s only goal is to check off boxes on a Pinterest board of hedonism. But where’s the *cost*? Where’s the *consequence*? The synopsis doesn’t even hint at the fact that Chloe’s “Get a Life” manifesto might be a desperate attempt to outrun something—her illness, her family’s legacy, her own self-loathing. It’s all *surface*, like a *Dior* ad for “I’m a bad girl” but with the emotional depth of a *McDonald’s Happy Meal*.

And Red Morgan—oh, Red Morgan. The synopsis paints him as a *mysterious artist* with *sex appeal*, which is code for “he’s a brooding, possibly traumatized man who paints at night and might have a *past*.” But where’s the *nuance*? Where’s the *conflict*? The synopsis doesn’t even *try* to make him more than a walking, talking *Instagram filter*. He’s the kind of character who’d show up in a *Netflix original* about “toxic masculinity” but only because the writers needed a *plot device* to make Chloe “feel things.”

The real crime here is the *vagueness*. The synopsis doesn’t *show* us anything—it just *tells* us that Chloe is “tired of being boring” and Red is “sexy.” It’s the literary equivalent of a *Tinder profile* where the only bio is “I like to have fun.” Where’s the *subtext*? Where’s the *irony*? The synopsis doesn’t even *hint* that Chloe’s “rebellion” might be performative, that her list is less about *living* and more about *performing* for an audience—whether that’s her family, her friends, or herself.

And let’s not forget the *thematic* emptiness. The synopsis mentions “do something bad,” but what does that even *mean*? Is it a metaphor for *self-destruction*? A *metaphor for healing*? The synopsis doesn’t *care*. It’s all *aesthetic*, like a *Tinder date* where the only conversation is “So, what’s your *vibe*?”

The real tragedy is that *The Last Thorn* could’ve been something *brilliant*—a *modern retelling of* *The Picture of Dorian Gray* if it had the guts to *commit* to its themes. But instead, it’s just another *romance novel* where the characters are *characters* and the stakes are *stakes* and the *entire thing* feels like it was written by someone who’s never actually *lived*.

The washing machine just flooded the laundry room.
