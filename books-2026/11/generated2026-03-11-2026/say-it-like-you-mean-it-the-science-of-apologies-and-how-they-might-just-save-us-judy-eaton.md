The fluorescent hum above these vinyl chairs is giving me a migraine, and Mrs. Higgins is currently explaining—for the fourth consecutive term—that little Alistair's refusal to share his crayons stems from a lack of emotional bandwidth. Utter garbage. The boy is simply a miniature sociopath with a shoddy pedigree, entirely lacking the self-preservation instinct required to maintain a facade of civilized grace. 

Which brings us precisely to Judy Eaton’s upcoming manual, *Say It Like You Mean It*. 

### The Decline of Aristocratic Contrition

We are expected to salivate over a supposed scientific breakthrough regarding the humble apology. A fascinating dive, the blurbs chirp. Warren Berger insists we won't be sorry. Oh, I am already deeply, profoundly sorry I share a breathing apparatus with the gullible plebs who think a televised grovel from a disgraced CEO will fix the corporate rot. Eaton purports to trace how our instinct to utter those three wretched syllables might save our species from extinction. 

Please. The modern world is completely cooked. 

And no amount of original research or quaint playground anecdotes will salvage a populace raised on social media non-apologies—those pathetic notes app screenshots featuring white text against a gray background. Eaton apparently tackles the heavy lifting: What makes a good apology? Why do some cultures grovel more than others? Groundbreaking questions, really. Did it take a PhD to figure out that nobody means it when they say "mistakes were made"? 

### The Cult of Insincerity

The synopsis leans heavily on cultural decay, lamenting the loss of earnest reconciliation. As if humanity ever possessed it. We merely traded public stocks and dueling pistols for passive-aggressive Slack messages and empty PR statements. Eaton wants us to believe that deploying genuine remorse can fuel a brighter, more human future. It is aggressively optimistic, aggressively earnest, and frankly, a bit cringe. One shudders at the sheer midwit appeal of diagnosing civilizational collapse through the lens of interpersonal conflict resolution. 

People do not want reconciliation; they want victory. Yet here we are, pretending a properly structured mea culpa is the secret to stopping the impending nuclear dumpster fire. 

If you enjoyed the insipid, pop-sociology comfort food of *Atomic Habits* or Malcolm Gladwell’s most aggressively superficial thought-experiments, this absolute mess will suit your pedestrian little heart. It is the literary equivalent of a participation trophy handed out by a terrified human resources director. Eaton is preaching to the choir of people who actually read self-help on airplanes. Meanwhile, the rest of us are just trying to survive this wretched institution without having to listen to another fifteen-minute soliloquy on Alistair's sensory processing issues. 

I need to finalize the hostile acquisition of that failing shipping conglomerate before the London exchange closes, or my blood pressure will actually rupture something vital.
