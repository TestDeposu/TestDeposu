**Page Count:** Unknown

Look, another November rain squall is rattling my windowpane, and I am sitting here staring at a blank document just trying to pad out a mandatory word count because capitalism demands sacrifice and my landlord demands rent. Lisa Unger is back at it with *She Was Never Here*, a literary contraption engineered in some corporate publishing laboratory to keep commuters awake on the subway. 

### The Real Estate Portfolio of Trauma

We begin with Ivy Chase, an aspiring writer who apparently forgot that living in a major metropolitan area requires actual capital. She loses her gig as a nanny, hits absolute zero, and immediately takes a job from a handsome widower named Evan White. Naturally. Because nothing says secure employment like boarding a private jet with a man whose previous employee vanished into thin air. 

Unger relies heavily on the age-old Gothic trope of the architectural gaslight. The isolated estate called Fox Glenn is practically overflowing with atmospheric cliché—sullen children, a dead wife who meticulously restored the place, and a menacing imaginary friend named Lavender because apparently, every spooky manuscript requires a floral motif to really sell the suburban dread. 

### Desire, Deception, and Dead Nannies

What elevates this synopsis from a standard airport-paperback vertical slice to high art? Absolutely nothing, yet we eat it up anyway. We have a scorned previous employer seeking petty vengeance while our protagonist tries to solve the mystery of the missing predecessor. It is pure, unfiltered midwit-lit. It is the literary equivalent of a jump scare in a PG-13 horror movie—derivative, loud, and entirely functional for the masses. 

### The Algorithmic Formula

Let us examine the scaffolding here. We have the requisite socio-economic precarity married to a domestic thriller paradigm that feels like Gillian Flynn got put through a corporate blender alongside every single Shari Lapena novel ever published. It is peak psychological thriller pastiche. The characters are not humans; they are walking plot devices designed to trigger maximum suspense before the inevitable third-act twist that everyone with half a functioning brain cell will spot by chapter three. Yet, we will all preorder it on Kindle anyway because our collective attention span has been fried by endless doomscrolling. 

I have written enough paragraphs to appease the content gods, and frankly, breathing this stagnant indoor air is starting to rot my synapses. I am going to abandon this keyboard, lace up my boots, and wander the concrete grid of the city streets at midnight seeking inspiration or at least a decent bowl of ramen.
