Another day, another digital prompt demanding I squeeze insightful blood from the promotional stone of a romance novel about a girl named Karla who is "perfectly fine." Sure she is. And I am entirely sane while listening to the milk frother shriek its dying mechanical death for the fourth hour straight. 

### The Architecture of Manufactured Whimsy

Lydia San Andres is back at it with *Kissing Daisy*, a book that apparently hinges on a YA book series, an internet forum, and a Miami Beach convention hotel featuring art deco architecture. But let us be entirely serious for a moment. This is yet another frictionless contrivance designed to extract currency from people who still believe in joy. Karla has an office manager job, a demanding mother, and roommates who apparently exist in a silent vacuum. A classic structural paradigm. Or, to put it in terms my sleep-deprived brain actually understands: a total vibe check of late-capitalist alienation disguised as a meet-cute. 

And look, I have to hit a specific word count for this garbage because the algorithm demands sustenance, so let us pretend there is profound literary analysis happening here. 

### The Epistemological Terror of the Internet Friend

What fascinates me—in that morbid way I usually reserve for true crime documentaries about people who live in the woods—is the reliance on the "internet friend" trope. San Andres leans hard into the digital intimacy of Fling, a social media app for romance readers. Because nothing says gothic dread quite like meeting a stranger from an online roleplaying forum in a quirky hotel. Will there be a twist where Daisy is actually an eldritch horror wearing pastel linen? Absolutely not. It is going to be aggressively sweet. It is going to give you diabetes of the soul. It reads like Emily Henry took a masterclass in corporate branding. 

Yet, I suspect the existential horror here isn't the monsters under the bed. It's the crushing realization that your life is remarkably mid until someone named Daisy shows up to validate your parasocial proclivities. Peak parasocial brainrot. 

Karla apparently challenges the story she’s been telling herself. Groundbreaking. A real paradigm shift for modern fiction. We are meant to swoon over a long-distance relationship forged in the fiery depths of a YA convention weekend. 

Maybe I am just bitter because my espresso grinder is jammed with oat milk sludge and the teenager at register three is currently crying into a box of scones. 

Anyway. If you enjoy sunshine, queer joy, and people communicating their feelings instead of repressing them until they manifest as stomach ulcers, you will probably pre-order this. Me? I am going back to staring at the drain. 

Wait. I just remembered I have to hand-deliver an annotated manuscript to my editor—a terrifying woman whose cold stare could freeze boiling water—by noon, or she will feed my favorite coffee mug to the paper shredder.
