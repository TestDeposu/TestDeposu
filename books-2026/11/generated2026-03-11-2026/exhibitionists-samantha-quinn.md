**Page Count:** Unknown

Look around at the folding chairs, the broken microphone stands, the third zoning board violation notice pinned to the community center door with masking tape because someone stole the bulletin board tacks again, and tell me humanity isn't just a slow-motion municipal disaster. We are out here passing out leaflets about pedestrian crossing lights while the sky slowly takes on the bruised, metallic color of an unwashed frying pan and everyone just keeps staring at their phones waiting for permission to care. That is the exact suffocating flavor of elite social machinery promised by *Exhibitionists*, a debut that steps squarely into the suffocating echo chambers of the London art world—though God knows Samantha Quinn's synopsis leans so heavily on tired buzzwords about "shifting alliances" and "power" that I am already developing a nervous twitch in my left eyelid from sheer predictability. 

### The Architecture of Petty Tyranny

We get it. Rich people are terrible. Old men in cardigans holding the keys to prestigious galleries like the Templeton Collection are always exploiting young women whose ambition outweighs their survival instincts. But spare me the melodramatic hand-wringing over whether Caroline will choose passion or loyalty when the real question is why any of us are expected to care about the fragile career anxieties of interns selling their souls for a spot near the canapé tray. It smells like every campus power-dynamic thriller published since the Pleistocene era, dressed up in a sleek black jacket and pretending to have something fresh to say about commodification. If you are going to write a ruthless critique of high-society transactionalism—and let us be honest here, Quinn is clearly gunning for the exact same cynical sandbox inhabited by Ottessa Moshfegh or Sally Rooney on a bad day—you need more than a generic triangular rivalry between an intern, an older chief curator, and a seductive challenger. You need teeth. You need prose that doesn't read like a press release drafted by an exhausted publicist who spent three days locked in a windowless boardroom. 

Predictable.

### The Commodity of Despair

And yet, watching people scramble up the greasy pole of cultural capital while the entire ecosystem collapses beneath their feet has a miserable, hypnotic sort of poetry to it. That is the singular trap of stories like this. They mirror the exact panic of standing in a drafty gymnasium while three dozen people yell over each other about funding grants and refuse to look at the cracks spreading across the ceiling plaster. Caroline and her little chess-game mentors at the Templeton are just rearranging deck chairs on a sinking luxury liner, obsessed with who gets to curate the exhibit while the sea level rises over the baseboards. Quinn is trying to tap into that universal dread of being disposable, but the synopsis plays every single card face-up on the table before the book even hits the shelves next year. There are secrets. There are rivalries. There is a terrible price to pay for success. Groundbreaking stuff. 

Sigh. I really need to get that jump rope untangled from the ceiling fan before the youth council meeting starts at six.
