**Author:** Lauren Ho  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Sharpen Your Knives* is a glittering, jagged thing—like a knife handle wrapped in velvet, promising both spectacle and the slow, deliberate sting of betrayal. It’s the kind of premise that makes you wonder if Lauren Ho is channeling the *queen* of domestic noir, Gillian Flynn, or if she’s just playing with the same old bones that *Big Little Lies* polished to a sheen. But here’s the thing: the real tension isn’t in the murder—it’s in the *gathering*. Five women, all sharpening their knives, but only one willing to use them. That’s not just a setup for a whodunit; it’s a setup for a *who-dare* scenario, where the stakes aren’t just legal but *social*, where the real crime is the idea that any of them might actually *want* to be the one left standing.

The synopsis leans hard into the *movie star as villain* trope—Robert West is less a man and more a *character*, a walking tabloid headline, the kind of figure who exists in the public imagination as a punchline or a cautionary tale. But the genius—or the risk—here is that the wives aren’t just victims. They’re *players*. The moment they’re summoned to his dying bed, they’re not just there to mourn or inherit; they’re there to *negotiate*. And that negotiation isn’t just about money—it’s about *narrative*. Who gets to control the story? Who gets to be the villain? Who gets to walk away with the last word? This isn’t just a murder mystery; it’s a *power fantasy* dressed up in the trappings of a true crime podcast.

The ghostwriter’s involvement is the wild card—because what does a ghostwriter even *do* in a scenario like this? Are they the neutral party? The confidant? The one who knows too much? Or are they just another pawn, someone who can be manipulated into revealing the truth—or burying it? The synopsis doesn’t say, and that’s the point. The real drama isn’t in the whodunit; it’s in the *who-doesn’t-want-to-be-the-killer*. Because in a room full of women who’ve all been burned by the same man, the question isn’t *who* will kill him—it’s *who* will let themselves be the one who does it.

Thematically, this feels like a cross between *The Stepford Wives* and *The Devil Wears Prada*, but with the psychological depth of *Gone Girl*—because the real horror isn’t the murder; it’s the idea that these women might *enjoy* the game. The synopsis doesn’t give us the psychology, but it *hints* at it. The wives aren’t just victims; they’re survivors. And survivors don’t just sharpen knives—they *learn* how to use them.

The only real problem with this premise is that it’s *too* neat. Five wives, one murder, a closed-room mystery—it’s the kind of structure that could easily devolve into a *Knives Out* clone if Ho doesn’t push harder. But if she’s smart, she’ll use the synopsis as a skeleton and fill it with *substance*. Because the real question isn’t *who* did it—it’s *why*. And why is where the art lies.

I’m already imagining the ending: the wives, all of them, standing around the body, knives still in hand, none of them willing to be the one to clean up the mess. Because in the end, the real crime isn’t murder—it’s *collusion*. And that’s a story worth telling.

Now I’m going to brew a cup of lapsang souchong and think about how little I trust anyone in a room full of ex-wives.
