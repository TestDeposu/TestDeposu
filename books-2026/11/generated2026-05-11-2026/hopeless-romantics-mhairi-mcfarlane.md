The fluorescent hum of this primary school hallway is an affront to anyone accustomed to the quiet dignity of a library at dusk. I am currently staring at a finger-painting that supposedly represents a family tree, yet the lack of genealogical rigor in the student’s work is frankly exhausting. It is much like the premise of Mhairi McFarlane’s upcoming *Hopeless Romantics*.

The synopsis presents a narrative built upon the fragile scaffolding of "what-ifs." A train journey, a bottle of cheap champagne, and the persistent, unrefined ache of a missed connection. It sounds exhausting. Why must every contemporary romance insist on the "right person, wrong time" trope as if it were a profound revelation rather than a structural failure of character? If these two individuals—Tess and the rather predictably named Ryan—possessed any real backbone, they would have sorted their affections within a single quarter, not a decade.

### The Pedigree of Indecision

The writing world is currently obsessed with these multi-timeline endeavors, a trend I find largely derivative. It reeks of Sally Rooney’s influence, though without the biting structural precision that makes such emotional wandering palatable. McFarlane is attempting to craft an epic out of the mundane, yet the "shocking twist" mentioned in the synopsis feels like a desperate attempt to inject tension into a story that should have ended before the train reached its final stop. 

Actually, perhaps I am being too harsh. The persistence of memory is, after all, a noble subject for prose. Maybe the ten-year span isn't a sign of weakness, but a commitment to the sort of endurance we rarely see in the ephemeral, TikTok-driven slush pile currently masquerading as literature. 

No, I take that back. It is a weakness. Watching two people circle one another for a decade is not romantic; it is an administrative error. It suggests a lack of agency. One wonders if Tess even has a life outside of pining for a stranger she met on a locomotive. The inclusion of a "cheerful stranger" and a "reserved wingman" creates a dynamic so tired it borders on the archetypal, which, in the hands of a lesser writer, would be an unmitigated disaster. 

### The Illusion of Depth

The prose, if the synopsis is any indication, leans heavily on the "tender" and "heartfelt"—codewords, in my estimation, for a lack of intellectual friction. It is a comfort read for those who find actual ambition too taxing. Still, there is a certain reliable quality to McFarlane’s work that prevents one from discarding it entirely. It is the literary equivalent of a well-pressed linen napkin: functional, expected, and ultimately replaceable. I suspect the readership will swoon, blinded by the nostalgia of what might have been. 

I really must depart. The headmaster’s office is far too drafty, and I have to review the architectural plans for my new villa in Tuscany.
