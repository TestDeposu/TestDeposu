**Author:** Wendy Heard  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Memory is a thief, isn’t it? It leaves you shivering in an empty room, grasping at shadows that aren’t even yours. In *How the Story Always Ends*, Wendy Heard seems to be weaponizing this exact kind of existential haunting. The premise—reincarnation as a trauma-response—is frankly exhausting, yet undeniably alluring. Most writers handle the soul’s migration with the grace of a sledgehammer, coating it in saccharine spirituality. Heard appears to be aiming for something colder, a jagged thriller that treats the past not as a lesson, but as a murder weapon.

### The Weight of Someone Else’s Ghost

Paloma and Dante are burdened by a dead man’s final moments. It is an interesting conceit—to suggest that our most visceral memories might be echoes of lives we never technically lived. Are we merely vessels for old grudges? The synopsis leans heavily into the idea that love is just a recurring cycle of tragic misidentification. I find the prospect of colonial-era backstories irritatingly cliché, likely serving as a dusty stage dressing for a plot that should stay firmly grounded in the frantic, lonely pulse of Los Angeles. 

Actually, I might be wrong about that. A change of scenery to the Caribbean could provide the necessary distance to make the reincarnation angle feel less like a gimmick and more like a fever dream. Forget the historical setting; focus on the murder.

The prose in the synopsis is sparse, which is a relief. If Heard avoids the overwrought sentimentality that plagues books like *The Midnight Library* or the more sluggish works of V.E. Schwab, she might actually have something sharp on her hands. We live in an era where everyone is obsessed with the idea of being "special" or "chosen" by fate. If this novel subverts that—if it turns out that being a reincarnated victim is not a grand destiny but a repetitive, agonizing curse—then it has a fighting chance at relevance. 

### Cycles of Failure

Love as a predestined failure. It feels accurate to my current situation, staring at a blank wall while the radiator hisses. People are always searching for their "soulmate" across time, as if finding the same person in another decade will suddenly make them stay. It won't. You just get to watch the tragedy unfold from a different perspective. 

The inclusion of a "reincarnation research team" sounds suspiciously like a plot device meant to explain away the heavy lifting of the narrative. Science has no business in a story about ghosts. If there is a "truth" to be uncovered, I hope it is hidden in the silence between the characters, not in some sterile lab result or a dusty archive. Books that explain their own magic usually stop being magical about midway through. I suspect this will be a quick read, something to finish in one sitting before the sun comes up and the cycle of disappointment starts all over again. I am going to brew a cup of lapsang souchong and ponder my existential dread.
