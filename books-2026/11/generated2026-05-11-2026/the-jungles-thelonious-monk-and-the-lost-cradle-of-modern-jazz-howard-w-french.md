History is rarely a matter of what occurred; it is a matter of what the victors decide to bury beneath the floorboards. Howard W. French proposes to exhume San Juan Hill, a neighborhood whose spectral presence haunts the blueprints of Lincoln Center. The premise of *The Jungles* suggests a cartography of loss, tracing the arc from the blood-soaked hills of Cuba in 1898 to the cramped, piano-crowded living quarters of 243 West 63rd Street.

### The Geography of Erasure

We have seen this script before, haven't we? The systematic bulldozing of a culture in the name of progress—it is the same tired maneuver that ruined the character of post-war Paris, only here the casualties are syncopated. French posits that Thelonious Monk was not merely a musician, but a living, breathing territorial dispute. He was the holdout. A man wedged into a space that the city had already declared dead on arrival. The synopsis hints at a tension between the rigid, sterile geometry of urban renewal and the jagged, unpredictable cadence of bebop. 

But is this biography, or is it a funeral march?

There is a distinct danger in tethering a historical account so tightly to a singular, iconic figure. If the book leans too heavily into the hagiography of Monk, it risks obscuring the collective pulse of the neighborhood itself. It’s like watching the Fyre Festival documentary—everyone focuses on the influencers, while the actual infrastructure of the disaster remains secondary to the spectacle. I suspect French intends to subvert this, yet the synopsis feels suspiciously vague regarding the texture of the daily life for those who weren't geniuses. Did they just exist as background noise for Monk’s chords?

### Architectural Negligence

The mention of *West Side Story* is the most potent incision in the entire proposal. It suggests a meta-critique of the industry’s penchant for sanitized, aestheticized poverty. To replace a complex Black and Caribbean reality with a simplified, choreographed gang rivalry is more than a creative choice; it is a profound act of cultural revisionism. One hopes French avoids the trap of romanticizing the decay. Historical accounts of "vanished worlds" often succumb to a cloying sentimentality that makes the reader want to gag, though I trust a writer of this pedigree understands that reality is often more grotesque and magnificent than any manufactured "cradle of jazz" mythos.

I find myself wary of the promise to "restore" what has been stolen. Memory is a fickle thing. If French treats the archive with the same rigor he brings to his geopolitical analysis, we might actually get a work that cuts through the noise. If he settles for a eulogy, however, he will have failed the very people he claims to preserve. It is a thin line between illuminating a legacy and merely performing a post-mortem. I suspect this will be either a seminal text or a grand, literary shrug.

I am absolutely losing my mind trying to find a decent dry cleaner in Manhattan that doesn't treat a silk tie like it’s been through a woodchipper.
