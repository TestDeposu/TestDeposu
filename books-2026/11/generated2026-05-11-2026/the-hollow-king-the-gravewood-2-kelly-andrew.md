**Author:** Kelly Andrew  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Hollow King* arrives like a poorly sealed letter from a lover who’s already forgotten half the words—just enough to make you ache for the rest. Kelly Andrew’s prose in *Your Blood, My Bones* had the kind of visceral, unflinching precision that makes you wonder if she’s been reading your diary while you slept. So here we are, staring at this bargain—Shea’s hearing aids for Lysander’s bloodlust, a trade that’s equal parts transaction and slow-motion betrayal. The framing is *chef’s kiss* for fans of dark romance where the romance is the wound, not the cure. Think *The Cruel Prince* meets *The Raven Boys*, but with the existential dread of a vampire who’s never been kissed and the emotional whiplash of a protagonist who’s already chosen love over survival.

The synopsis leans into the *obsession* angle with the kind of deliberate vagueness that’s either genius or a cop-out. Shea’s betrayal—*oh, the betrayal*—is the kind of narrative pivot that makes you clutch your chest, but the real question is: *Who’s the monster here?* Lysander, the frozen-hearted king of the Gravewood, or Shea, the girl who broke him? The phrasing is *deliciously* ambiguous, which is either a sign of bold thematic ambition or a lazy way to avoid committing to a villain. Either way, it’s the kind of setup that could either elevate or collapse under its own weight. Andrew’s work so far suggests she’s the former, but the synopsis doesn’t give us enough to tell.

Yet—*yet*—there’s something in the way it’s written, the way it *hints* at disability as more than a plot device but less than a full character study. Hearing aids aren’t just batteries; they’re a barrier, a necessity, a vulnerability. The way Shea’s need is framed as a *bargain*—not a demand, not a plea, but a transaction—is either a masterstroke or a missed opportunity. It could have been the kind of nuanced portrayal of disability that makes you sit up straight, like in *The House in the Cerulean Sea*, where the characters’ differences aren’t just acknowledged but *felt*. Or it could be the kind of surface-level inclusion that makes you roll your eyes, like so many YA books that treat disability as a plot convenience.

The romantic tension is *electric*, but the synopsis doesn’t tell us if it’s the kind of love that’s beautiful or the kind that’s *dangerous*—and in Andrew’s world, those are often the same thing. The line about Shea breaking Lysander’s heart is the kind of line that makes you pause, because in dark romance, hearts aren’t just broken; they’re *rebuilt in blood*. The question is whether this will be the kind of story where love is the poison or the antidote. And honestly? I’m not sure I care enough to wait until 2026 to find out.

But then—*but then*—I remember how *Your Blood, My Bones* ended, how it left you breathless and wondering if the characters would ever get a happy ending. The synopsis for *The Hollow King* feels like it’s playing the same game: dangle the carrot of emotional payoff just out of reach. It’s the kind of storytelling that makes you want to scream, *Just tell me already!* But also, it’s the kind of storytelling that makes you *need* to read it.

The real test will be whether Andrew can balance the *obsession* with the *loyalty*, whether she can make the betrayal feel earned and the love feel *real*. Because if this is another book where the romance is the villain, then it’s just another entry in the *Twilight* graveyard. But if it’s something more—something *raw*, something *uncomfortable*—then it might just be the kind of story that sticks with you like a bruise.
