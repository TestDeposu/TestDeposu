The idea that food is a moral battlefield—where every bite is either a victory or a surrender—has been so thoroughly mined by self-help gurus that *Jesus Ate Carbs* arrives like a particularly earnest afterthought in a genre that’s already been exhausted by the sheer volume of its own clichés. Abbie Frye’s SATISFIED framework sounds less like a radical departure and more like a spiritualized version of the same old "intuitive eating" playbook, the kind that’s been recycled since the ‘90s, when *The Body Myth* by Susan Bordo was still fresh enough to feel like a revelation. But where Bordo dissected the cultural machinery of body shame, Frye seems content to offer a gentler, more Jesus-adjacent version of the same critique—one that trades Marxist analysis for a pastoral pat on the head.

The synopsis’s insistence that "God never designed you to micromanage food" is the kind of phrasing that makes you wonder if Frye has ever actually read *The Gospel of Thomas*, where Jesus’s sayings about fasting and feasting are far more ambiguous than this book’s tidy theology suggests. The idea that hunger and fullness are divine signals to be trusted—without the messy historical context of how diet culture weaponized those very signals—feels like a missed opportunity. Where’s the reckoning with the fact that the "intuitive eating" movement itself has been co-opted by wellness capitalism, turning body autonomy into another product to sell? Instead, we get a framework that sounds less like liberation and more like a spiritualized version of the same old "just eat what you want" advice that’s been failing people for decades.

And then there’s the title itself—*Jesus Ate Carbs*—which is either a brilliant stroke of marketing or the kind of reductive branding that would make Michelangelo weep if he’d ever had to explain his *Sistine Chapel* ceiling to a focus group. The implication that Jesus, of all people, was a carb-loving slob is either a profound theological insight or the kind of lazy shorthand that would make the Vatican’s PR team cringe. It’s the literary equivalent of slapping a "Now with 50% more Jesus!" sticker on a diet book, as if the solution to our collective food anxiety is just to add a dash of divine approval to the same old restrictive logic.

The SATISFIED framework—with its acronym and its promise of "kindness, patience, and joy"—feels like it’s borrowing from the same playbook as *The Gifts of Imperfection* by Brené Brown, but without the same level of psychological depth. Brown’s work, for all its flaws, at least acknowledges the role of vulnerability in healing; Frye’s approach seems to suggest that if you just *trust yourself* (and God), everything will magically fall into place. It’s the kind of simplistic, feel-good spirituality that would make the late, great philosopher Slavoj Žižek roll his eyes so hard his glasses would fly off his face.

The real tragedy here is that the book’s core argument—that food isn’t the enemy—isn’t particularly original. It’s the kind of idea that’s been floating around in wellness circles since the dawn of time, like the ghost of a half-remembered seminar from a yoga retreat in 2012. What’s missing is the kind of sharp, unflinching critique that would make this more than just another entry in the "eat less, feel better" industry. Where’s the dissection of how diet culture is a tool of capitalism? Where’s the analysis of how the church itself has often been complicit in body shame? Instead, we get a book that sounds like it was written by someone who’s never actually struggled with an eating disorder, who’s never had to navigate the minefield of food rules, and who’s never had to explain to a well-meaning pastor why "God’s design" doesn’t include a 12-step program for carb addiction.

The most infuriating part? The synopsis doesn’t even attempt to grapple with the fact that for many people, food isn’t just about "intuition" or "trust." It’s about survival. It’s about trauma. It’s about the way diet culture has been weaponized against marginalized bodies for centuries. But Frye’s approach is so gently pastoral that it feels like she’s offering a lollipop to someone who’s just been through a war. It’s not enough.

And yet—despite all this—there’s a part of me that wonders if the book might actually work for the right audience. Maybe for someone who’s never been deeply wounded by diet culture, who’s just looking for a spiritualized version of "eat what you want" without the existential dread. But then again, if that’s the case, why not just read *The Alchemist* and call it a day?
