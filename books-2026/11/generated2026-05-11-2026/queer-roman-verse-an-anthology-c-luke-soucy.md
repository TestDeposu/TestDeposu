Squeezed between a man smelling like damp wool and a screeching metal rail, I am thinking about how the past is just a long game of telephone played by people who were deathly afraid of their own shadows. *Queer Roman Verse: An Anthology* lands on my radar like a brick through a window. Or maybe a love letter sent to the wrong address. C. Luke Soucy is promising to peel back the varnish applied by those suffocating Victorian prudes who couldn’t handle the fact that Ovid or Virgil might have been looking at a man with the exact same hunger they reserved for their prim, lace-collared wives.

### The Erasure of the Libidinous Muse

The synopsis is irritatingly coy, dangling the promise of a "lexicon" we supposedly lost. I have heard this pitch before—the "lost truth" angle is classic, though usually deployed by people selling me some absolute trash about ancient mystery cults. But if Soucy actually manages to make Horace sound like a human being with a pulse rather than a marble bust, I will forgive the marketing fluff. Translation is an act of violent appropriation; we have spent two centuries reading these poets through the lens of moral discipline, which is essentially just code for "let’s pretend the ancients were as repressed as our great-grandfathers." If this collection manages to inject some actual vitality into the Latin—if it captures the sleaze, the longing, the sheer chaos of Roman desire—it might just be the most significant thing to happen to the classics since Emily Wilson gave us that jagged, glorious *Odyssey*.

### A Decaying Imperial Aesthetic

I am tired of the sanitized canon. We have been spoon-fed these pristine, marble-white versions of Rome for ages, ignoring the fact that it was a city built on blood, sex, and total lack of chill. The Victorian revivalists were terrified that admitting the Romans were queer would undermine the entire "civilized" project. They were right. It does. And I am here for the rubble. Soucy is stepping into a space previously occupied by the likes of Anne Carson—who understood that myth is only as good as the blood you can draw from it—and I suspect this book will be less of a dry academic exercise and more of a gut punch to the tweed-jacketed gatekeepers. The autumn chill is seeping through the carriage doors, and the damp air makes me nostalgic for a version of history that wasn't laundered for a Sunday school pamphlet.

This book looks like it might actually possess some legitimate CRINGE-WORTHY BANGER ENERGY, provided the translator doesn't get too bogged down in his own ego. Or maybe it’s just another attempt to retrofit our current obsessions onto a culture that would have looked at us, shrugged, and probably stabbed us for being annoying. We will see. I need to get off at the next stop, and I have a sneaking suspicion that when I finally get home, I will have to spend an hour trying to untangle the garden hose that has tied itself into a Gordian knot because the November frost is already hardening the rubber. It is never simple, is it?
