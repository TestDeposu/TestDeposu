**Author:** Clare Osongco  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The coffee here tastes like it’s been brewed with regret and stale napkins, the kind of sludge that makes you wonder if the barista’s soul is as jaded as the beans. *Suddenly I Miss Everyone* arrives like a text from someone you haven’t spoken to in years—half a confession, half a dare. The synopsis is a masterclass in *minimalist emotional blackmail*, the kind of setup that makes you lean in, fingers twitching to turn the page even though you’ve never read a word. It’s *You’ve Reached Sam* meets *Seoulmates*, which is either a brilliant mashup or a desperate attempt to ride the coattails of two books that already feel like they’ve been overanalyzed into dust. But here’s the thing—this isn’t just a romance. It’s a *grief road movie*, a genre that’s been sorely underrepresented in YA, and if Clare Osongco pulls it off, it’ll feel like stumbling upon a lost *Thelma & Louise* for the emotionally stunted.

Tessa’s silence after her father’s death isn’t just a plot device—it’s a *character in itself*. The way the synopsis frames her withdrawal as both a shield and a prison is where the real tension lies. There’s a *Cormac McCarthy* bleakness to her isolation, the kind of quiet that’s louder than any scream. But then Jenna—*the meddling girlboss sister*—shows up, and suddenly we’re in *Legally Blonde* territory, but with the emotional weight of a *Normal People* breakup. The ghost-hunting angle is either a gimmick or a metaphor for Tessa’s own haunted past, and if Osongco leans too hard into the supernatural, it’ll feel like *Goosebumps* for people who’ve never actually been scared. Yet if she treats it as a *literal* manifestation of Tessa’s guilt, it could be something fresh—like *The Lovely Bones* meets *Book Lovers*, but with more road trips and less angelic intervention.

The Justin subplot is where the synopsis starts to crack under its own weight. A years-long crush? A draft email? It’s the kind of setup that makes you think of *To All the Boys I’ve Loved Before*, but with the emotional stakes of *The Fault in Our Stars*. The problem isn’t the trope—it’s the *timing*. If this is supposed to be a slow-burn, then the synopsis is rushing it like a TikTok edit. If it’s a fast-paced reunion, then the grief feels like an afterthought. Either way, it’s a gamble, and gambles in YA are either gold or lead.

But here’s the real question: *Can grief be romanticized?* Because that’s what this book seems to be asking. The synopsis doesn’t say, but it *hints*—like a text left unsent, like a door left ajar. And that’s the beauty of it. It’s not about the answers. It’s about the *waiting*, the *almost*, the way a story can make you feel like you’re holding your breath, even when you know the air’s thin.
