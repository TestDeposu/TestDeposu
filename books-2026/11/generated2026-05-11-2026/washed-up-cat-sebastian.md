Autumn in Salzburg always smells faintly of wet limestone, expensive tobacco, and the crushing weight of Habsburg ghosts. I am currently sitting on a velvet chair that has seen far too many political coups, watching intellectual elites nod sagely at papers about post-industrial melancholia. Yet my mind keeps drifting to the promotional packet resting on my lap for Cat Sebastian’s upcoming *Washed Up*. God help us all, another queer contemporary romance engineered in a lab to cure the parasocial loneliness of millennials who refuse to pay for therapy. 

### The Pathology of the Canceled Child Star

The premise leans heavily into the commodification of youth — a thoroughly exhausted trope that Alexis Hall and Casey McQuiston have beaten into a bloody pulp for years. But Sebastian brings a certain domestic coziness to the bourgeoisie misery. We get Gabriel, the fugitive star fleeing some phantom generational curse, hiding out in New Hampshire like a moody Henry David Thoreau with better skincare. And Matthew, the cynical journalist turned glorified gossip hound who should know better than to mix business with teenage trauma. 

The power dynamic is deliciously toxic. Power shifts from the glossy hegemony of television studios to the claustrophobic confines of a crumbling cottage. Decay as a romantic aesthetic. Groundbreaking. 

### Ephemera and Emotional Bankruptcy

Let us be entirely serious for a moment. This book is going to utterly destroy every basic girlie on BookTok, and honestly? Vibe. No notes. The synopsis promises the exact brand of second-chance yearning that makes otherwise rational adults abandon their careers to read fan-fiction on AO3 until 4:00 AM. 

Yet, the synopsis relies on narrative crutches so lazy they deserve a handicap placard. A family curse? What is this, a Gothic melodrama written by a nineteenth-century spinster with tuberculosis? We are talking about modern Hollywood nepotism and studio churn, not ancestral bloodlines blighting the cinematic lineage. Sebastian is too clever for cheap supernatural dressing, which makes the inclusion baffling. Give me structural critique, give me union strikes, give me the cold, unfeeling machinery of late-stage capitalism chewing up child actors — spare me the spooky ancestral ghosts. 

And yet, I will preorder the hardcover. Naturally. Because deep down, beneath the layers of cynical geopolitical analysis and my absolute exhaustion with second-chance reconciliation tropes, I am just as pathetic as the rest of them. We crave the domestic salvation of two broken boys in a dilapidated cabin because the actual world outside these symposium walls is entirely devoid of soft landings. 

The keynote speaker is currently droning on about transnational trade agreements, but my skull is throbbing. Time to leave this suffocating hall of academic dinosaurs. I am going to take my vintage Porsche out for a spin along the winding mountain passes to clear my head and aggressively listen to industrial techno until the existential dread subsides.
