Velvet drapes block out the morning glare, leaving only the amber glow of a reading lamp to illuminate the stack of galley proofs currently cluttering my mahogany desk. M.A. Carrick returns with *The Eye of Leviathan*, a title dripping with the maritime pomposity of the Costa Concordia sinking, yet promising an alternate Spanish Golden Age. One raises an eyebrow. We are promised fae masquerading as mortals, human proxies traded in some bizarre existential barter system called "The Hungry Girl," and a grand crusade against an imperial machine. But does the prose possess the required meter, or is it merely recycled parchment masquerading as high art?

### The Architecture of Deception

The premise relies on a double-sided identity crisis that feels dangerously close to an episode of *The Real Housewives of Beverly Hills* played out in corsets and doublets. Estevan hides his faerie nature from the very inquisitors who hunt his kind. Meanwhile, his human substitute—the aforementioned Hungry Girl—stumbles blindly into the Otherworld before joining Spanish explorers in the titular Sea Beyond. 

And what do they find there? Atrocities. Naturally. Because no modern fantasy writer can resist the urge to graft twenty-first-century geopolitical moralizing onto an alternate sixteenth-century epoch. 

Give us cadence. Give us rhythm. I care little for didactic lectures on corporate or imperial exploitation disguised as magical world-building. Give me biting wit, razor-sharp dialogue, and sentences that pirouette across the page like a Balanchine dancer. Instead, we are handed a marketing hook about a "faerie pact" that separated our star-crossed duo. 

### The Aesthetic Verdict

Call it what you will—an alternate history, a duology starter, a Library Journal darling—yet the synopsis reads like a pitch meeting orchestrated by algorithmic committee. Where is the lyricism? Where are the rolling cadences of a true stylist who understands the intoxicating weight of a well-placed caesura? Leigh Bardugo managed a certain atmospheric charm in *The Familiar*, yes. But Carrick risks sinking beneath the sheer ballast of her own high-concept plotting. 

Expect breathless pacing. Expect heavy-handed metaphors about stolen resources and institutional greed. And do pray the dialogue avoids sounding like modern teenagers whispering in a coffee shop while wearing ruffled collars. If the text lacks the stylistic discipline of a true poet, I shall simply toss it onto the fire alongside the dreadful quarterlies from the London Literary Society. 

My chauffeur is already idling the Daimler in the gravel drive, and I must endure a dreadfully tedious board meeting for the Symphony Guild where several patronizing bores will drone on about fundraising galas until my ears bleed.
