**Author:** Annie Neugebauer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Another glossy little blurb has landed on my espresso-ringed counter like a dead pigeon dropped by a frustrated hawk, and honestly, the literary industrial complex needs to recalibrate its serotonin levels. *The Spare* by Annie Neugebauer—part of this mysterious, self-congratulatory *The Outsiders Sequence*—promises us a "beautifully written nightmare" because apparently, nobody in publishing has read an actual piece of existential horror since Shirley Jackson cashed her last royalty check. Zoje Stage is blurbing it, naturally, comparing it to *Baby Teeth* because why invent a new rhetorical mode when you can just mash two mid-tier thriller titles together like a pair of toddlers playing with building blocks? 

### The Psychopathology of Family Dysfunction

Let us dissect the synopsis with the tweezers it deserves. The protagonist, Kasper, is suffering from the universal human condition of dreading extended family reunions—truly, a staggering conceptual leap for contemporary fiction. We are informed she is the "odd one out" on a remote camping trip where the head-count keeps fluctuating. Spooky! Math problems in the woods! Is it ghosts? Is it an uninvited cousin from Boise? Is it just elite-tier gaslighting by people who wear Patagonia vests unironically? Nobody knows, because the synopsis is basically a literary Rorschach test designed to separate gullible sub-scribers from their hard-earned twenty-eight bucks. 

And oh, the sheer audacity of the subtitle: *It's not a sequel. It's next in the sequence...* 

Give me a break. It is a sequel, darling. Slapping a pretentious little lowercase ellipsis at the end of a marketing hook doesn't elevate your franchise into high art; it just makes you look like a moody teenager updating their Tumblr in 2011. We get it. You watched one too many elevated-horror A24 flicks and decided that ambiguity is a substitute for an actual plot structure. 

### The Woods Are Lovely, Dark, and Deeply Overrated

Forest-based dread is officially the pumpkin spice latte of the publishing world. Every suburban auteur with a laptop and a grudge against their mother feels compelled to drag a group of dysfunctional relatives into the timberlands to discover that—*gasp*—they don't actually like each other. Groundbreaking. Kafka is literally weeping in his grave. But let us look at the lexical architecture here. Kasper feels outnumbered, which is a mildly amusing trope if executed with the psychosexual claustrophobia of Katja operación-style prose, but knowing modern genre fiction, it will probably read like an elongated Reddit r/nosleep thread padded out to three hundred pages so airport bookstores have something to stack next to the celebrity memoirs. 

Yet—and this is where the algorithmic marketing machine actually succeeds in capturing my jaded attention—there is something deliciously petty about the premise of being trapped in the wilderness with people who remember you when you had bangs and a lisp. We are looking at a masterclass in low-stakes urban neurosis projected onto pine trees. It gives me major *The Ritual* meets family trauma TikTok vibes, but make it academic and dripping with low-key contempt. Will it redefine the genre? Absolutely not. Will it sell out at indie pop-up shops while hipsters pretend they understand liminal spaces? For sure. 

My favorite indie cafe has replaced their scarred wooden tables with sterile marble.
