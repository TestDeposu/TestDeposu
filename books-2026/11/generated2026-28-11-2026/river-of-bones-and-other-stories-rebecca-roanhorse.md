The coffee is gone, replaced by a half-empty glass of Chianti that tastes entirely too much like iron and old regrets, and the clock refuses to move past three in the morning. Existence feels like a slow leak in a vintage pneumatic tire. But the literary world insists on spinning, bringing us *River of Bones and Other Stories* by Rebecca Roanhorse, a collection promising to carve open the American spine and see what crawls out. Or maybe it just collects dust on a nightstand while the ceiling groans under the weight of an unseasonably heavy rain. 

### The Currency of Dissent

Look, short story anthologies usually read like literary graveyards—good intentions buried under weak editing and half-baked concepts. Yet Roanhorse commands a different sort of gravity. Her *Trail of Lightning* and *Black Sun* proved she operates on frequencies most contemporary fabulists can't even tune into. The synopsis tells us we are getting the award-winning heavy hitters alongside a return to the Sixth World with Kai's perspective in the title novella. I am thrilled. No, wait. Short fiction is invariably exhausting, a parade of narrative appetizers that leaves you malnourished. Let us be entirely serious for a moment: anthologies are an insult to the art of the long-form sustained delusion. Give me a sprawling, three-hundred-page architectural disaster over ten tight, punchy vignettes any day of the week. 

### Celestial Obsessions and Disparate Universes

The thematic breadcrumbs here point directly toward identity, indigenous futurism, and the specific cruelty of the modern American machine. We have *A Brief Lesson in Native American Astronomy* dissecting the cost of ambition in a near-future Hollywood that chews up tradition for breakfast. We have *Eye and Tooth*, a black horror piece featuring monster-hunting siblings. Because nothing says leisure quite like existential dread wrapped in speculative genre packaging. I haven't known peace since I finished page one. Or perhaps I won't have peace. The blurbs suggest a vanguard voice slicing through tropes like a scalpel through rotten silk. 

One imagines Stephen Graham Jones and Carmen Maria Machado nodding approvingly from some shadowy corner of the literary VIP lounge, sharing a dark vintage while lesser writers churn out stale corporate thrillers. Roanhorse does not write comfort reads. She writes the sort of speculative fiction that makes you check the deadbolts twice and stare blankly out the frosted glass of a high-rise window, wondering if the apocalypse is already here or if it is merely waiting for the coffee maker to finish its cycle. 

The prose will undoubtedly dazzle. The pacing will likely fluctuate—as it always does when an author shifts from the breathless expanse of a Nebula-winning short like *Welcome to Your Authentic Indian Experience™* to the tight constraints of genre anthologies. But we do not read for uniform perfection. We read to feel the floor drop out beneath our tailored loafers. 

The thunder is getting louder, rattling the imported French panes in their frames. I have to go clean out the gutters before the storm hits.
