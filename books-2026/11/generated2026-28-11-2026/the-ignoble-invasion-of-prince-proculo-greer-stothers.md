**Page Count:** Unknown

Four shots of espresso deep and staring down another line of frothy oat milk requests while a commercial grinder shrieks like a banshee—that is the exact mental bandwidth I have left to process Greer Stothers deciding that what modern literature is missing is an intergalactic parasite getting demoted to a wet-paper-bag prince. *The Ignoble Invasion of Prince Proculo* arrives in twenty-twenty-six like some unholy collision between Douglas Adams and whatever fever-dream fanfiction crawled out of the darkest corners of the internet after midnight. 

### Tentacles Versus Tantrums

The premise demands absolute scrutiny. We are looking at a cosmic conqueror—named after a heavy radioactive metal, because subtlety is clearly dead—who gets humbled by some petty deity and stuffed into a weedy human carcass. Yet the marketing desperately wants us to view this as a laugh-out-loud romp. Right. Because nothing screams lighthearted comedy quite like body horror meeting bureaucratic fantasy politics. Or maybe I am just bitter because my morning rush-hour crowd looks less appealing than a half-human, half-spider monstrosity named Tantalus. Actually, scratch that—Tantalus sounds genuinely refreshing compared to the human race right now. Give me a guy with bristling muscles and sharp fangs over someone who complains about oat milk temperature any day.

Stothers is apparently gunning for that specific sweet spot carved out by Caitlin Rozakis's *Dreadful* and John Wiswell's *Someone You Can Build a Nest In*. And honestly? That comparison does some heavy lifting. We are supposed to care about alien imperialism turning into domestic bliss because—plot twist—true love conquers all. Except I completely retract my earlier sneering about the romance angle. If you are going to write a gay monster romantasy featuring an extraterrestrial slug-creature falling head over heels for an arachnid hybrid, you might as well lean entirely into the absurdity. No half-measures. Let the tentacles fly. 

### The Absurdity Index

The thematic core here seems suspiciously cheerful for a book featuring a protagonist whose resume includes destroying civilizations. Total world domination usually requires a bit more edge than getting banished to a fantasy realm to scrap with local fauna. But who am I to judge a celestial parasite's midlife crisis? The genre-bending chaos of throwing an Isekai trope into a sci-fi monster narrative wrapped in a queer romance is either going to implode under its own weight or become the exact sarcastic antidote to twenty-first-century sincerity we desperately need. I predict it will either sweep the indie charts or be remaindered by March. No in-between. 

Now, if you will excuse me, my fifteen-minute break is evaporating and I need to find a needle and heavy-duty thread to patch a massive moth hole in my favorite tweed jacket before the dinner shift starts.
