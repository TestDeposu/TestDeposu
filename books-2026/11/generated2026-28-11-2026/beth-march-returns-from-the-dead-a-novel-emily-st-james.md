The frost on the corner office glass is thick enough this morning to carve initials into, which feels entirely too fitting for a novel dragging Louisa May Alcott kicking and screaming into the upper Midwest. Emily St. James is attempting something perilous with *Beth March Returns from the Dead*. Everyone wants a millennial cult classic until they are forced to read the structural arithmetic required to make dead siblings and transgender transition metaphors walk the same snowy sidewalk in Duluth. 

### The Architecture of Nostalgia Bait

We are looking at a narrative engine powered entirely by the exact brand of millennial ennui that made *Woodworking* a darling of the glossy quarterlies—and by that, I mean it smells faintly of overpriced cedar and deferred dental insurance. Transatlantic signals crossed with nineteenth-century domesticity. It is the literary equivalent of the 1997 Fyre Festival, minus the cheese sandwiches. St. James leans so heavily on the structural scaffolding of the March family that one half expects Laurie to show up with a vape and a trust fund. 

Tom Vega is a character constructed from a mold kept in the back room of every indie bookstore in Brooklyn. A grieving handyman with a deadbeat aesthetic and a repressed queer panic. Groundbreaking. We have not seen this man since every single movie that premiered at Sundance between 2012 and 2018. 

### The Cost of Cleverness

And then there is the central conflict. Disappearing into heterosexuality? Really? We are staging ideological battles about community betrayal over a frozen cup of drip coffee in Minnesota. The stakes rattle around the page like loose change in an empty coat pocket. St. James wants us to buy into the gravity of these choices while keeping the narrative weight as light as a paperback left out in the November drizzle. 

### The Verdict on the Premise

Give me a break. Or rather, give the reading public something that does not rely on a high-concept title to do eighty percent of the heavy lifting. The premise alone requires the narrative agility of the 1980 Olympic hockey team, yet the synopsis promises a slow slog of lingering glances across drafty hardware store aisles. 

I have to sign off now because my communications director is sobbing in the hallway over a rogue tweet about our winter catalog that was supposed to be a draft.
