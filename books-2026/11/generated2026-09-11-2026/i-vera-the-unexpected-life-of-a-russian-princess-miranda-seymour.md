Landlord is banging on the radiator again, threatening to evict me for subletting my closet to a raccoon, so naturally, I am staring down a looming rent deadline while writing about a 19th-century princess who used masculine pronouns. Miranda Seymour wrote *I, Vera: The Unexpected Life of a Russian Princess*, and I am genuinely praying to whatever deity governs historical biographies that this thing doesn't lean into historical fiction fluff. Give me hard medical procedures or give me nothing. 

### Scalpels and Royal Courtyards

The synopsis promises a protagonist who rejected embroidery for billiards and went on to invent modern battlefield medicine. But let us be real for a second—biographies about eccentric aristocrats always risk turning a genuinely radical scientific mind into a quirky period-piece accessory. Wait, scratch that. Seymour wrote *In My Father's House* and various literary biographies, so she actually knows how to handle primary sources without romanticizing every drop of blood. Or maybe she will. Historiography is a mess of filtered archives, especially when dealing with the Russian Empire right before everything caught fire. 

### The Mechanics of Saving Lives Under Fire

Vera Gedroits frogmarching Rasputin out of a hospital ward is the exact kind of historical anecdote that keeps me awake at 3:00 AM wondering how humans ever survived past thirty without proper antibiotics. Yet, what fascinates me here isn't just the royal proximity or the sheer audacity of a woman operating on the frontlines of the Russo-Japanese War. It's the mechanics. How do you revolutionize battlefield surgery when the state actively wants to erase you? Stalin's regime later scrubbed her name from records, which is the ultimate bureaucratic erasure. 

And yet, this is where the synopsis gets slightly cagey. It throws around phrases like "true medical miracles" and "novelistic story" without telling us what methodologies Seymour used to reconstruct the lost memoirs. I am deeply concerned this book will gloss over the actual surgical innovations in favor of palace gossip. Still, if Seymour actually details the transition from makeshift field dressings to organized triage, it might save us from another round of sanitized historical profiles. 

My ramen is cold. The electricity bill is threatening to send me back to the dark ages. But if *I, Vera* delivers on the grit of a groundbreaking surgeon who refused to wear dresses or play by the rules of an empire crashing down around her, I might actually forgive the universe for existing. 

I need to go power-wash the driveway, which is my only solace.
