**Page Count:** Unknown

They tell us to look for the art in the mundane—to find meaning in the forgotten scraps of canvas, the brushstrokes left behind by people who refused to stay in their designated boxes—yet I find myself staring at the summary of *Side Streams* with the emotional resonance of a wet sponge sitting on a granite countertop. Yasmin Adele Majeed wants us to care about Aliya Amjad, a girl caught between a Filipino American past she was never invited into and a tight-knit Pakistani American neighborhood that probably judges her choice in shoes, all while a mysterious art installation sits in the workspace like a three-dimensional manifestation of a grant committee's fever dream. And yes, there are secrets—because apparently, no contemporary literary fiction can exist without characters whispering in hallways like amateur spies in a third-rate John le Carré pastiche—featuring dual timelines, unspoken desires involving neighborhood boys glancing too long at masjid walls, and a covert kiss between a father and a houseguest that feels entirely engineered by an algorithm designed to maximize generational trauma bingo cards. 

Whatever.

### The Architecture of Omission

We are promised a polyphonic exploration of silence—as if silence were an architectural marvel rather than just people being too cowardly to use their words over a period of forty years. 

Boring.

If I have to read one more debut about secrets tearing apart diaspora communities while everybody drinks chai and stares longingly at a mural that nobody has the common sense to appraise or throw away, I might actually throw myself through the nearest wall. 

Hollow.

Aliya and Samir discover their parents have hidden lives, as if anyone over thirty possessing a hidden life is a shocking literary revelation rather than a Tuesday afternoon in the suburbs. Majeed is clearly trying to write the next *Pachinko* or a quieter, more melancholy cousin to Zadie Smith’s *White Teeth*, but the synopsis reads like a mood board compiled by an exhausted MFA graduate who spent too much time scrolling through aesthetic Pinterest boards dedicated to bittersweet nostalgia. 

Whatever.

Maybe the prose will save it. Maybe the phrasing will ache so exquisitely that I won't care about the recycled tropes of unspoken queer yearning and mysterious middle-aged houseguests harboring forbidden passions. But right now, sitting in this quiet room while the radiator clanks its usual death rattle, I feel nothing for the Amjad family or their canvas mysteries. 

Nothing.

I need to patch a hole in the drywall caused by an indoor football incident.
