**Author:** Olivia Waite  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Double Dorothy* arrives like a well-worn letter from a relative you’ve never met—familiar in its structure, but the handwriting is just *off*. It’s the kind of premise that makes you pause mid-chopping vegetables, knife still suspended over the onion, wondering if the author has ever actually *lived* on a ship for five centuries. Because here we are, again, with a detective who’s been dead and reincarnated so many times she’s probably forgotten what it’s like to have a pulse. And yet—there’s something in the way the synopsis frames this latest crisis that makes you wonder if Waite is playing with the same tired sci-fi tropes we’ve all seen before, or if she’s finally found a way to make the "eternal detective" formula feel less like a haunted house and more like a cozy mystery with a side of existential dread.

The real kicker is that doppelgänger angle. It’s not just that Dorothy has a *copy*—it’s that the copy is *wrong*, a glitch in the system, a memory-book misfired like a bad batch of heirloom tomatoes. The implication is that someone *erased* something, and now Dorothy’s final reembodiment is at risk of being permanent. Which, let’s be honest, is the kind of plot device that would make Agatha Christie roll in her grave—if she’d had the patience to wait for the Fairweather’s next port call. But then again, Christie never had to deal with a murder mystery that spans five hundred years of interstellar travel. The synopsis doesn’t say whether the ship’s AI is sentient, or if the crew is a ragtag bunch of misfits, or if the murder from the first voyage was ever solved—just that it *wasn’t*, and that Dorothy was a *witness*, not the detective. That’s a juicy detail, buried under layers of bureaucratic jargon about "memory-books" and "reembodiment laws." It’s the kind of thing that makes you want to demand answers, but also makes you wonder if Waite is setting up a meta-commentary on how easily evidence gets lost in the shuffle of time.

And then there’s the old enemy. Oh, the old enemy. Because nothing says "cozy mystery" like a villain who’s been lurking in the shadows since the ship’s first voyage. The synopsis doesn’t say what they want, or why they’re finally stepping out of the dark, but you can already hear the groans from fans of *The Long Way to a Small, Angry Planet*—another villain who’s been simmering for decades, waiting for the right moment to strike. It’s the kind of setup that works if the execution is tight, but if Waite’s not careful, it’ll feel like she’s just repackaging the same old chestnut with a new coat of paint. The real question is whether this time, the mystery will feel fresh, or if it’ll just be another layer of bureaucracy on top of an already overcomplicated premise.

But let’s talk about the *real* problem here: the synopsis is so vague it’s almost insulting. We get that Dorothy’s body is failing, that someone triggered her reembodiment early, and that there’s a murder from five hundred years ago that’s still unsolved. But where’s the *humanity*? Where’s the sense of what it’s like to be Dorothy, a woman who’s been through so much she’s probably forgotten what it’s like to be *alive* in the present? The synopsis reads like a legal document, not a story. It’s all systems, laws, and memory-books, with no sense of the emotional weight of what’s happening. It’s like reading a farm report on soil pH levels instead of a character study. Where’s the tension? Where’s the *feeling* that Dorothy’s life is actually on the line?

And don’t even get me started on the kids’ cartoons that do this better. Take *Paw Patrol*, for instance—yes, *Paw Patrol*—because at least they know how to make a mystery feel urgent. In one episode, Marshall the terrier has to solve a case where someone stole all the fire hydrants. The stakes are clear, the characters have distinct personalities, and the solution is satisfying. But *The Double Dorothy*? We’ve got a detective who’s been dead for centuries, a ship that’s been traveling for five hundred years, and a murder that’s still unsolved. And the synopsis doesn’t even tell us *why* it matters. Is it about justice? Is it about closure? Is it about Dorothy finally getting her due? Or is it just another layer of sci-fi bureaucracy, like trying to figure out why your compost heap won’t stop attracting raccoons?

The synopsis leaves you with more questions than answers, which is usually a good sign—except when it’s just lazy writing. Because here’s the thing: if Waite wants to make this work, she’s going to need to give us something more than just a list of plot points. She’s going to need to make us *care* about Dorothy, about the ship, about the murder from five hundred years ago. And she’s going to need to make sure that the old enemy isn’t just another faceless villain, but a character with depth, with motives, with a reason to be here. Because right now, the synopsis reads like a checklist, not a story. And that’s a shame, because if Waite can pull this off, it could be something special. But if she doesn’t, it’ll just be another entry in the endless sea of reincarnated detectives who’ve been dead longer than the dinosaurs.

And now I have to go to that indie film festival downtown, where they’re screening a documentary about a man who raised chickens for forty years and still couldn’t figure out how to make them lay eggs on demand. Literally.
