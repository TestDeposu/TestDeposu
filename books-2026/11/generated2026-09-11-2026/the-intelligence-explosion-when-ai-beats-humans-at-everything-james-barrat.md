Another prophetic warning about silicon gods and our impending digital obsolescence has landed on the desk, arriving just in time to distract us from the quiet, crushing weight of our actual lives. James Barrat is back to tell us that the machines are coming for our jobs, our art, and presumably our souls, packaged neatly in *The Intelligence Explosion*. Because what humanity desperately needs right now, while struggling to piece together a coherent narrative out of the random tragedies that pile up in the living room every Tuesday, is yet another glossy techno-thriller of a nonfiction book predicting our collective extinction by spreadsheet. 

### The Gospel of Silicon Panic

The marketing copy leans heavily into the usual apocalyptic vernacular, promising insider interviews and existential terror about systems that are supposedly error-prone and opaque. Yet, one has to wonder if this entire subgenre—sitting comfortably beside Nick Bostrom’s *Superintelligence* and Max Tegmark’s *Life 3.0*—is merely a sophisticated coping mechanism for people who cannot bear to look at the messy, uncontrollable variables of human grief and mortality. We invent grand, terrifying narratives about rogue algorithms because it feels infinitely safer to imagine a hyper-intelligent machine ending the world than to accept that the universe is fundamentally indifferent to our pain. Or perhaps the book actually offers something profound about human hubris. Actually, strike that. It will probably just recycle the same tired paperclip-maximizer thought experiments while charging thirty dollars for the privilege of feeling panicked before bedtime. 

### The Error-Prone Void

We are told the author equips readers to navigate this chaotic landscape. What a charmingly hollow promise. How exactly does one navigate a landscape where tech executives apparently built things they do not understand? We buy the book. We nod solemnly at the cocktail party. We feel very smart and very doomed. 

Exhausting.
