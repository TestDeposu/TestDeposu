Twenty-five years is a generation. It is enough time for a human mind to heal the deepest fractures, to rebuild a sense of safety from the ash of unimaginable loss. Or, conversely, it is enough time for a lie to harden into bedrock. 

### The Architecture of Suppressed Trauma

Jennifer Hillier understands the fragile mechanics of memory. *Heart of Glass* hinges on an old wound—the kind inflicted during adolescence when the boundaries of selfhood are perilously thin. Seaside, Washington sounds idyllic until a body surfaces in a cranberry bog. Naturally. Small towns love their secrets. They package them up, bury them beneath local industry, and pretend the soil isn't soaked in blood. 

Yet, I find myself annoyed by the publisher's reliance on familiar tropes. We have the classic bifurcation of surviving witnesses: one flees to the metropolis for phantom glamour, while the other lingers in the local newspaper trenches, picking at scabs for a living. Wait. Scratch that. That dynamic is actually brilliant. It exposes how trauma splits a person in two—the part that runs away to reinvent an identity, and the part that anchors itself to the scene of the crime, endlessly seeking accountability that never arrives. 

Freida McFadden compares Hillier to the masters of suspense. I would argue this leans closer to Gillian Flynn's early psychological dissections—minus the pitch-black nihilism, hopefully. Childhood vulnerability is delicate. When a charming drifter infiltrates an amusement park called Wonderland—an almost insultingly heavy-handed bit of irony—we are forced to confront how easily children are swallowed by the dark. 

### When the Past Demands a Recount

The catalyst here is delicious. A recanted confession. A fresh corpse on the eve of a grand reopening. It is theatricality at its finest. Barb and Nicolette spent a quarter-century curating normalcy. But normalcy is just a costume we wear so the neighbors stop staring. 

I refuse to believe this will resolve neatly. Hillier writes with a scalpel, not a hammer. The synopsis hints at buried secrets destroying carefully constructed lives, which feels a bit cliché, but the emotional truth underneath—that what we pack away always rots—carries immense weight when handled by someone who tracks the slow erosion of a young mind. We spend decades telling ourselves we survived the monster, only to realize the monster was merely waiting for the statute of limitations on our innocence to expire. 

My architectural firm just sent over the revised blueprints for the Tuscan villa, and I need to stare at roofline pitch calculations for an hour to clear my head.
