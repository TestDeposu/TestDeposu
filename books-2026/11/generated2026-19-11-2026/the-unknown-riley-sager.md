Look at the glass of this high-rise pane. The city below is a blur of petty negotiations, territorial squabbles, and boardroom coups, yet my heart is currently fluttering with the absolute, foolish yearning of a twenty-year-old poet trespassing in a rose garden. Absurd, perhaps. But beauty demands a reckless surrender. Riley Sager’s upcoming *The Unknown* arrives with a familiar premise—isolated women, cryptic diaries, and a century-old vanishing act that feels suspiciously like a marketing stunt wrapped in a shroud. 

### Echoes in the Foliage

Struggling actresses and legendary divas converging on a Vermont commune of vanished spiritual mediums. One hundred years of silence punctuated by five dresses hanging from an old oak tree. The imagery is deliciously gothic, dripping with the kind of theatricality that usually makes my eye twitch during state dinners. Here's the catch. I am hopelessly charmed by the sheer audacity of it. Sager leans heavily into the locked-room mystery aesthetic, channeling a modern Shirley Jackson by way of a Hollywood press junket. 

The thematic architecture relies entirely on the permeable boundary between performance and reality. When the cast gets stranded—because of course a sudden health emergency traps them on the island, courtesy of narrative convenience—the artifice of filmmaking dissolves into genuine panic. Are the mysterious symbols carved into the bark a cynical ploy to derail the production, or did the 1926 séance genuinely summon a malevolent specter with a penchant for historical reenactment? Actually, scratch that. The symbols are definitely just a disgruntled PA trying to secure early wrap times. No, wait, that completely undermines the atmospheric dread Sager is painstakingly constructing. Let us pivot entirely: the terror must be deeply metaphysical, an ontological rot eating through the celluloid dreams of the director. 

### The Currency of Ghosts

Spiritualism in the roaring twenties was essentially the ultimate diplomatic gambit—negotiating terms with an invisible sovereign state where the electorate cannot vote, complain, or leak memos to the press. Daisy Rue’s recovered diary functions as a diplomatic cable from the dead. It is the ultimate macabre artifact. Sager clearly understands how to weaponize nostalgia, turning Lake Faraday into a holding pen for our collective anxieties about things we cannot control or audit. 

Yet, one must interrogate the boilerplate mechanics of the setup. Stranded actors parsing cryptic journals while picking off tropes one by one feels dangerously close to standard-issue slasher territory masquerading as prestige suspense. Where is the structural innovation, darling? We have seen this cinematic chess match before, played with slightly different pieces. Still, the romantic in me hopes the supernatural elements actually materialize with a vengeance, slaying the cynicism right out of the third act. 

Excuse me for a second. I need to go aggressively scream at the gardener for planting blue hydrangeas in alkaline soil because someone clearly failed to read the topographical memo.
