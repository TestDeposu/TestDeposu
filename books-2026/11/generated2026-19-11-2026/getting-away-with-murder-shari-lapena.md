**Author:** Shari Lapena  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-07-28 APL

The synopsis for *Getting Away with Murder* arrives like a poorly wrapped gift—elegant in its own way, but already stained with the faintest hint of something *off*. The prose is slick, the premise polished, but there’s a certain… *familiarity* to it, like a vintage Chanel perfume that’s been worn by every society matron in the last decade. Jill and Ted’s brownstone isn’t just a setting; it’s a character in its own right, a gilded cage where the walls whisper of *The Girl on the Train*’s suburban rot, but with the veneer of *Gone Girl*’s calculated cruelty. And yet—where is the *sharpness*? Where is the *unpredictability*? The synopsis reads like a menu at a five-star restaurant where every dish is labeled "Surprise," but you’ve already seen the recipe.

The core tension here is the classic "marriage as a crime syndicate," a trope so well-worn it’s starting to smell like the abandoned set of *The Stepford Wives*—but with the budget of a Netflix limited series. Jill and Ted’s financial desperation is the kind of motivation that feels like it was lifted from a *Bachelor* contestant’s desperate Instagram caption: *"We just need one more inheritance to save our dream life."* It’s the kind of logic that makes you wonder if the author has ever actually *lived* in a brownstone, or if they’ve just Googled "how to make a murder plot sound plausible." The synopsis leans hard into the "trust is fragile" angle, which is fine—until you realize that’s the emotional core of *every* psychological thriller since *The Silence of the Lambs*. Where is the *twist*? Where is the *subversion*? The way the synopsis trails off with *"As long as there are no surprises…"* is almost *too* on-the-nose, like a villain in a bad soap opera who thinks their own monologue is the climax.

But then—*pivot*—perhaps that’s the point. Maybe Lapena is playing with the expectations of the genre, letting the reader *think* they’re in for another *The Couple Next Door*, only to deliver something far more insidious. The synopsis doesn’t *tell* you the book will be a masterclass in unreliable narration, but it *hints* at it, like a hostess slipping a glass of wine with something extra into your hand. And if Lapena is anything like her earlier work, she’ll likely drip-feed the reader with red herrings so elaborate they feel like the set designs from *Knives Out*—every detail a potential clue, every character a potential liar. The question isn’t whether Jill and Ted will get away with murder—it’s whether the reader will.

Thematic depth, though? That’s where the synopsis falters. There’s no exploration of the *psychology* behind their decision, no examination of how a couple can convince themselves that love and logic are the same thing. It’s all surface-level: *"They have carefully, expensively, made every inch of it their own."* How *original*. The brownstone isn’t just a home; it’s a *statement*, a monument to their own delusions of grandeur. And yet, the synopsis doesn’t ask the critical question: *What happens when the facade collapses?* Is this a story about the cost of perfection, or is it just another cautionary tale about people who think they’re smarter than they are? The latter feels more likely, given how neatly the plot fits into the "rich people do terrible things" narrative that’s been mined ad nauseam since *The Wolf of Wall Street*’s more tragic cousin, *The Big Short*.

And then there’s the *zero-context pop culture drop*—because of course there is. The synopsis’s reliance on the "inheritance as salvation" trope makes me think of the *2007 Met Gala disaster*, when a designer’s gown was so poorly executed that it looked like it had been cobbled together from a thrift store and a dream. The kind of disaster where everyone in the room *knew* it was a mistake, but no one dared say it aloud. That’s the vibe here: a thriller that’s been stitched together with the enthusiasm of a seamstress who’s never held a needle, but thinks she knows how it’s done.

The final irony? The synopsis itself is so *smooth* that it’s almost *boring*. There’s no urgency, no desperation—just the cold, calculated rhythm of a thriller that’s been plotted out like a *MasterChef* episode where the judges already know who’s going to win. And yet, if Lapena delivers on even half her potential, this could be the kind of book that makes you question whether you’re reading a thriller or a psychological autopsy. But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the book is just another entry in the *"look how clever I am"* graveyard of contemporary fiction, where the real crime isn’t the murder—it’s the reader’s patience.
