**Author:** Kelsey Cox  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-07-07 APL

The synopsis for *Pretty Dead Things* reads like a script for a pageant version of *The Stepford Wives*—if the Stepford wives were also trapped in a blackout with a murderer who might be the hostess. The premise is so broad it could swallow any number of subplots whole, leaving only the most generic of psychological thrills intact. The 2000 disappearance of Isabelle Whitmore is the kind of setup that feels like it belongs in a *True Detective* season finale, but the 2025 pageant angle is where things start to unravel into something dangerously close to a *Big Little Lie* parody.

The characters are introduced with the kind of vague menace that makes them feel like they’ve been plucked from a *Gossip Girl* reunion episode—Ingrid fleeing a crime, Melanie drowning in nostalgia, Cat sobering up like a bad habit, Sarah Lynn treating the pageant like a throne she’s never willing to relinquish. It’s all very *Sharp Objects* in its fixation on small-town trauma, but where Gillian Flynn’s work had the precision of a scalpel, this feels more like a blunt instrument left in a hotel room. The storm that traps everyone together? A classic *Knives Out* device, but here it’s deployed with the subtlety of a sledgehammer.

Yet—and here’s where the pivot happens—there’s something undeniably *fun* about the way the synopsis leans into the absurdity of it all. The idea of a pageant as a pressure cooker for murder is so overdone it borders on satire, but if Cox can make the tension feel as electric as a *Big Little Lie* finale without resorting to the same tired tropes, she might just pull it off. The real question is whether she’ll commit to the bit or get bogged down in the kind of melodrama that makes *The Girlfriend Experience* look like a masterclass in restraint.

The synopsis doesn’t do much to distinguish itself from the endless cycle of women’s psychological thrillers that dominate the market right now, but the fact that it’s being marketed as an *instant* bestseller suggests it’s banking on the same formula that made *The Housemaid* and *The Wife Between Us* fly off shelves. The twist, if it exists, will likely hinge on some variation of "the killer was hiding in plain sight," which is so predictable it’s almost endearing in its lack of ambition. But then again, maybe that’s the point—maybe the whole thing is a satire of the genre itself, a *Pretty Little Liars* meets *The Stepford Wives* satire where the real horror is how easily we believe in these narratives.

The vague references to "long-standing grudges" and "old demons" are the kind of clichés that make you wonder if Cox is trying to write a book or a *Bachelor* season. The storm, the trapped women, the murder—it’s all so familiar it’s almost comforting, like a favorite sweater that’s seen better days. But if she can inject some of the sharp wit of *Rachel Hawkins* or the dark humor of *Amanda Eyre Ward*, she might just make this work. For now, though, it feels like another entry in the "beauty pageant as metaphor for female rage" subgenre, which is fine if you’re looking for a predictable slog.

The only thing that might save it is the title itself—*Pretty Dead Things*—which has a certain *Twin Peaks* vibe to it, like something out of a David Lynch script where the beauty is as poisonous as the roses. If Cox can make the pageant feel as surreal and unsettling as a *Lynch* film, she might just pull off something special. But if she sticks to the usual suspects—trapped women, a killer among them, a storm that’s just a plot device—then it’ll be another entry in the endless cycle of women’s thrillers that feel like they were written by committee.

And now, because I’ve spent too much time thinking about this, I need to call my broker about the sudden market shift in the "women’s psychological thriller" sector. The numbers are looking grim, and I’m not sure if I want to invest in another round of *The Wife Between Us* clones. Maybe I’ll just stick to fine wine.
