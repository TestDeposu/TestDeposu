Leather bound octavos do not smell like modern digital galleys, do they? They smell like decaying hide and twenty-year-old pipe tobacco left to cure on a mahogany writing desk overlooking Mayfair. Yet here we are, staring down the precipice of Ben Reeves' *Everything was Beautiful and Nothing Hurt*, due in July 2026. A title borrowed from Kurt Vonnegut—or perhaps lifted as a desperate plea for cultural relevance before a single page is even printed. How remarkably quaint. 

### The Mercantile Death Trope

We are expected to marvel at personified mortality living in a small, gray town with a cat. Groundbreaking. One can practically hear the collective sigh of every creative writing MFA cohort from Iowa to East Anglia. Travis is Death. Travis has a feline companion. Travis meets a midwife and her boisterous child. The sheer predictability of it reads like a treatment drafted during a three-martini lunch at the Groucho Club circa 1994, only to be digitized and repackaged for commuters reading on oversized phones. 

And the blurbs. Good grief, the blurbs. Jodi Picoult comparing a book about a soft-hearted reaper to "nothing you've ever read before." Has she forgotten *On Point* or perhaps basic mythology? It gives off severe Björk swan dress energy—desperately theatrical, entirely derivative, and exhausting to look at. 

### Chronological Whimsy and Manufactured Melancholy

Reeves clearly fancies himself the heir to the whimsical-mortality throne. But what happens when the magical realism wears off? You are left with a stoic, naive reaper learning what it means to be alive because a child yells in a hallway. The domesticity of the afterlife. How utterly domestic. 

### The Perils of Inevitable Nostalgia

We will undoubtedly be subjected to endless discussions about "the brevity of life." As if any reader over the age of twelve required a celestial bureaucrat with a cat to explain the linear passage of time. The prose promises radiant warmth. Give me cold, clinical misanthropy any day over engineered sentimentalism designed to move units at airport bookstores. 

One wonders if publishers actually read the manuscripts, or if they simply feed tropes into an algorithm that spits out titles featuring mortality, cats, and single mothers across the hall. The trajectory is as steep and tragic as the ill-fated maiden voyage of the Titanic submersible. 

I am going to pour a glass of fifteen-year-old scotch and log off.
