**Author:** Lisa See  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-06-09 APL

Lisa See is back at it, dragging us through historical trauma like she gets a commission per tear shed. *Daughters of the Sun and Moon* drops us squarely into 1870s Los Angeles, because apparently, our current timeline isn't bleak enough. Three women—Dove, Petal, and Moon—arrive in a dusty pueblo that treats them with the warmth of a freezing meat locker. Dove is innocent and bound-footed. Petal was sold for rice seed. Moon is educated, beautiful, and limping. 

File this under "What were they thinking?"

The publisher’s copy reads like a checklist for historical fiction bingo: arranged marriages, foot-binding, Gold Mountain despair, and a looming historical atrocity called the Night of Horrors. It is giving high-concept misery porn, but make it literary. We have the classic triad dynamic. The innocent. The pragmatist. The intellectual. Are they real people or archetypes pulled from a 101-level Asian American studies syllabus? Hard to tell from the blurb alone, seeing as the actual manuscript is locked in a vault somewhere while I subsist entirely on lukewarm tap water and spite. 

### The Commodification of Suffering

Let us discuss the thematic infrastructure here, assuming the prose rises above the cliché plot points. See relies heavily on the concept of "eating bitterness," an idiom that translates roughly to enduring systemic abuse without losing your mind—or at least trying not to. But wait, let me walk that back. Maybe the endurance angle is actually a masterclass in feminist resilience. Actually, no. It is just capitalist historical fiction capitalizing on pain tourism. Why invent complex interpersonal dynamics when you can drop characters into an anti-Chinese race riot and call it character development? 

The structural opposition between Dove, Petal, and Moon maps out a tidy little grid of female agency under patriarchal duress. Dove wants love. Petal wants freedom. Moon wants justice. It is all terribly neat. Real life is messier. People are trash. Yet readers will gobble this up on their Sunday commutes, chasing that sweet, sweet catharsis before returning to their spreadsheets. If you liked *Pachinko* or anything by Amy Tan that made you ugly-cry on a Delta flight, this is your next fix. The prose will undoubtedly be polished, clean, and utterly sanitized for the book club crowd. 

### The Aesthetic of Despair

And yet, I find myself deeply annoyed by the marketing copy's reliance on buzzwords. "Unexpected friendship." "Turmoil." "Brutal historical reality." It is all so terribly predictable. Give me some narrative risk. Give me a protagonist who is aggressively unlikable. Instead, we get noble sufferers fighting against a hostile world. It is the literary equivalent of a beige living room. 

The historical backdrop—the actual 1871 Los Angeles Chinese massacre—is a genuinely horrific event that deserves careful, bruising novelistic excavation. Whether See treats it with gravitas or reduces it to a dramatic backdrop for a girl-power bonding montage remains to be seen. I would bet fifty bucks on the latter. But what do I know? I am just sitting here staring at an unread proof while my landlord texts me about rent. 

I have to go debate the merits of absurdism with a stranger in a dive bar.
