**Author:** Sarah Pekkanen  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-08-04 APL

Silence arrives in quiet shifts after the bedroom door finally closes and the house exhales. Sometimes it feels heavy enough to touch, settling right across my chest like a physical weight while I stare at the ceiling and wonder how a heart can keep beating when a piece of it is permanently missing elsewhere. But books offer a different kind of quiet—a space to wrestle with ghosts that aren't your own. 

Sarah Pekkanen is stepping away from domestic drama in *The Women in White*, dragging us backward into 1964 with parapsychology, missing university students, and a house completely devoid of modern light. And honestly? I am exhausted by the current literary obsession with split timelines. We get it. The past bleeds into the present. We do not need a temporal anchor in every single thriller published this decade. 

Or maybe we do. 

Because grief has a funny way of making time fold in on itself, trapping you in a single decade while the rest of the world moves on to high-speed internet and cell phones. Betty living in total isolation with her 5 o'clock martinis makes a twisted sort of sense. She is guarding secrets that belong to an era before the noise took over. 

Paranormal experiments always skirt the dangerous edge of cheese. If Pekkanen leans too heavily into cheap jump scares and phantom footsteps, this whole premise will collapse under its own weight. Yet the psychological anchor—a divorced woman trying to rebuild her shattered life by taking care of a frozen widow—holds real promise. It reminds me of the atmospheric dread in Simone St. James’s work, where historical trauma lingers in the floorboards like mold. 

Suspicion.

Isolation.

Redemption.

Nobody gets out of 1964 alive. Or at least, not without some serious psychological damage. Pekkanen usually writes sharp, character-driven domestic suspense, so sliding into the speculative realm feels like a risky gamble for her brand. Will her traditional readership follow her down this weird psychic rabbit hole? 

We shall see. 

Wait. Did I just say this is a risky gamble? Let me retract that entirely. Readers eat up nostalgia mixed with the supernatural—just look at the endless appetite for mid-century gothic horror right now. It is actually the safest possible bet an author can make in this market. 

God help me. I hear the distinctive, permanent squeak of a Sharpie downstairs against the newly painted hallway baseboards.
