The synopsis for *The Leveret* arrives bearing the usual blurb-heavy artillery—praise from a once-in-a-generation talent, no less—yet it manages to sketch out a genuinely suffocating atmosphere of domestic dread. Countryside relocations in contemporary fiction rarely end with herb gardens and restorative sleep. Instead, Anna Goldreich trades pastoral charm for psychological claustrophobia, trapping two women in a rural past one of them desperately fled. Give me an urban gothic over a cabin in the woods any day of the week, but I will make an exception for feral wildlife introduced to a fragile partnership. 

Clare drifts through the cottage while Phoebe throws herself into family farm labor. A classic literary dichotomy of internal dissociation versus external avoidance. Then comes the wild card. Or rather, the wild hare. 

### The Zoology of Misplaced Affection

Naming a baby hare Isla immediately signals metaphorical weight. Too much weight. Whenever a debut author introduces a biting, scratching marsupial or lagomorph into a domestic crisis, you know the creature is doing heavy lifting for a relationship on life support. Is Isla a metaphor for grief? A manifestation of Clare's deteriorating grip on reality? A furry grenade tossed into an already volatile dynamic? Probably all three. I am already exhausted by the symbolism. No, wait—let me walk that back entirely. Maybe the hare is just a hare, and critics like myself project too much literary architecture onto what is simply a story about a woman who loses her mind over a rodent. 

Or perhaps I am just reacting to the sheer predictability of rural alienation tropes. We have seen this exact psychological weather pattern in *The Loney* or even *The Harpy*. But Goldreich allegedly transmutes grief into a dark fantasy of refuge. Notice how the synopsis carefully avoids telling us whether anything supernatural actually happens, or if we are trapped inside Clare's unreliable, fracturing perspective. Ambiguity is the easiest trick in the gothic playbook. Just blur the lines between madness and magic and let the reader do the heavy lifting. I'm formally requesting a refund on my time. 

### The Cult of the Bruisingly Tender

The phrase bruisingly tender needs to be retired from publishing marketing copy immediately. It implies a narrative that beats you about the head with emotional authenticity until you cry into your oat milk. Yet the tension between Phoebe watching the creature grow wild and Clare retreating into her own skull actually holds some structural promise. It mimics the stubborn way humans try to domesticate their own trauma—feeding it, naming it, only for it to turn around and scratch the hand that holds the carrot. 

And yet, I remain fiercely hostile toward any book that promises to leave me gasping for air. Just write a clean sentence. Do not choke the reader. Goldreich might pull off this delicate tightrope walk between grief memoir and feral fable, or the book might drown in its own heavy-breathing adjectives. Time will tell. Mostly, I just distrust anyone who names a baby hare before checking if the local vet handles wildlife. 

I need to check the tire pressure on my wife's car before her road trip.
