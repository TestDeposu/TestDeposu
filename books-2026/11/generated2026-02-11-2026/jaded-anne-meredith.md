**Author:** Anne Meredith  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The air outside smells of damp leaves and the faintest hint of woodsmoke, the kind that lingers after a neighbor’s fire has long since died down. It’s the kind of November that makes you wonder if autumn is just a season for nostalgia, a time when the world slows down enough to let you examine its rot. *Jaded* arrives like one of those old campus novels—thick with the kind of heat that doesn’t just simmer but *boils*—and yet, the synopsis is so deliberately vague it feels like a professor mid-lecture, waving their hands over a topic they’ve only half-thought through. "A mysterious young woman," "a dance of obsession," "every secret becomes a weapon"—oh, for the love of it, just *tell us*.

The problem isn’t that the premise is uninteresting—no, it’s that it’s *too* interested in being interesting. There’s a certain kind of literary laziness in dropping names like "power," "complicity," and "abuse" as if they’re props in a play rather than the actual bones of the story. It’s the same way some contemporary fiction treats consent like a mood board—check the boxes, nod sagely, and call it a day. But here’s the thing: if *Jaded* is going to be about the "messy web of lies," then why not make the lies *messy*? Why not make the power dynamics *uncomfortable*? Instead, we get the kind of academic-sounding blurb that sounds like it was ghostwritten by a committee of people who’ve never actually been in a room with a married couple who also happen to be professors. It’s the literary equivalent of a Yelp review that says, "The ambiance was *very* atmospheric."

And then there’s the casting of Jade—this "hand grenade" who "breaks all their rules." —which is either a brilliant metaphor or a lazy one, depending on whether the author actually understands what a hand grenade *does*. Does she detonate? Does she just sit there, ominous as a ticking clock? The synopsis doesn’t say. It’s all smoke and mirrors, the kind of vague menace that makes you suspect the book will be less about psychological depth and more about *style*—the kind of style that thinks "a dance of obsession" is a line that needs no explanation. It’s the same energy as that one *Stranger Things* episode where the characters just *stand around* and talk about their feelings like they’re in a focus group for *The Secret History*.

But let’s be fair—this isn’t the first time we’ve seen this trope. There’s something about married professors and their student affairs that’s been mined so thoroughly it’s starting to feel like a genre unto itself. Think *The Secret History*, but with less literary pretension and more *Real Housewives* energy. The difference here is that *Jaded* seems to want to be *both*—the highbrow campus drama and the tabloid scandal, all wrapped up in a bow of "thematic depth." And yet, the synopsis reads like it was written by someone who’s never actually *read* a book about power dynamics, let alone lived one. It’s the literary equivalent of a TikTok trend—catchy, performative, and ultimately hollow.

The real question is whether *Jaded* will deliver on the promise of its own vagueness. Will Jade be more than just a plot device? Will Sam and Vera be more than caricatures of marital stagnation? Or will this be another book where the "explosive ending" is just a gimmick, a way to sell copies without actually *earning* them? The synopsis doesn’t give us enough to hope—or to despair.
