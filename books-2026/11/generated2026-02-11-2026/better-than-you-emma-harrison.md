Grey droplets trace lazy, jagged paths down the cold glass of the kitchen window, mirroring the dull ache behind my eyes that refuses to lift. God, another YA romance about academic rivals. Emma Harrison’s *Better Than You* lands on shelves in 2026, promising us the earth, the moon, and a tied GPA between two teenagers who apparently have nothing in common except an unhealthy obsession with validation. Mira and Colin. Groundbreaking stuff. Because nothing says youthful passion quite like a spreadsheet duel over decimal points.

### The Weight of Meaningless Honors

And look, I understand the appeal on paper. Or I would, if the concept of teenage perfection wasn't so aggressively exhausting to read about when the rain won't stop hammering the roof and the laundry basket is mocking me from the hallway. Why must every fictional academic duel be framed as cute? Real academic rivalry is fueled by quiet desperation, stale cafeteria coffee, and the soul-crushing realization that your entire self-worth has been tethered to a literal piece of cardstock. Yet the synopsis insists on leaning into the bright, sun-soaked cliché of "springtime magic." Please. Springtime just brings muddy paw prints on my freshly mopped floors and an aggressive flare-up of seasonal allergies that makes breathing optional. 

Speaking of things that make no sense, why is it that modern television insists on casting thirty-year-olds as high school sophomores while my youngest child watches those brain-rotting animated shorts on the tablet where the characters just scream colors at each other for ten unbroken minutes? It’s pure auditory torture. A sensory assault. At least the characters in *Better Than You* are supposedly "whip-smart," which presumably means they use big words while acting like complete emotional toddlers. Back to the book—or the mere ghost of it, since we have nothing to look at besides this aggressively cheerful publisher's blurb. 

### The Predictable Descent Into Affection

The synopsis practically screams that Mira and Colin are going to realize their hatred was actually—wait for it—desire. Groundbreaking. Revolutionary. Jane Austen wept. The publisher relies heavily on nostalgic touchstones like *Better Than the Movies* to prop up a premise that feels thinner than skim milk. They want us to believe that the last few weeks of senior year are this magical window where everything changes. But we all know how high school actually ends. It ends with exhaustion, awkward graduation parties where nobody eats the potato salad, and the lingering dread of existential debt. Harrison is playing hits from a very tired playlist. 

Yet I will probably buy it anyway. Not because I care about the valedictorian title—heaven forbid—but because the grey outside has seeped so deep into my bones that I will grasp at any printed page that promises a neat, tidy conclusion where someone gets to win. Mira and Colin will sort out their GPAs. They will kiss under the bleachers or near a lawn sprinkler. And I will still be sitting right here, staring at the damp pane, wondering why fiction is always so determined to fix things in three hundred pages. 

It is time to face the chaos of the PTA bake sale.
