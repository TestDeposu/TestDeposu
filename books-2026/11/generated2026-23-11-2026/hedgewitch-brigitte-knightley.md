Smuggling a dagger under the stiff lace of a governess's collar while your employer plots your entire demographic's extinction is certainly one way to handle unresolved tension. Brigitte Knightley returns in 2026 with *Hedgewitch*, leaning heavily into the marketable comfort zone of enemies-to-lovers tropes that currently dominate TikTok book spaces with the predictability of a metronome. We are expected to swoon over a prickly protagonist named Thalline who infiltrates the fortress of a Highlands warlord—Lord Breoch, a man with a bounty on her kind—only to discover that his dark aura feels strangely magnetic instead of immediately repellant. 

Do not get me started on the absolute disaster that was trying to convince the local school board last Tuesday that denying a bruised nine-year-old access to the sensory garden because of a broken fence post is fundamentally counterproductive to nervous system regulation, especially when you can physically watch the cortisol spikes detonating behind their terrified eyes like tiny, silent fireworks in a foggy jar. Anyway.

Knightley knows her audience well, trading in the slow-burn currency popularized by Sarah J. Maas and Jennifer L. Armentrout, though one prays the pacing avoids the three-hundred-page middle sag that plagues modern fantasy like a damp rot in the floorboards. 

Predictable.

### Shadows in the Parlor

The thematic architecture relies entirely on the tired dichotomy of the hidden marginalized group versus the stoic, menacing authority figure who inevitably harbors a tragic backstory involving a dead mother or a cursed bloodline. Thalline thrives in the margins, the gaps, the dark places—metaphors that read like an interior designer's mood board for trauma chic rather than actual psychological depth. Yet the synopsis asks us to buy into a lethal espionage mission that immediately defangs itself the moment the assassin starts noticing how well her terrifying target fills out a doublet. 

Boring.

We will undoubtedly wade through endless chapters of lingering eye contact across porridge bowls and sharp verbal sparring that lacks any actual danger, because modern romantasy refuses to let its leads commit to genuine cruelty. Breoch's terrifying darkness will evaporate the second he bandages her scraped knuckle by the hearth. 

Groundbreaking. 

I need to go read Marcus Aurelius right now to cleanse my mind of this plot.
