**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Money buys a lot of things, yet it conspicuously fails to purchase an original premise. Abigail Owen’s upcoming *The Bone Singer* reads like a panicked Frankenstein creation stitched together from discarded pitch decks at a mid-tier streaming studio. We are expected to care about Elizabeth—a woman currently losing her grip on sanity while the men in her life gaslight her about a dead infant—because apparently, postpartum gaslighting is the hot new literary trend. It has all the psychological subtlety of the Fyre Festival. 

### Rotting Bones and Recycled Tropes

Look at the structural division offered in the marketing copy: "Lost Souls" paired with "The Queen Unmasked." How profoundly lazy. We get dual narratives because writers today apparently suffer from a collective attention span deficit. One side gives us gaslit domestic dread in an insane asylum. The other gives us a forced marriage to an unknown king on a distant island because a sister committed an "unforgivable betrayal." 

And what is that betrayal? Stealing an heirloom? Poisoning a falcon? The synopsis leaves it blank, forcing us to buy the damn thing just to find out if the stakes are higher than a mid-season episode of *Succession*. 

Envy is rottenness to the bones, or so the blurb claims. Yet the real rot here sits in the writer's room, where someone decided that doubling up on tired tropes constitutes world-building. We have the classic madhouse motif colliding with the ancient fairy-tale trope of the reluctant bride shipped off to a remote fortress. It is the literary equivalent of serving a wagyu steak topped with Cheez Whiz. 

### The Aesthetics of Despair

Grimdark deserves better than this derivative shuffling of psychological distress and royal coercion. Where is the teeth? Where is the genuine, soul-crushing nihilism that makes a reader want to pour a neat scotch and stare into a marble fireplace for three hours? Instead, we get Elizabeth wandering around wondering if her mother’s madness is hereditary, while halfway across the map, Ivy deals with a mandatory wedding. 

Call it what it is: commercialized misery porn packaged for people who think reading Joe Abercrombie makes them dangerous. I have seen more coherent psychological horror in a private equity board meeting. 

My youngest just painted the family cat blue.
