**Author:** Ross Montgomery  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-01-06 APL

Chopping these carrots feels entirely redundant when the youngest refuses to touch anything orange, yet here I am, knife in hand, glancing sideways at *The Murder at World's End*. Ross Montgomery is trying to sell us a Cornish apocalypse in 1910, complete with Halley's Comet and a sealed manor. 

I respectfully disagree with the publisher's hype. 

Comparing every single country house mystery to *Knives Out* or *Downton Abbey* has become an absolute laziness of modern marketing. Can we not let a book stand on its own two feet anymore? Every time someone pitches a wealthy eccentric kicking the bucket in a draughty estate, the suits in marketing immediately dust off the Rian Johnson comparisons like they invented upper-class snobbery. 

Give me a break. 

The setup relies entirely on the classic locked-room trope—a Viscount dead by his own crossbow inside a sealed study while waiting for the end of the world. It is delightfully theatrical. But let us be entirely honest about the execution potential here. Half these contemporary historical mysteries spend three hundred pages bogged down in tedious tea-service logistics before anyone actually bothers to inspect the corpse. 

And speaking of logistics, if my eight-year-old drops one more raw egg onto the linoleum floor while supposedly helping with the baking, there will be a real-life homicide in this kitchen before tomorrow morning. Why did I ever agree to let them crack the eggs independently? It was an act of pure domestic madness. The shells are currently mingling with the flour in a fashion that defies all culinary decency. 

Back to Cornwall. 

Stephen Pike—fresh out of Borstal for a crime he did not commit—is paired with Miss Decima Stockingham, an octogenarian who sounds delightfully foul-mouthed. That dynamic actually saves the premise from sliding into pure, unadulterated pastiche. An under-butler and a sarcastic eighty-year-old matriarch hunting a killer through secret passages? That I can get behind. It avoids the tired, tweed-clad detective trope that has plagued British crime fiction since the Golden Age. 

We need more unhinged old women solving crimes. Literature has been too polite for too long. If Decima does not spend at least half the book terrorizing the remaining staff with her sharp tongue, Montgomery has missed a massive trick. 

The thematic core here is isolation—the literal sealing of Tithe Hall against cosmic doom mirroring the emotional walls of its inhabitants. It is a solid, sturdy framework for a mystery. Secrets and buried grudges always taste better when the doors are bolted from the inside. 

I need to go bake three dozen cupcakes by tomorrow morning.
