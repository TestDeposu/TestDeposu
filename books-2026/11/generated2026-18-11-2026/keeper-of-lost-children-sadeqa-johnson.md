The laundry room smells perpetually of sour mildew and wet dog because nothing in this house ever truly dries during a damp mid-Atlantic Tuesday. Or maybe it is just my own clothes, soaked through from standing under the leaking gutters outside while staring at the mailbox for a book that refuses to arrive faster. Sadeqa Johnson is returning with *Keeper of Lost Children*, and I am already clutching my chest before a single page has even turned. 

### The Architecture of Absence

We are promised rubble, divided loyalties, and the cruel geography of the 1950s—a landscape where children are left behind like luggage in a train station. But let us be entirely honest about the synopsis. It relies on that familiar, heavy-handed machinery of historical fiction where every coincidence feels engineered by a divine clockmaker with a cruel streak. Ozzie meets Jelka in Manheim; Ethel stumbles upon an orphanage; Sophia finds a secret at an elite Maryland boarding school. Are we really supposed to believe these three trajectories will weave together without feeling like a contrivance cooked up in a publisher’s boardroom? Yes, probably. Because Johnson writes with the kind of emotional artillery that makes cynical readers weep into their cold coffee anyway. 

The television in the den has been blaring that infernal cartoon about the blue heeler family for forty-five minutes straight because the toddler refuses to blink, and I swear if I hear one more cheerful Australian accent lecture me about sharing feelings while I am trying to dissect the brutal socio-political realities of post-war occupation policies, I am going to launch the remote through the drywall. Honestly, why can't modern animation just feature silent, stoic animals staring blankly into the middle distance like the black-and-white films of my youth? It would be far more appropriate for the existential dread radiating from these multi-generational family secrets. 

### Inheritance and the Weight of Belonging

Strip away the marketing copy and what remains is an obsession with parentage. Who claims us when the state looks away? Sophia hunting for her identity in 1965 Maryland hits a nerve—the quiet desperation of a girl staring at white walls in a fancy school, realizing her whole life is built on a foundation of omission. It reminds me of the crushing weight found in Yaa Gyasi’s *Homegoing* or Brit Bennett’s *The Vanishing Half*, where bloodlines become crime scenes and identity is something you have to steal back from history. 

And yet, I am terrified. Will Ethel's mission in Occupied Germany read like a white savior narrative with a polished veneer, or will it actually reckon with the staggering intersection of race, military occupation, and institutional erasure? The synopsis stays safely in the realm of inspirational blurbs—"love in its myriad of forms"—which usually means the rough edges of history are going to be sanded down into a palatable, book-club-friendly tearjerker. I want teeth. I want the bitter gall of abandonment, not just transcendent healing wrapped in a pretty bow. But Johnson earned our trust with *The House of Eve*, so I suppose I will clear the weeping schedule for February. 

The washing machine just flooded the laundry room.
