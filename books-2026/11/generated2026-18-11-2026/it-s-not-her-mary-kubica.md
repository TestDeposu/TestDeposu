The frost is already hardening the mud in the lower pasture, crunching beneath my muck boots like cheap glass every time I haul another split round of oak toward the porch. Autumn in this hollow is a sharp business, stripping the maples down to grey skeletons while the crows bicker endlessly over the remaining frozen persimmons. Yet my mind is entirely snagged on a lakeside cottage somewhere miles away, where a November chill of a distinctly human variety is freezing blood rather than sap. Mary Kubica is back with *It’s Not Her*, and the premise relies on that most reliable of suburban Gothic anxieties: the rot hidden beneath the cedar siding of a vacation home. 

Consider the staging. A secluded lake resort. Two families. A shattered silence. It is a formula that feels intensely claustrophobic—though perhaps claustrophobia is too generous a word for a narrative setup we have seen polished to a dull sheen by half a dozen domestic suspense authors this decade alone. Let me correct myself immediately. I claimed the setting was tired, but the truth is I am entirely seduced by the sheer economy of it. Give me a corpse by the water and a missing teenager named Reese, and I will happily ignore the structural echoes of Ruth Ware or Lisa Jewell. 

### The Geography of Malice

Kubica has built her career on the architecture of unreliable memory and domestic betrayal—think of *Local Woman Missing* or *She's Not Sorry*. Here, the synopsis leans heavily into the claustrophobia of the woods and the water. Is the missing girl a victim, or is she the architect of the bloodshed? That binary is the oldest trick in the thriller playbook. And yet, the prose hooked me while I stood ankle-deep in compost, turning over wet leaves with a pitchfork. The text promises a town full of secrets, which is literary shorthand for everyone in the zip code lying about their tax returns and their extramarital affairs. 

I demand absolute fidelity to psychological realism in these rural noir setups. If the local police chief turns out to be compromised by a thirty-year-old municipal cover-up involving a missing canoe and a wealthy patriarch, I am throwing the paperback directly into the woodstove. But Kubica usually avoids the more ridiculous supernatural dips of contemporary suspense, keeping her boots firmly planted in the cold mud of human greed and marital resentment. 

The sleeping nephew upstairs—unharmed, untouched—is the detail that elevates the hook from mundane true-crime pastiche to actual art. Why leave one child and slaughter the parents? The juxtaposition of infantile innocence against adult carnage is cheap manipulation, yes, but it is effective manipulation. My coffee is growing cold on the workbench, iced over with a skin of frost, and I have completely neglected the winterizing of the greenhouse irrigation lines because I am staring at a promotional blurb from Jeneva Rose. 

We are drowning in domestic suspense, an endless sea of dead spouses and gaslit wives. Still, Kubica writes with an obsidian-sharp clarity that cuts through the noise of the genre. 

I am going to collapse in my sagging leather recliner with a wool blanket over my knees and attempt to completely ignore the fact that the sheep have broken through the fence into the winter squash patch.
