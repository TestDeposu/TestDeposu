**Author:** Will Wight  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The air in this gala’s private salon is thick with the scent of overpriced cognac and the faint metallic tang of someone’s third martini—probably the man in the velvet jacket who just called *The Last of Us* "a depressing indictment of capitalism" while clutching his phone like it was a lifeline. I should be scoffing at that, but instead, I’m oddly intrigued by the synopsis for *Overworld*, because—well, here’s the thing: it’s the kind of premise that makes you wonder if Will Wight finally cracked the code on self-publishing’s sweet spot. A butler. A collapsing world. Ten days. It’s the kind of setup that could either be a gimmick or a masterstroke, depending on whether the author leans into the absurdity or drowns in it.

The butler trope is *so* 2010s, isn’t it? We’ve had *Downton Abbey*’s valet, *The Butcher’s Daughter*’s morally gray efficiency, even that one *Assassin’s Creed* game where the butler was just a glorified NPC with a side quest about polishing silver. But here’s the kicker: Eldric Altmoore isn’t just a butler. He’s a *royal* butler, which immediately elevates the stakes—because nothing says "high stakes" like a man who’s spent his life folding napkins for kings now having to outmaneuver goblins and time itself. The tension between his meticulous, almost obsessive orderliness and the chaos of the Overworld is the kind of contrast that could either feel like a gimmick or a *brilliant* thematic anchor. Think of it as the literary equivalent of a chef serving a perfectly seared steak on a plate of raw meat—if done right, it’s a revelation.

Yet—and here’s where the vagueness of the synopsis becomes a problem—there’s no real sense of *why* Eldric is the one chosen for this. Is he a reluctant hero? A man who’s finally snapped under the weight of his own perfectionism? Or is this just another case of "the protagonist is the least likely candidate," a trope so overused it’s practically a meme? The synopsis doesn’t say. It doesn’t even hint at the mechanics of the Overworld—is it a sandbox? A linear quest? A Lovecraftian nightmare? The lack of specificity is frustrating, because *Overworld* sounds like it could be either a *Baldree*-esque romp or a *Sanderson*-style epic, but without more detail, it’s just a placeholder for whatever Wight wants it to be.

But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the beauty of *Overworld* is in its ambiguity, the way it invites readers to fill in the blanks with their own expectations. And if it’s anything like Wight’s previous work, it’ll likely deliver on the "addictive LitRPG" promise—meaning we’re talking about a book that’s as much about the *experience* of reading as it is about the story itself. The ten-day countdown is a classic trope, sure, but it’s also a brilliant way to force pacing and tension. It’s the literary equivalent of a video game’s "final boss" timer—you know the clock’s ticking, and that’s half the fun.

Yet—*lexical whiplash*—I can’t help but wonder if this isn’t just another example of the "chosen one" fatigue we’ve all developed after a decade of fantasy tropes. The "little girl who just might change the fate of them all" line reads like it could be ripped straight from a *Game of Thrones* synopsis, and while I love a good underdog story, I also love a story that doesn’t feel like it’s trying too hard to be *The Chosen One*. Where’s the subversion? Where’s the twist? The synopsis doesn’t say. And that’s the problem with relying on synopses alone—you’re left with the impression that *Overworld* is a book that could be *anything*, which is either a strength or a weakness, depending on how you look at it.

But here’s the thing: I’m *intrigued*. Because Wight has a knack for taking familiar tropes and making them feel fresh, even if it’s just through execution. And if *Overworld* delivers on its promise of being "addictive," then it’ll be the kind of book that lingers in your mind long after you’ve turned the last page. It’s the kind of thing that makes you want to lose yourself in a world where the rules are different, where the stakes are high, and where the protagonist—flawed, rigid, *human*—has to adapt or die.

And then, of course, there’s the fact that it’s being sold without DRM. That alone is enough to make me suspect this is a book that’s going to be *lived* in, not just read. It’s the kind of thing that’ll end up on Kindle Unlimited, passed around in Reddit threads, debated in fantasy forums. It’s the kind of book that’ll either be a cult hit or a fleeting trend, but either way, it’ll be talked about.

But now—*pivot*—I realize I’ve been sitting here too long, staring at my phone like some kind of literary groupie. My tailor’s waiting for me to try on this damn tuxedo for the awards ceremony, and I still haven’t decided if I’m going to wear the pocket square with the *Dune* quote or the one with the *Blade Runner* still. Either way, I’m going to need a drink after this. Maybe three.
