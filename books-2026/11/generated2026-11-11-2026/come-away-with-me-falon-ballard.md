**Author:** Falon Ballard  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Come Away with Me* reads like a love letter to the kind of rom-com that’s been recycled so often it’s starting to smell like a hotel shampoo sample left in a backpack. The premise—grandparents’ love story, forced proximity, European tour, fated mates—is the kind of setup that makes you wonder if Ballard’s editor handed her a checklist from a 2018 Hallmark movie and said, *"Here, make this."* The idea that Lake Walker, a woman who’s clearly never left her grandmother’s basement, is suddenly thrust into a whirlwind romance with an Italian man she’s never met before is so clichéd it borders on the cruel. It’s the literary equivalent of serving a kid a plate of mac and cheese and calling it gourmet.

But let’s talk about the *real* problem here: the synopsis doesn’t even *try* to hide how thin this plot is. It’s not just a love story—it’s a *love story about a love story*, which is like watching a movie about a movie about a movie. The grandparents’ romance is treated like a mythic event, something so grand it’s only mentioned in hushed tones, as if their love was the kind of thing that happens in *The Notebook* but with better lighting. And yet, the actual *meat* of the book—Lake and Luca’s relationship—feels like it’s being written by someone who’s never actually been in love, just in a really long airplane line. The synopsis doesn’t even bother to suggest how this romance might *feel* different from the grandparents’; it just assumes the reader will buy into the idea that two strangers will fall in love over a series of tourist traps.

The European setting is another red flag. Ballard’s clearly trying to sell this as some kind of *The Holiday*-esque escape, but without the actual depth of *The Holiday* or even the charm of *Eat Pray Love*. Europe in romance novels is usually either a place of enlightenment (see: *The Guernsey Literary and Potato Peel Pie Society*) or a backdrop for shallow hedonism (see: *The Sun Also Rises*, but make it *The Love Hypothesis*). Here, it’s just a series of stops on a bus tour, with the only real tension coming from Luca’s "family business" which, based on the synopsis, is either a crime syndicate or a very confusing side hustle involving olive oil. The fact that the synopsis doesn’t even *name* what his business is tells you everything you need to know: Ballard’s either too lazy to develop it or too afraid it’ll ruin the fairy tale.

And don’t even get me started on the ending. The synopsis drops the classic rom-com bait-and-switch: Lake has to go home, Luca’s too busy, so will they find a way back to each other? It’s the same old question, asked every time a romance novel tries to sell itself as "realistic." The answer is always no, because the whole point of these books is to make the reader believe in the impossible. But the way it’s phrased—*"Will they be able to find a way back to each other, or will they end up just like their grandparents did, all those years ago?"*—sounds like it was written by someone who’s never actually been in love, just in a really long Netflix binge. It’s the literary equivalent of a text message that says, *"So… we should probably talk about this… maybe?"*

The worst part? The synopsis doesn’t even *try* to make this feel like anything more than a formula. It’s not *Bridgerton*’s historical romance, not *Colleen Hoover*’s emotional whiplash, not even *Emily Henry*’s witty banter. It’s just… a book. And not even a good one. It’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if Ballard’s ever read anything outside her genre, or if she just thinks that throwing in a few European cities and a grandparent’s love story is enough to make it feel sophisticated.

Literally.
