God, another wet clump caught right on the toe of my boot. My neighbors probably think I have lost my mind, hauling damp maples into mountainous brown heaps while the light drains out of the sky at five o'clock. But the rake provides a rhythmic, violent kind of therapy that sitting indoors simply cannot touch. Especially when the mind is chewing on the peculiar premise of *Somewhere, Something Incredible*. Susan Meissner is carving out a niche for mid-century domestic friction, much like Beatriz Williams or Kristin Hannah do when they want to make suburban women weep into their coffee cups. 

### The Gravity of a Broken Promise

San Diego in 1969 is a brilliant crucible for a family drama. While the entire planet stares fixedly at television sets waiting for Neil Armstrong to bounce on basalt dust, Gwen is stuck on earth scrubbing the grease of familial obligation off her hands. Or rather, she is trying to manage an octogenarian mother who suddenly decides that chronological age is merely a suggestion. Head injuries in fiction always act as convenient narrative crowbars—forcing open locked doors of propriety and letting the drafts of wild, erratic impulses blow through dusty parlors. Yet I wonder if the synopsis leans too heavily on the quirky-elderly trope. Is Lucille’s sudden embrace of a ponytailed opportunist a genuinely poignant exploration of late-in-life autonomy, or is it just sitcom fodder dressed up in a vintage polyester jumpsuit? 

Actually, scratch that critique entirely. The emotional core here isn't the mother's sudden behavioral shift at all. It is about the suffocating weight of being the designated anchor in a household where everyone else’s sails are violently shredding in the wind. 

Speaking of shredding things, why do toddler animated shows insist on using the exact same screeching sound effect for every cartoon animal falling off a cliff? My youngest has watched the same thirty seconds of a beaver tumbling down a muddy hill seventy-four times this week, and I am fairly certain my frontal lobe is dissolving faster than these decomposing leaves under my fingernails. At least Susan Meissner is not writing about animated beavers. She is writing about the terrifying vertigo of watching your children grieve things you cannot fix, like an eighteen-year-old girl going silent because a friend didn't make it back from Vietnam. 

### The Art of Letting Go

And that generational juxtaposition is where this novel will likely live or die. Gwen has to let go of her mother, let go of her daughter's grief, and let go of the rigid promises she made to a dead father. That is a lot of letting go for one hundred and ninety-some days of Apollo missions. I just hope the prose avoids sinking into the saccharine quicksand that plagues so much historical women's fiction these days. We do not need a tidy epiphany under the glow of a color television set. We need the messy, jagged reality of people colliding in small, carpeted rooms. 

Wait. Was that a metallic crash from the side alley behind the garbage bins, or did a neighborhood raccoon finally manage to tear down the chicken wire I spent three hours securing last Sunday? Excuse me, I need to go drop this rake right now and investigate before the entire recycling bin goes airborne.
