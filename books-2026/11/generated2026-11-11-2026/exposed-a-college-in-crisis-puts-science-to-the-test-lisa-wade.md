Governments have fallen over less incompetence than what was displayed by university chancellors in the spring of twenty-twenty. They traded diplomacy for spreadsheet panic. And now, Lisa Wade has decided to remind us of the wreckage in *Exposed: A College in Crisis Puts Science to the Test*. 

One shudders at the premise. It reads less like an academic inquiry and more like the archival notes from the HMS Titanic, compiled while the musicians were still adjusting their bow ties. 

### The Hubris of the Slide Rule

For decades, we watched the technocrats dismantle the humanities with the cheerful ruthlessness of a demolition derby. They poured every endowment dollar into laboratories and hard sciences, convinced that a quadratic equation could solve human grief. Then an airborne pathogen arrived. And what happened? The brilliant minds running our most prestigious academies completely forgot that undergraduates are, by biological necessity, social animals. 

It is the equivalent of the 1980 Olympic hockey team trying to negotiate a disarmament treaty at Studio 54. 

Wade’s thesis—or what we can glean from the promotional material, since the text itself remains safely sequestered from us—focuses on this catastrophic oversight. The administrators built elaborate protocols for scrubbing doorknobs while entirely ignoring the relentless, hormone-driven imperative of late adolescence. Friendship, sex, and love do not submit to bureaucratic decree. Yet the faculty boards acted shocked when students behaved like, well, students. 

### The Anatomy of a Debacle

The fracturing of these elite campuses into warring ideological factions feels less like a sociological revelation and more like a poorly cast production of Lord of the Flies on an Ivy League lawn. Fraternities operated with impunity while marginalized communities absorbed the actual collateral damage. 

And why? Because the people in charge understood molecular biology, but possessed the geopolitical and sociological acumen of a golden retriever. 

We are promised insights into social solidarity and the redefinition of freedom. Big words. Heavy lifting. One wonders if the author actually believes universities are capable of introspection, or if this text is merely a post-mortem for an institution that died of its own self-regard long before the virus ever landed. 

The synopsis leaves us stranded in the wreckage. It diagnoses the rot—the overinvestment in profitable STEM fields at the expense of sociological reasoning—without offering a single usable antidote for the next crisis. But perhaps asking an academic to fix the ivory tower is like asking the architect of the Hindenburg to review the weather report. 

I must stop writing this immediately. I am entirely unprepared to host the neighborhood playdate.
