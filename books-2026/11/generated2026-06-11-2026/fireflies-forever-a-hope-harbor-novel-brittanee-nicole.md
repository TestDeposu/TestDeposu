Listen closely, little ones, because this reminds me of the old tales we whisper around the hearth when the winter wind howls against the panes—only instead of gods hurling thunderbolts on Mount Olympus, we have a small-town dating auction run by meddling old cyprians called the Liberty Ladies. Brittanee Nicole is spinning another yarn in *Fireflies & Forever*, a modern myth where a single mother named Billie Walker is thrust onto the sacrificial block, forced to bid on her brother’s former hockey-playing best friend, Eli Davis, in a plot contrivance so ancient it probably smells like damp parchment and stale mead. 

Markets for human affection are as old as Babylon, yet somehow contemporary romance authors treat a charity auction like a revolutionary narrative device rather than the dusty trope it truly is. 

Wait. Actually, perhaps the forced proximity angle redeems the whole enterprise. 

Or perhaps not. 

The synopsis tells us—with all the breathless urgency of a village crier announcing a plague—that Eli is supposed to help Billie find herself outside of motherhood, which sounds suspiciously like unpaid emotional labor masquerading as a sweet contemporary romance akin to something churned out during a peak Emily Henry paper-mill summer. Zeus himself never dreamed of punishments as clever as five mandatory dates overseen by town busybodies armed with matchmaker clipboards and hearts of gold. Intertwined fingers at the Daffodil Festival. Sweet kisses under the glow of insects. It is all terribly predictable, yet the grandchildren of today will undoubtedly devour these pages just as our ancestors devoured tales of mortals defying the gods for a stolen glimpse of forbidden passion. 

Expectations. 

Dust. 

We watch mortals stumble into traps of their own making, binding themselves with invisible threads of obligation and proximity, pretending they did not walk willingly into the labyrinth. Nicole knows the recipe for suburban escapism—take one brooding athlete who learned kindness at a single mother's knee, mix with a heroine harboring an identity crisis, and bake until golden brown under the neon lights of a festival. But do we care about the fireflies? Or do we merely crave the inevitable reconciliation when the angry brother finally discovers his sacred code was violated over a plate of funnel cake? 

I must rush down to the smoky basement to hear a friend's experimental jazz quintet play a seventeen-minute cover of *Giant Steps*.
