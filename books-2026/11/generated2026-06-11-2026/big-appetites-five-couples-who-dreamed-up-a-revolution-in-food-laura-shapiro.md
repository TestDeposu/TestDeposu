The synopsis for *Big Appetites* is so broad it could be describing a food blog or a late-night cooking show—except Shapiro’s name is already a guarantee of something sharper. Five couples, a revolution, thirty-minute recipes. It sounds like the kind of book that would make you want to pull out your own family’s recipe cards and interrogate them like they were historical documents. But here’s the problem: the synopsis doesn’t tell us *why* these couples matter beyond the vague promise of "transformed food culture." Did they fight for local farms? Did they invent the slow-food movement before it was cool? Or are we just getting a very polished origin story for the modern meal-prep aesthetic?

The real question is whether Shapiro will resist the temptation to turn this into a feel-good origin myth. Because food history often gets reduced to nostalgia—think of how *Salt Fat Acid Heat* made us all want to cry over our grandmothers’ kitchens—when the actual work of revolution is messy, ideological, and sometimes just plain stubborn. The synopsis mentions "sustainable agriculture" and "farmers markets," but it doesn’t say whether these couples were idealists, hustlers, or just really good at networking. Were they the kind of people who could turn a backyard garden into a political statement, or were they more like the couples in *The Taste of Country Life*, where the revolution was less about ideology and more about the sheer joy of eating well? The synopsis doesn’t tell us.

And then there’s the issue of *couples*—plural. Five of them. It’s a trope now, isn’t it? The idea that food movements are always collaborative, always romantic, always *together*. But what if the real story is about the ones who got left behind? The ones who didn’t have a partner to share the burden? The ones who had to do it alone, like the women in *The Jungle* but with better knives. The synopsis doesn’t even hint at that kind of friction.

But let’s be honest—this is Shapiro we’re talking about. She’s not going to write a dry academic treatise. She’ll make you smell the garlic, feel the weight of the cast-iron skillet, hear the arguments over who forgot to take out the trash. She’ll probably name-drop some of the usual suspects—Michael Pollan, maybe, or Barbara Kingsolver—but she’ll also dig up the lesser-known figures, the ones who got lost in the shuffle. The ones who didn’t just dream up a revolution but had to *live* it, day after day, recipe after recipe.

Yet the synopsis is so vague it could apply to any number of books. It could be about the couples who started Whole Foods. It could be about the ones who ran the first CSA programs. It could even be about the couples who just really liked to cook and happened to live through the 1970s. The lack of specificity is frustrating, but then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the book is about how food revolutions start not with grand declarations but with small, stubborn acts—like chopping an onion or arguing over the salt.

But here’s what I really want to know: will Shapiro make us care about these people? Will she make us *see* them, beyond the postcard images of farmers’ markets and perfectly arranged plates? Or will this just be another book that makes us feel good about eating local while ignoring the labor that made it possible?

I need to put up the holiday decorations before my spouse starts complaining about the state of the kitchen. Again.
