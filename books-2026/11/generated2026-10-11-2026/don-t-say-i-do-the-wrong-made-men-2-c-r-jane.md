**Author:** C.R. Jane  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Don’t Say I Do*—this *curated chaos* of music anecdotes—isn’t a book so much as a *mood board left on a bar stool*, the kind of thing you’d find scribbled in the margins of a jazz club’s ledger. It’s the literary equivalent of a vinyl record with a scratch on side two, where the first half is *there*, but the second half is just *potential*. You can hear the energy, the *grit*, the way these stories are meant to be *passed around like a mic stand*—but the execution? Well, that’s where the real question lies.

The first anecdote about Black Flag and Henry Rollins is *gold*, isn’t it? That moment where Chuck Dukowski—*the real MVP here*—tells Rollins to stop whining and just *play his ass off*? It’s the kind of raw, unfiltered wisdom that makes you want to high-five the page. But then you read the rest of the synopsis, and it’s like the author *lost the thread*. One minute you’re in the *gritty, no-BS world of punk*, the next you’re in *some kind of avant-garde jazz seminar* with David Amram and Miles Davis. And then—*oh, then*—you get that Leonard Bernstein family language thing, which is either *brilliant* or *painfully pretentious*, depending on whether you’re feeling charitable. It’s like the author *forgot to proofread their own vibe*.

The problem isn’t that these stories are *bad*—it’s that they’re *too scattered*. You don’t get the sense of a *narrative arc*, not even a *thematic spine*. It’s not *The Art of Fielding*, where every anecdote feels like a puzzle piece in a larger portrait of ambition and failure. It’s not even *The Secret History*, where the digressions *serve* the themes. This feels more like *someone’s Spotify Wrapped*, where the algorithm just dumped a bunch of random playlists into a folder labeled “Music Stories” and called it a day. And yet—*yet*—there’s something *undeniably* compelling about the raw material. The way Rollins talks about *keeping your blood clean and your mind sharp*? That’s the kind of *anti-self-help* philosophy that could’ve been *so* good if it had a backbone.

But then—*here’s the pivot*—maybe that’s the point. Maybe *Don’t Say I Do* isn’t meant to be a *book* at all, but a *conversation starter*, the kind of thing you’d hand to a musician in a dive bar and say, *“Read this and tell me if I’m full of shit.”* The Bernstein anecdote, for instance—*Rybernian*—is the kind of thing that would *actually* make you laugh out loud if you were sitting across from a composer who’d just spent three hours explaining why his daughter’s mispronunciations are *genius*. It’s the literary equivalent of *someone’s weirdest family story*, the kind that gets passed down because it’s *too specific to be boring*.

The flexidisc story? *Chef’s kiss.* That’s the kind of *meta* humor that makes you think, *“Oh, this author actually gets it.”* But then you realize—*no, they don’t*. Because if they did, they’d have *structured* it better. They’d have *woven* it into something. Instead, it’s just *there*, like a loose thread on a coat you’re not sure you’ll ever wear.

I can already predict this book will be *loved by the right people*—the ones who see it as *fodder for deep dives*, the kind of thing you’d use to fuel a podcast episode or a late-night rant on Twitter. But for the rest of us? It’s *a little too much like a scrapbook*. And scrapbooks, no matter how charming, are *not* literature.
