**Author:** James Bennett  
**Page Count:** Unknown

The year is 1192 AD, or so the marketing copy insists. Another medieval romp through the European mud, complete with shipwrecked knights, vengeful men, and ancient terrors lurking in the Alpine crags. Because what contemporary publishing desperately needs is yet another grimdark exercise in frostbite and theological anxiety. We are told to brace ourselves for something akin to the bleak misery of *The Terror* fused with the apocalyptic fervor of *Between Two Fires*. Such comparisons are always a gamble. Usually, they are written by publicists who have never opened a manuscript in their lives. 

### The Geography of Cliche

Jean, the mandatory teller of tales, supposedly carries the weight of a legendary king and a priceless relic on his weary shoulders. How thrilling. Another doomed pilgrimage through the snow. This smells entirely of the Costa Concordia disaster—a slow-motion maritime catastrophe where nobody looks good in hindsight. Bennett leans heavily into the trope of the loyal servant willing to walk into hell itself for a master. Riveting stuff if you happen to enjoy the literary equivalent of stale parchment. 

### Faith, Frost, and Fatigue

The thematic architecture relies entirely on the worn-out dichotomy between the Christian God and things older and darker. Haven't we exhausted this particular quarry? Every modern medievalist believes that throwing pagan monsters into a twelfth-century snowdrift constitutes high art. Jean wrestles with suspicion. Men fall victim one by one. The predictable rhythm of a slaughterhouse dressed up in religious conflict. Yet the prose demands silence and reverence from us. 

### The Precious Cargo

Let us look at the stakes. A king falls. A relic breaks. A servant weeps. It is the literary equivalent of a third-rate opera at La Scala where the tenor misses every high C and the audience pretends to weep out of sheer exhaustion. Bennett wants us breathless. Instead, we are merely glancing at our watches, wondering if the manuscript will ever find its footing beneath the weight of its own self-importance. 

Expectations remain subterranean. The Alps have seen better corpses.
