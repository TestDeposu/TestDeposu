**Author:** Karabi George  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

November winds rattle the balcony glass, carrying the distinct scent of decaying foliage and damp wool from the street below. Yet my irritation has less to do with the seasonal dampness and everything to do with Karabi George’s forthcoming debut, *Not a Good Mother*. We are expected to swoon over yet another manuscript trading in the most exhausted domestic suspense clichés available in the marketplace. Another frantic mother. Another missing child. Another algorithmic thriller engineered for commuters who consume paperbacks like fast food at an airport terminal. 

### The Geography of Paranoia

Displacement is a recurring currency in modern fiction. Put an isolated woman in a foreign country, strip away her support system, and watch the narrative machinery grind into motion. The synopsis leans heavily on this tired architecture. Reema takes her infant to a park, meets an online acquaintance named Elizabeth, and vanishes for four minutes—just long enough for a baby to evaporate into thin air. 

One can practically smell the ghostwriter's coffee growing cold over a Zoom plotting session. 

### The Weaponized Gaze

The true horror in these pages is never the missing infant. It is the immediate, casual cruelty of societal surveillance. The police arrive at 4:45 PM—because apparently, bureaucracy runs on strict scheduling in this fictional universe—and immediately pivot their suspicion toward the grieving mother. Her husband, his family, the neighbors, the media. They all close ranks. They all diagnose her failure with the chilling efficiency of a board meeting at Davos. 

This brings to mind the disastrous corporate optics of the 2017 United Airlines passenger dragging incident. Public shaming masquerading as moral order. 

### A Predictable Descent

By Saturday afternoon, Reema takes matters into her own hands. Of course she does. Because no contemporary protagonist is allowed to simply sit in their despair; they must become amateur sleuths, leaping across logical chasms to prove their worth to a society that already regards them with contempt. George’s premise asks how much we can trust the people closest to us. A profoundly unoriginal question. We get it. Nobody is safe, suburban lawns conceal bodies, and online friends are invariably syndicate operatives or unhinged sociopaths. 

Give us texture. Give us geopolitical friction. Give us prose that does not read like a treatment for a straight-to-streaming adaptation. Instead, we are handed a relentless, pulse-pounding checklist of paranoia designed to trigger the anxiety glands of the bourgeoisie between flights to Geneva. 

The prose promises a high-velocity investigation into secrets and lies. Yet the architecture of the setup feels as transparent as a glass pavilion in a tax haven. We will read it anyway, won't we? We will consume the panic, close the back cover, and stare blankly out the window at the gray November dusk, wondering why our contemporary literary diet consists almost entirely of manufactured hysteria.

Before I can draft a formal rejection of this aesthetic decline, I must go outside and untangle the garden hose that has tied itself into a Gordian knot across the frost-bitten patio.
