**Author:** Annabel Paulsen  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The rain outside taps against the window like a metronome counting down to something—maybe the end of the season, maybe the end of a band. *Creative Differences* arrives just as the air turns brittle, the kind of crisp that makes you inhale too deeply and realize how much you’ve been holding back. Five years is a long time to bury a kiss, but five years is also the exact amount of time it takes for a hit song to become a cultural earworm, for a lead singer to forget what it felt like to be part of something smaller than herself. The synopsis doesn’t spell it out, but the tension between Amelia’s sold-out stadiums and Ramona’s radio station slot is the kind of friction that could either ignite or collapse under its own weight.

The framing of this as a "second-chance romance" is a double-edged sword—it’s the kind of trope that either lands with the precision of a well-placed guitar solo or flops like a band that forgot to tune up. The comparisons to *Here We Go Again* and *Daisy Jones & The Six* are telling. Both books thrive on the alchemy of nostalgia and regret, but where *Daisy Jones* leans into the chaos of creation, *Creative Differences* seems to be playing it safer, trading in the raw, unscripted energy of a band’s implosion for the more contained, if no less explosive, chemistry of a reunion. And yet—here’s where the synopsis betrays itself—it’s not just about the reunion. It’s about the lie they all lived with. Bea’s ignorance of the kiss that unraveled Swanboat is the book’s most interesting structural choice, a ghost at the feast that no one can quite name. The question isn’t whether they’ll get back together—it’s whether they’ll ever be honest about why they broke apart in the first place.

The pacing here is the kind of thing that makes you wonder if the author’s ever actually been in a band. Five years is a long time to avoid a song, but Ramona’s radio station slot feels like a detail plucked from a checklist rather than a moment of real tension. And Amelia’s transition into pop? It’s the kind of career pivot that could either feel like a betrayal or a liberation, but the synopsis doesn’t give us enough to tell which. The danger is that this will read like a love story where the real conflict is the music industry’s version of emotional labor—Amelia’s fame is a cage, Ramona’s obscurity is a prison, and Bea’s silence is the only thing keeping them all from falling apart. But if the writing has the guts to lean into the absurdity of it all—like the way a band that once defined intimacy now has to perform it onstage—then maybe it’ll avoid becoming just another sapphic reunion dramedy.

My eyes rolled so hard they hurt when I read "confessional-indie rock trio." That’s a mouthful of genre signifiers that could either feel like a cozy blanket or a straitjacket, depending on how the prose lands. The synopsis doesn’t give us a single line of dialogue, no snippets of lyrics, no description of a setlist that could ground us in the world. It’s all potential, which is both thrilling and infuriating. The real test will be whether the book can make us care about the songs they’re playing as much as the people playing them. Because if this is going to work, it needs to feel like a love letter to music itself—not just the romance, but the art.

And then there’s the scotch. Fifteen years old, because that’s how long it takes for a good lie to start tasting like the truth. I’ll pour a glass before I read it, just to see if the book can hold up under the weight of its own expectations. Or if it’ll crumble like a vinyl record left in the rain.
