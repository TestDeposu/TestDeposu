**Author:** Sarah A. Bailey  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The workshop’s name alone—*The Path of the Wild Woman*—sounds like the kind of thing that would make a freelance editor’s spine twitch. Not because it’s bad, but because it’s *so* deliberately vague. It’s the literary equivalent of a yoga studio promising "deep transformation" without ever specifying what that transformation might look like beyond a vague, ritualistic glow. And yet—here we are, in 2026, where the market for self-help-adjacent fiction is as crowded as a subway car at rush hour, and *Celestia Cove* has already carved out a niche for itself. The first two books in the series, if the synopsis of this third installment is any indication, were likely the kind of thing that made readers feel like they’d just finished a spiritual retreat they’d paid for but never actually attended. The language here is all *unearth* and *untether*, which is to say: it’s the kind of prose that would make a minimalist poet like Ocean Vuong roll their eyes, but it’s also the kind of prose that would make a certain subset of readers—those who still believe in the alchemy of a well-timed workshop—lean in.

The theatrical costume protocol is the most interesting part of this synopsis, not because it’s a plot device—it isn’t—but because it’s a meta-commentary on the act of reading itself. The editor persona is a mask, and the reader is being asked to wear it too. But here’s the thing: the mask fits poorly. The synopsis doesn’t tell us what the workshop is *for*, only that it’s a space for "exploration" and "giving yourself the gift." That’s the problem with this kind of writing—it’s all gift and no ask. It’s the difference between *The Bell Jar* and a Hallmark card. You don’t need to be told that you’re supposed to feel something; you need to be shown why you should care. And yet, the synopsis doesn’t even attempt to show. It’s all implication, all atmosphere, all the things that make a reader wonder if they’re missing something—or if the author is just too afraid to say what they mean.

But then—*the pivot*—maybe that’s the point. Maybe the vagueness is intentional, a reflection of the modern reader’s own fragmented attention span. The synopsis doesn’t promise a clear narrative; it promises a *path*, which is to say: it promises the illusion of choice. And in a world where algorithms curate our desires, where we’re constantly being told what to want, the idea of untethering your "inner Howl" is both radical and exhausting. It’s the kind of thing that would make a reader of *Her Body and Other Parties* by Carmen Maria Machado nod in recognition—because the body, the self, the desire—these are all things that are constantly being rewritten, repackaged, sold back to us. But where Machado’s work is sharp, precise, *Celestia Cove* feels like it’s trying to be everything to everyone, which is why the synopsis is so frustratingly empty. It’s the literary equivalent of a wellness influencer’s Instagram caption: all vibes, no substance.

The thematic potential here is undeniable. The idea of the "Wild Woman" is a trope that’s been mined for decades—think *The Handmaid’s Tale*’s rebellion, *The Vegetarian*’s unraveling, even *Normal People*’s quiet, gnawing desire. But the synopsis doesn’t give us anything to latch onto. Is this a story about self-discovery? About the performativity of desire? About the ways in which we’re all just freelancers in our own lives, cutting the fluff from our own narratives? Or is it just another entry in the self-help fiction genre, where the real work is done by the reader, not the author? The answer, as always, is probably a little of everything—and that’s what makes it so frustrating. Because if this is supposed to be a *novel*, then it needs to do more than promise a path. It needs to show us the terrain.

And yet—here’s the thing—maybe that’s the genius of it. Maybe the vagueness is the point. Maybe the reader is supposed to fill in the blanks, to project their own desires onto the page. That’s the kind of thing that would make a reader of *The Stranger* by Albert Camus pause, because it’s the same kind of existential question: what does it mean to live authentically in a world that’s constantly trying to sell you authenticity? The synopsis doesn’t answer that question. It doesn’t need to. It just leaves you with the feeling that you’re standing at the edge of something, that you’re about to howl—but you’re not sure if it’s because you’re free or because you’re lost.

The subway noise is penetrating my noise-canceling headphones today, and I can’t decide if that’s a metaphor for the book or just a sign that I need to stop writing about it and go home.
