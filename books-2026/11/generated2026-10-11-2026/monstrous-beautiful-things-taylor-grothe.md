**Author:** Taylor Grothe  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-08-18 APL

Sunlight is cutting through the grease-filmed windowpanes of this kitchenette right now, hitting the chipped Formica counter just right, making everything look less like an indoor cave and more like a space where good things might actually happen for once. The rent check cleared yesterday, the radiator isn't clanking its usual death rattle, and Taylor Grothe’s upcoming *Monstrous Beautiful Things* has somehow landed on the pile of galleys threatening to avalanche off the wobbly side table. Or wait. Is it called *The Sleeping Sisters* in the text? The synopsis can’t seem to decide on its own damn title. Who approved this? The marketing department is clearly spinning a wheel in the dark. 

Still, the premise hits a nerve. Fortuna Miércoles fleeing a bloodline curse across the Rio Grande—it sounds drenched in the kind of suffocating atmosphere that Carmen Maria Machado or Stephen Graham Jones wields like a switchblade. But let us be entirely serious for a moment. The blurb tosses around terms like feral hymn and cactus thorn with the enthusiastic abandon of a debut writer trying to out-poet the entire southwestern gothic canon. I usually despise this brand of overwrought, myth-soaked marketing hyperbole. Actually, scratch that—I adore it when it bites. There is something intoxicating about a narrative leaning so heavily into Chicana-Indigenous folklore that the page practically bleeds red dust and heatstroke. 

The thematic core here isn't merely about a cold case or a dormant volcano waking up beneath the desert. It is about inheritance. The way violence gets baked into the marrow of a family tree until the women at the trunk have to decide whether to prune the branches or set the whole orchard on fire. Detective Jeanette Palacio hunting ghosts alongside the memory of unavenged cousins offers a brilliant, bruised counterweight to Fortuna’s maternal desperation. Two women tethered to the same arid landscape, both trapped in different cages of history and expectation. 

Grothe is carving out a messy, visceral space for horror that refuses to sanitize the domestic sphere. Motherhood as a monstrous bargain. We have seen this thematic well drawn from before—Nat Cassidy and T. Kingfisher both circle these dark waters—yet the specific geography of Albuquerque mesas and dormant peaks gives this particular telling a sharp, distinct bite. Whether the prose can sustain the fever-dream intensity promised by the blurbs remains to be seen. Advance praise is notoriously bought and paid for with mutual praise society nods. Yet the bones of the thing feel sturdy. Sharp. Ready to draw blood.

I need to drag myself away from the stack of proofs and hunt through the couch cushions for enough quarters to drag a heavy basket of damp laundry down the cracked concrete stairs to the basement laundromat before the evening rush starts.
