Stare at the blank parchment long enough and the fading November light outside the studio window begins to curdle into something resembling malice. The radiator hisses—a dry, metallic wheeze that mirrors my current cranial vacancy. Staring down Frank Scozzari’s upcoming 2006—no, 2026—curiosity piece, *An Orphan of the Light*, feels remarkably like appraising a forged Rothko. You spot the seams immediately, yet you keep squinting, desperate for some hidden stroke of genius. 

### The Architecture of Despair

Los Angeles noir has a particular stench. It smells of ozone, cheap gin, and the rotting corpses of ambitions left to bake under the San Fernando sun. Scozzari drags us back to 2003, dredging up a 1979 cold case that mirrors the infamous 1932 demise of Peg Entwistle. And yet, this chronological Russian roulette—bouncing across 1932, 1979, and 2003—threatens to become an absolute structural mess if the prose lacks teeth. But who am I kidding? The framing device of a cynical LAPD detective obsessed with a dead starlet screams James Ellroy fan-fiction written after one too many martinis at Musso & Frank. 

Let us pause for a recalibration. Actually, scratch that entire critique; the temporal stacking is precisely where the aesthetic genius might actually lie, provided the author avoids standard procedural banalities. 

### Doppelgängers and Dead Ends

Detective Garrett Connelly finds Moira, a waitress bearing a striking resemblance to the deceased Nora Sims. How delightfully pedestrian. We are wading knee-deep into pulp tropes here—the haunted facsimile, the doom-laden romance, the familial curse. It's giving major mid-tier James Ellroy mixed with *Mulholland Drive* fan-cam energy, yet somehow deeply mid. Moira harbors secrets. Connelly harbors an obsession. They unearth the past. Then—plot twist—she goes on a revenge bender. 

The synopsis telegraphs the third act with the subtlety of a neon sign crashing onto Hollywood Boulevard. Yet, the core anatomy of ambition weaponized against the fragile human psyche remains an evergreen carcass to pick clean. If Scozzari anchors the prose in tangible sensory rot—the greasy linoleum of the diner, the sickly sweet scent of decaying magnolias in Laurel Canyon—the predictability of the vendetta might actually slap. Or it might completely bomb, serving up stale tropes dressed in vintage attire. 

My private flight to London has been delayed by an hour.
