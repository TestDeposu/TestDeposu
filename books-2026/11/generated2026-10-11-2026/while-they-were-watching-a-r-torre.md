**Author:** A. R. Torre  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis of *While They Were Watching* is a masterclass in how not to sell a book—because it’s not selling anything at all. It’s just *One of the Boys* in a different state, with the same hollow, self-congratulatory blurbs that read like a committee of exhausted therapists trying to make a family drama sound like a *Little Life* knockoff. The problem isn’t that the premise is weak—it’s that the synopsis itself is a betrayal of the reader’s trust. Where’s the tension? Where’s the *edge*? Instead, we get a slow-motion car crash of a family dynamic, described with the same clinical detachment as a *National Geographic* special on urban decay.

The brothers’ journey—driving through the night, joining basketball teams, making friends—is framed as a triumph of resilience, but it’s really just a series of set pieces waiting for the father to unravel. And unravel he will, of course, because this is the kind of story where the father’s descent is the only thing keeping the reader from dozing off. The synopsis doesn’t even bother to hint at the *specificity* of the abuse, just the *vibe*: "the late night noises, the comings and goings of increasingly odd characters." Odd? Really? That’s the most interesting thing about this family? The father’s erratic behavior is treated like a plot device rather than a psychological pressure cooker, which is why the comparison to *A Little Life* feels like a stretch—unless the author thinks readers will mistake this for a literary masterpiece instead of a cautionary tale about how not to write about trauma.

But then—*and this is where the synopsis fails entirely*—there’s the sibling bond. Oh, the sibling bond. The one thing this story seems to exist for. The brothers’ devotion is described with the same saccharine earnestness as a Hallmark movie, as if their love for a man who’s clearly losing his mind is some kind of noble endurance test. It’s not. It’s a trap. The synopsis doesn’t ask: *What does this love cost them?* It just assumes the reader will be moved by it, which is why the blurbs keep circling back to "tender" and "beautiful." But tenderness isn’t a selling point—it’s a cop-out. If this were actually a gripping story, it would make the reader *uncomfortable*, not comfort them with the idea that family is always worth saving.

And yet—*here’s the pivot*—there’s something undeniably compelling in the way the synopsis avoids specificity. It’s not that it’s vague; it’s that it’s *deliberately* abstract, as if the author knows the real horror isn’t in the details but in the *implication*. The father’s "dead-eyed absentmindedness," the "cheap cigars to hide another smell"—these are the kinds of lines that make you wonder if the book will ever rise above the level of a *True Detective* episode’s first act. But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the whole thing is just a slow burn, a *Waiting for Godot* for suburban New Mexico, where the real tragedy isn’t the father’s abuse but the fact that no one in this story ever *does* anything about it.

The comparison to *We the Animals* is the most insulting of all, because *We the Animals* had *rage*. It had *movement*. It had a sense that the boys’ suffering was part of something larger, something that could be fought. This synopsis suggests a story where the boys are just… watching. Waiting. Like they’re in some kind of psychological purgatory, where the only thing that matters is the father’s slow unraveling. And the worst part? The synopsis doesn’t even *care* if you’re bored. It just assumes you’ll be moved by the *idea* of it.

But then—*the pop-culture drop*—there’s the way the synopsis describes the father’s behavior as "increasingly odd." Odd? Really? That’s the word they’re going with? Because if this were actually a story about a man losing his grip on reality, it wouldn’t be described as "odd." It would be described as *the kind of thing that happens in the final act of a David Fincher film*, where the father’s descent isn’t just a plot point but a *visual spectacle*, a slow-motion train wreck that leaves the audience breathless. Instead, we get "odd," which is the literary equivalent of saying "it was a little bit scary" after watching *The Exorcist*.

The real question is: *Does this book have the guts to be as brutal as it needs to be?* Or is it just another in a long line of stories about damaged families that mistake pathos for depth? The synopsis doesn’t tell us. It just leaves us with the sinking feeling that we’re about to read a book that’s more interested in *feeling* good than in *hurting* us.

And now, as my driver swerves to avoid a stalled Tesla in the Hamptons traffic, I realize I’ve spent longer thinking about this than I have reading anything in months. Which, I suppose, is the point. The book hasn’t even been published yet, and it’s already winning. Not because it’s good, but because it’s *safe*. Because it’s the kind of story that makes you feel like you’re *participating* in someone else’s pain, rather than being *confronted* by it.
