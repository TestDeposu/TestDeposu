**Author:** Alex Marzano-Lesnevich  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The subway’s fluorescent lights buzz like a dying insect, casting everything in that sickly yellow glow that makes you want to scream. You’re supposed to be reading *Both and Neither*—Alex Marzano-Lesnevich’s new book, which sounds like it’s going to be that rare thing: a memoir that doesn’t just tell you about history but *haunts* you with it. The synopsis is all sharp edges and implied weight, like holding a knife by the handle and wondering when you’ll finally get cut. Four lives—Joseph Lobdell, Gerd Katter, Pauli Murray, Claude Cahun—each a ghost in the machine of gender, each a name that should’ve been burned into the archives but got buried under the weight of erasure. And then there’s the author, weaving their own story into the fray, which is either brave or reckless, depending on how much you trust the person holding the scalpel.

The problem with this synopsis is that it’s *too* neat. It’s like someone took a beautiful, messy tapestry and ironed it flat—now it’s just a series of portraits, each one framed and polished, but none of them quite breathing. Where’s the dirt? Where’s the sweat? Where’s the moment when the author realizes they’re not just writing about these people but *through* them, like a ghost story where the narrator keeps forgetting they’re the one who’s supposed to be dead? The Whiting Grant suggests this isn’t just another academic exercise—it’s supposed to be *alive*, but the synopsis reads like a museum exhibit description. You can almost hear the curator’s voice: *"And now, ladies and gentlemen, let us examine the trans body in history!"* Where’s the ache? Where’s the part where the author stumbles over their own words because the past is too heavy?

Marzano-Lesnevich’s last book, *The Fact of a Body*, was a slow-burn thriller of legal horror, the kind that made you forget you were reading nonfiction until the last page. This one sounds like it’s going to be something else entirely—less courtroom drama, more *haunting*. The title itself is a lie, or at least a provocation: *Both and Neither*. It’s not a binary, not a choice, not even a spectrum. It’s the space *between* things, the liminal, the in-between that gets erased when you try to pin it down. And yet, the synopsis treats it like a checklist—here’s four people, here’s some history, here’s the author’s personal stuff. But the best books about gender don’t just *tell* you about the past; they make you *feel* it, like the air in a room where someone just died. Where’s the moment when the author realizes they’re not just a witness but a participant? Where’s the part where the history starts bleeding into the present, and you can’t tell which one is the ghost anymore?

The cast is intriguing—Lobdell, the trans man locked up for loving a woman; Katter, the carpenter who got castrated by the Nazis; Murray, the legal genius who fought for both civil rights and feminism; Cahun, the artist who wrote *"I am myself. I am no one."*—but the synopsis doesn’t do them justice. It’s like being handed a menu where every dish is described as *"delicious"* and *"fresh"* and *"perfectly balanced."* You want the juicy details, the parts where the author gets messy, where they admit they don’t have all the answers, where they let the reader in on the fact that history isn’t just something you study—it’s something that *studies you back*. Where’s the part where the author says, *"I don’t know how to write this, but I have to try anyway"*?

And then there’s the subtitle: *Or, A Haunting*. That’s the clue. The best books about gender aren’t just about facts—they’re about *presence*. They’re about the way a name lingers in the air, the way a body occupies space even when it’s been erased. *The Lesbian Historians* by Sarah Schulman did this—made you feel the weight of the past like a physical thing. *Citizen* by Claudia Rankine did it with language, turning history into something that *hits* you in the chest. This book sounds like it’s going to do the same, but the synopsis is too clean. It’s like someone took a photograph of a crime scene and called it *"art."* You want the blood. You want the mess. You want the part where the author realizes they’re not just writing about these people—they’re *haunted* by them.

But here you are, stuck on a subway, trying to write this review because someone paid you to do it, and the book hasn’t even come out yet. The synopsis is all promise and no payoff, like a movie trailer that shows you the best parts but never lets you know what the actual story is. You can already tell it’s going to be good—because Marzano-Lesnevich’s work has a way of making you feel like you’re standing at the edge of a cliff, looking down at something you can’t quite name. But you also know it’s going to be *hard*. Not in the way that’s easy to digest, but in the way that stays with you, like a song you can’t get out of your head. And that’s the thing about good books—they don’t just tell you something. They *change* you.
