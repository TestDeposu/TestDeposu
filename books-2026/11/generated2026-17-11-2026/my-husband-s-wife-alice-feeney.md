**Author:** Alice Feeney  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-01-20 APL

The synopsis for *My Husband’s Wife* reads like a fever dream someone scribbled down after three espressos and a particularly vivid nightmare—equal parts *Gone Girl*’s structural audacity and *The Guest List*’s gothic dread, but with the emotional temperature cranked to "simmering paranoia." The problem? It’s so deliberately opaque it might as well be a Rorschach test for genre expectations. One house. One husband. Two women. Someone is lying. But which someone? The artist? The reclusive Londoner? The husband, who seems to have inherited a goddamn *Twilight Zone* episode? The synopsis doesn’t tell you—it just *hints*, like a used car salesman who won’t let you test-drive the plot.

The dual timelines are the book’s only real selling point, and even then, they’re executed with the subtlety of a sledgehammer. Six months earlier, Birdy inherits a house, stumbles upon a death-predicting clinic, and decides to "right old wrongs." Now? Eden Fox, an artist on the brink of her big break, returns to find her key doesn’t fit and her husband insists she’s mistaken. The setup is so clichéd it’s almost charming—if it weren’t for the way Feeney seems to think readers will suspend disbelief by sheer force of will. This isn’t *The Girl on the Train*; it’s *The Girl Who Mistook Her Husband for a Villain*, and the synopsis doesn’t even bother to sell the villainy. It just *assumes* we’ll buy it.

Yet—and here’s where the pivot happens—there’s a sliver of something interesting buried under the layers of "wife swap" fatigue. The London clinic angle suggests a deeper psychological unraveling, something closer to *The End of the Affair*’s existential dread than *The Husband’s Secret*’s domestic whodunit. But the synopsis doesn’t *show* this; it just *tells* us Birdy feels compelled to "right old wrongs," which is such a broad, undercooked emotional beat it might as well be a placeholder for whatever Feeney’s therapy notes said last week. The real tension should come from the *why*—why does Birdy care? Why does Eden’s key not fit? But the synopsis doesn’t give a damn. It just drops us into the chaos like we’re supposed to *feel* the mystery, not analyze it.

The thematic potential here is undeniable—identity, inheritance, the fragility of perception—but the execution feels like it’s been rushed through a blender. Feeney’s strength in *His & Hers* was her ability to weave psychological tension with a light, almost breezy touch. Here, the tone swings between gothic menace and sitcom-level absurdity, like a writer who’s read too many *New York Times* bestseller lists and decided to check all the boxes without ever committing to a single mood. The result? A book that feels like it’s been written by committee—part *Big Little Lies*, part *The Secret History*, but with none of the depth or originality of either.

And yet—*and yet*—there’s a reason this is being hyped as the next *New York Times* bestseller. The premise is *perfect* for our current obsession with "twist-heavy" thrillers, where the real star is the reader’s ability to out-guess the author. But the synopsis doesn’t even *attempt* to sell the twist; it just *dangles* it like a carrot on a stick. It’s the literary equivalent of a Netflix trailer that cuts to black after the first act. We’re supposed to *believe* in this, but the synopsis doesn’t give us a damn reason to.

I need to review a tedious film adaptation contract before I can even consider whether this will hold up under scrutiny. The synopsis is so vague it might as well be a placeholder for whatever Feeney’s actual story is—because right now, it’s just a series of questions with no answers, and that’s not how you sell a book. It’s how you sell a *mystery*, and even then, only if you’re willing to gamble that the reader will fill in the blanks with their own paranoia.
