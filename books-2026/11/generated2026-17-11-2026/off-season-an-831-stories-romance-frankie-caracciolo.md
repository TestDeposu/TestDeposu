**Page Count:** Unknown

One must applaud the sheer audacity of lowering one’s gaze from the peerage registries to examine the sporting class. *Off Season* arrives courtesy of Frankie Caracciolo with a premise as predictable as the third act of *La Scala*'s *La Traviata*. We are expected to concern ourselves with the vulgar anxieties of a baseball pitcher suffering from the psychological affliction known charmingly in the colonies as the yips. Good heavens. One shudders at the proletariat drama of it all. Yet, I find myself in a remarkably charitable frame of mind this afternoon. Perhaps it is the sunlight catching the parquetry just so, or perhaps it is the rare pleasure of dissecting a text built entirely upon ancient, predictable foundations.

### The Heavy Footprint of Ancestry

The protagonist, a certain Valley Magnolia—what a terribly affected name, reminiscent of a bankrupt plantation owner—steps into a role at the San Francisco Seals. And why is she there? Naturally, she carries the burdensome ghost of a patriarch. Her father spent decades coaching the very same team. One can already smell the stale popcorn and institutional neglect rotting beneath the stadium seats. Caracciolo leans heavily into this hereditary haunting. It is the literary equivalent of a Duke discovering a mortgage on the family seat. We have seen this exact lineage-driven neurosis played out in Edith Wharton, albeit with considerably more cashmere and fewer curveballs. 

### Somatic Cures and Vulgar Metaphors

The synopsis breathlessly informs us that our heroine helps the afflicted athlete get "out of his brain and into his body." How dreadfully pedestrian. This brings to mind the disastrous choreography of the 1981 Royal Opera House *Nutcracker* incident—an utter collapse of physical discipline that still brings a polite shudder to the drawing rooms of Mayfair. Cal Monte cannot pitch, and Valley must fix him through professional intervention. But the author’s hand is entirely transparent. The professional boundary will crumble faster than a stale biscuit at a third-rate vicarage tea. They will grow closer. Lives will be upended. Spare me the melodrama. 

### The Predictable Anatomy of Despair

Every single narrative beat is telegraphed with the subtlety of a foghorn. Sports romance demands a specific brand of emotional bruising, a sort of low-stakes suffering that appeals exclusively to those who have never had to manage a trust fund or worry about the extinction of an ancient peerage. Caracciolo is merely following the modern playbook, giving the masses their mandated dose of proximity-forced yearning. One almost wishes for a genuine tragedy—a sudden bankruptcy or an arranged marriage to a distant cousin with gout—just to inject some proper aristocratic friction into the proceedings. Instead, we get lingering glances in a dugout. 

My personal chef used the wrong truffles in last night's risotto.
