**Author:** Rachel Hawkins  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-01-06 APL

St. Medard’s Bay sounds exhausting. Give me a pristine limestone terrace in the Marais over a damp Alabama hotel surviving another meteorological disaster any day of the year. Yet here we are, bracing for Rachel Hawkins and *The Storm*. 

The synopsis practically reeks of saltwater, old money, and desperation. A century-old inn clinging to survival while a true-crime grifter and a local pariah waltz back into town? It is the exact narrative energy of the Fyre Festival documentary, just wearing a damp linen shirt. Hawkins knows how to construct a beach read that stains your designer tote with sunscreen, but let us be entirely serious. This reads like a Lifetime movie written by someone who exclusively drinks vintage Sancerre. 

### The Architecture of Petty Grudges

What fascinates me—if one can use that word without gagging—is the structural obsession with the past. We have Geneva, the beleaguered innkeeper hoping a murder will pay her exorbitant property taxes. We have August, the opportunist writer. And, naturally, Lo. The woman accused of slaying a political scion back in nineteen-eighty-four during Hurricane Marie. 

Classic southern gothic mechanics at play. Except stripped of any actual Faulknerian weight and replaced with the literary equivalent of the yacht-rock playlist on your neighbor's Sonos. People returning to small towns to settle old scores never actually achieve anything compelling; they just ruin the local oyster supply and drag up mid-century municipal corruption that nobody cares about. A triumph of endurance over enjoyment. 

### Meteorological Melodrama

The hurricane as a literal and metaphorical device is terribly pedestrian. Oh, look. The weather matches the emotional climate of the protagonist. How daring. One expects the wind to howl precisely when a dark family secret is revealed in the lobby's rotting Victorian parlor. It is a predictable waltz of red herrings and damp upholstery. Hawkins leans hard into the premise that human malice rivals Mother Nature. Perhaps. But Mother Nature rarely needs a publicist to drum up paperback sales. 

Expect breathless pacing. Expect dialogue that sounds like it was workshopped by an algorithmic focus group in Manhattan. Yet, despite my profound aesthetic objections, I will undoubtedly find myself flipping through the advance copy on a long-haul flight simply because the alternative is reading the inflight magazine's article on sustainable aquaculture. 

The business class lounge was far too crowded today, ruining my concentration.
