**Author:** Ashley Elston  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-01-13 APL

Another day, another breathless thriller about rich people behaving badly in the humid American South because apparently, inherited trust funds and monogrammed stationery do not cure a profound spiritual vacancy. Ashley Elston returns to milk the domestic suspense cash cow after *First Lie Wins*, bringing us *Anatomy of an Alibi*. And let us all pretend to be shocked by the premise. Two women, one dead husband, zero originality. Groundbreaking stuff. 

### The Ontology of Suburban Deception

The plot hinges on a classic trope—identity swapping for the low, low price of twelve hours—which requires an extraordinary suspension of disbelief that I simply refuse to manufacture on an empty stomach. Camille Bayliss has a hotshot lawyer husband who tracks her every move because marriage in these novels is apparently just a low-budget psychological hostage situation. Enter Aubrey Price, a woman carrying baggage from a decade ago and residing in a "house full of criminals," which sounds less like a compelling thriller setup and more like a rejected pitch for a basic cable soap opera. They hatch a plan. Because nothing screams foolproof logic quite like impersonating a wealthy socialite at a bar while said socialite plays amateur sleuth against a surveillance-happy spouse. 

### The Epistemology of the Dead Husband

Naturally, the poor husband turns up dead by morning. Shocking. Truly, nobody could have foreseen this catastrophic twist except every single reader who has ever glanced at a book cover in the airport terminal. Now we are subjected to the ultimate logistical puzzle: who has the alibi? The entire thematic weight rests on the slippery nature of truth, perception, and whether trading clothes in a dimly lit cocktail lounge constitutes a valid legal defense. Yet Elston leans so heavily into manufactured convolution that one wonders if the characters possess brain cells or merely decorative fillings. This is high-concept nonsense dressed up in designer heels and sipping a Negroni, trading genuine psychological horror for cheap, algorithmic plot contrivances that play exclusively to the mass-market paperback crowd. 

Give me Virginia Woolf or give me death. At least Woolf understood the existential dread of drawing-room banality without needing a corpse in the foyer to keep the pages turning. But no, we must endure another round of suburban paranoia where every husband is a secret monster and every wife is an undercover mastermind waiting for her moment to shine. It is exhausting. It is derivative. It is begging to be adapted into a limited series on a streaming platform that everyone cancels by month three. 

I would love to dissect the bourgeoisie angst further, but my afternoon obligations are calling. Specifically, I need to go wash a soccer uniform that smells truly horrific.
