**Author:** John Marrs  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-03-08 APL

The synopsis for *Dead in the Water* is so deliberately opaque it might as well be a cryptic fortune cookie left on a diplomat’s desk after a particularly dull cocktail party. One hundred three chapters—*one hundred three*—and not a single word about what happens in them, just a promise of "comprehensive literary analysis" and "book club questions" that presumably will make everyone feel smarter than they are. It’s the literary equivalent of a buffet where the menu reads only "Surprise" in cursive, and the chef is a man who once served raw oysters to a room full of people who didn’t know they were allergic to iodine.

The real crime here isn’t the lack of plot—it’s the *audacity* of the marketing. SuperSummary’s pitch is so aggressively performative it reads like a late-night infomercial for a timeshare in a theme park that hasn’t been built yet. "Raise students’ grades!" "Save teachers time!" "Spark dynamic book discussions!"—because nothing says "engagement" like a study guide that treats a novel like a tax form. And yet, the numbers they cite—96% of students getting higher grades, 98% of educators saving time—are the kind of stats that make you wonder if they’ve been running focus groups with people who’ve never read a book in their lives, just like the focus groups that greenlit *The Room*.

But let’s talk about the *themes*, because that’s where the real artistry—or lack thereof—will lie. The synopsis doesn’t say what the book is about, but it *does* say it’s got "character arcs from tragedy to triumph," which is either a masterclass in false advertising or a nod to the kind of sentimentalism that makes you want to unplug your brain after the third *Harry Potter* sequel. If this is a story about redemption, it’ll feel like a bad episode of *This Is Us* where the protagonist’s arc is just "I had a hard childhood, now I’m a better person." If it’s about survival, it’ll be the kind of survival that’s more about outlasting the reader’s patience than any actual stakes. And if it’s about water—*Dead in the Water*, after all—then I can only assume it’s either a metaphor for drowning in bureaucracy or a thinly veiled allegory for the way corporate America treats its employees, which would be a shame, because that’s a theme that’s been mined so thoroughly it’s starting to smell like a wet sock left in a conference room.

The real question is whether this book will be the literary equivalent of a *Titanic* remake—all the fanfare, none of the substance—or if it’ll be the kind of novel that makes you wonder why anyone thought it needed 103 chapters when a good thriller can be told in 103 tweets. The fact that the synopsis doesn’t even attempt to describe the plot is either a sign of genius or a sign that the author is so confident in their own obscurity that they don’t need to explain themselves. Either way, it’s a gamble, and in the world of publishing, gambles are usually made by people who’ve already lost everything else.

I guess we're doing this.

The only thing more predictable than the themes here will be the way critics will dissect them—like vultures circling a carcass they’ve already picked clean. And if the book is half as bad as its marketing is good, it’ll be the kind of disappointment that makes you question whether you should’ve spent your time reading something like *The Goldfinch* instead, where at least the prose had the decency to be interesting. But then again, maybe that’s the point: to make you realize how much you’d rather be anywhere else.
