The silence in the hallway at 3:00 AM carries a distinct weight when you spend your life waiting for a child to finally feel safe enough to sleep without turning the light on. Yet Simone St. James expects us to believe that a childhood vanishing act—eighteen years of agonizing emptiness—can be packaged into a tidy, supernatural homecoming without leaving permanent scars on the human psyche. *A Box Full of Darkness* drops siblings Violet, Vail, and Dodie right back into the exact geography of their developmental trauma, summoned by the phantom voice of a brother who vanished during a game of hide-and-seek. Hide-and-seek. The ultimate betrayal of domestic security. 

St. James returns to the dread-soaked corridors of Fell, New York, previously mapped out in *The Sun Down Motel*. But let us be entirely serious for a moment. Memory is not a ghost story. Real kids do not process an eighteen-year absence by strolling back into a haunted town for answers; they spend decades in quiet devastation, learning how to breathe through the phantom pain of empty bedrooms. 

### The Architecture of Abandonment

Call it what it is—a structural reliance on cheap hauntings to avoid the messier, agonizing reality of familial fracture. St. James leans heavily into the supernatural, arming Violet with the ability to see dead people and a lingering menace named Sister. But childhood trauma does not manifest as a physical specter lurking by the railroad tracks. It is the invisible static that fills a house after a child goes missing. It is the permanent stiffening of a parent's spine. 

Wait. Let me correct myself on that point. Maybe the supernatural is the only honest way to write about the unfathomable. Perhaps a ghost is the only narrative vessel sturdy enough to hold the sheer, suffocating terror of a little boy who simply ceased to exist because he closed his eyes and hid. 

### The Ghost in the Blueprint

The promotional machine wants us to read this as a propulsive, twist-laden thriller in the vein of *The Broken Girls* or *The Book of Cold Cases*. They want us gasping at the plot mechanics. I care nothing for the mechanics. I care about the Esmie siblings walking back through a door they bolted two decades ago. The synopsis promises that Ben is back and ready to lead them to answers. What an utterly terrifying burden to place on a missing child. 

We have seen this trope a thousand times—Stephen King practically built a retirement fund on the backs of traumatized adults returning to their blighted childhood ZIP codes. Yet St. James possesses a sharp, biting wit that usually cuts through the genre conventions of modern horror. I just refuse to forgive any narrative that treats a missing sibling as a mere catalyst for a spooky roadside motel adventure. Real kids carry their dark boxes inward. They do not need a roadside attraction to remind them of the shadows. 

I am going to go drink absinthe and pretend I am in 1920s Paris.
