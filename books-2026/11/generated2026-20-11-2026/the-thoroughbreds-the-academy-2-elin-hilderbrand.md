Class is back in session, or so the promotional industrial complex screams at us from the rooftops of New England prep schools. *The Thoroughbreds* struts onto the yard with the exact kind of inherited smugness you would expect from a collaboration designed to squeeze every last drop of franchise equity out of an already bloated genre. Because nothing says grassroots working-class liberation like watching wealthy teenagers obsess over college applications and elite breeding grounds. 

Wait. Let us rewind for a second. Why am I even looking at boarding school drama when the local zoning board just gutted our community garden to build another sterile luxury subdivision? It is infuriating. They paved over three distinct heirloom tomato patches just to appease real estate developers who probably shop exclusively at Whole Foods. The sheer audacity of capitalistic encroachment never ceases to make my eye twitch. Speaking of structural rot, let us examine the class dynamics in *The Thoroughbreds*.

Charley and East are on the rocks. Shocker. Because teenage fictional romance needs all the artificial scaffolding of a sci-fi dystopia to stay afloat. Except there are no lasers, no planetary rebellions, just chapel bells and petty malice. Hilderbrand and Cunningham throw a dead girl named Cinnamon Peters into the mix via a mysterious email attachment because a standard teenage tragedy simply lacks cinematic punch without a corpse to anchor the plot. 

Actually, disregard that last critique entirely. The corpse is actually the most honest narrative choice here. It strips away the pretense of a standard coming-of-age romp and leans directly into the macabre absurdity of privilege. 

### The Architecture of Entitlement

Ambition is the engine driving this entire machine. Tiffin Academy acts as a localized ecosystem where social status equals survival. And yet, the synopsis presents these high-stakes boarding school anxieties as if they hold geopolitical weight. It is *Gossip Girl* meets an elite sociological study on generational hoarding. Pippa Sacks enters as the disruptive posh day student meant to dismantle a delicate social hierarchy, which sounds remarkably like every single YA thriller published since the paper shortage began. We have read this blueprint in *A Deadly Education* and countless other contemporary dark academia iterations that mistake tuition fees for genuine stakes. 

Treachery becomes the currency of the sixth formers. Friendship is transactional. Head of School Audre Robinson scrambles to save her job from financial ruin, proving that institutional panic trickles down from the boardrooms straight to the dormitories. 

Treachery and ambition remain the central pillars. Expect zero genuine systemic critique. The authors will likely wrap this up in a neat little bow that forgives every morally bankrupt teenager because their trust funds are vast and their pedigree is unblemished. 

I have to go argue about the geopolitical implications of absurdist theater with a very confused stranger in a smoke-filled dive bar downtown.
