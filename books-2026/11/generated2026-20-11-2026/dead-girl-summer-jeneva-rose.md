**Author:** Jeneva Rose  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The lake’s surface in those promotional images is too still, like someone’s breath held too long. You can almost see the ripples of what’s coming—*Dead Girl Summer*—but the synopsis doesn’t tell you whether the water will be warm or cold when the bodies surface. That’s the problem with this kind of setup. It’s got the bones of a classic, the kind that makes you think of *Sharp Objects*’s razor-sharp dread or *The Girl on the Train*’s unreliable narration, but it’s still just a skeleton until the flesh is fleshed out.

Taryn Voss is the kind of character who should be fascinating—disgraced true-crime host, chasing redemption like it’s a final exam she can’t fail. But the synopsis doesn’t give her edges. Is she a predator or a survivor? Does she *want* to solve the murders, or is she just digging for her own story? The way it’s written, she could be either, and that’s the problem. Jeneva Rose’s last book, *The Last Time I Lied*, had a protagonist who was undeniably compelling because she was morally gray in a way that felt earned. Here, Taryn feels like a placeholder—someone who exists to serve the mystery, not to complicate it.

Then there’s Dana Mercer, the local cop. She’s the foil, the one who *doesn’t* want Taryn there, which is smart—conflict is the engine of these stories. But the synopsis doesn’t tell us what makes her tick beyond her loyalty to her town. Is she a good cop? A corrupt one? Someone who’s seen too much and doesn’t care anymore? The way it’s phrased, she could be any of those, and that’s the issue. The best small-town thrillers—*Gone Girl*’s rural twists, *The Woman in the Window*’s psychological unraveling—work because the locals aren’t just red herrings. They’re characters. And right now, Dana feels like a plot device.

But then—*the fourth body*. That’s where the synopsis starts to breathe. Three drowned girls, and then one more. It’s the kind of escalation that makes you lean in, the kind of detail that suggests this isn’t just a series of accidents. It’s a pattern. And if Jeneva Rose can make that pattern feel inevitable, then she’s got a winner. The question is whether she’ll give us the psychological weight to match the stakes. Because right now, it feels like a checklist: disgraced journalist, small town, bodies in the water, someone who doesn’t want the outsider there. That’s the formula, sure, but where’s the soul?

And that’s the thing—this synopsis doesn’t tell you what the *point* is. Is it about the cost of fame? The dangers of sensationalism? The way small towns weaponize their own myths? It could be any of those, but it’s not clear. The best thrillers—*The Silent Patient*, *My Year of Rest and Relaxation*—don’t just tell you the story; they make you *feel* the weight of it. This one, so far, just tells you the setup.

But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the vagueness is intentional, a way to lure you in. Because let’s be honest, the real mystery here isn’t who killed the girls—it’s whether Jeneva Rose can make you care. And that’s the part that’s still up in the air.

I should probably stop now. My upstairs neighbor is tuning her cello again, and the way it vibrates through the walls is making me think about how sound carries, how stories do too. How some of them just fade into the background noise.
