**Author:** Alice Hoffman  
**Page Count:** Unknown

The Lilith Society’s name alone should’ve been a red flag—like a child scrawling *I’m not a witch* in crayon on the family Bible. But Alice Hoffman, bless her, has always had a knack for turning cautionary tales into glittering fairy traps. *The Witches of Cambridge* isn’t just another witchy college romance; it’s a slow-burning incantation, the kind that makes you lean in closer to the page like you’re afraid the words might vanish if you blink. The synopsis reads like a college essay written by a girl who’s been reading too much *The Crucible* and not nearly enough *The Bell Jar*—which, honestly, is the problem with so many of these historical witchcraft books. They mistake nostalgia for depth.

Ava and Lauren, the book’s two leads, are the kind of opposites that make you suspect the author’s been watching too many *Gilmore Girls* reruns. Ava is the quiet, bookish girl from Maine—imagine if Lizzie McGuire had been born in the 1950s and sent to Radcliffe instead of NYU. Lauren, meanwhile, is the Cambridge heiress with a name that sounds like it belongs on a vintage perfume bottle. Their dynamic is the literary equivalent of a power couple—until you realize Hoffman’s not writing about love so much as she is about the alchemy of rebellion. The Lilith Society isn’t just a secret book club; it’s a lineage, a curse, a *thing* that hums with the same energy as the witches in *The Crucible* but with the campy charm of *Charmed* if it were directed by Tim Burton.

The real genius—or the real problem, depending on your tolerance for historical anachronisms—is how Hoffman weaves McCarthyism into the fabric of the story. It’s not just a backdrop; it’s a living, breathing threat, the kind that makes you wonder if the missing girls are being hunted by the FBI or by something older, something that’s been waiting in the shadows since the Salem trials. The synopsis drops this like a bombshell—*"the witch trials of the past may not be as deeply buried as they once believed"*—but it’s the kind of line that makes you want to demand more. Where’s the tension? Where’s the *danger*? Because right now, it sounds like a *Twilight* fanfiction where the vampires are just really into historical reenactments.

And don’t even get me started on the fact that Hoffman’s been riding the witchwave for decades now. *Practical Magic* was a masterclass in blending folklore with small-town drama, but *The Witches of Cambridge* feels like she’s trying to outdo herself by making it *college*. It’s not that it won’t work—Hoffman’s always worked—but the synopsis leaves you wondering if she’s leaning too hard into the "dark academia meets witchcraft" trend. Because let’s be honest, if you’re going to write about witches in the 1950s, you either need to make it *really* dark or *really* funny. Right now, it’s just *really* safe.

But here’s the thing: I don’t care. I’ll read it anyway. Because Hoffman has this way of making even the most clichéd premises feel like a secret. The Lilith Society isn’t just a group of witches—it’s a *covenant*, a promise, a name that carries the weight of all the women who came before them. And in a world where women are still being drowned in rivers—metaphorically, at least—it’s a theme that refuses to go away.

Yet the synopsis is frustratingly vague about how this all plays out. Are the missing girls a plot device? A metaphor? A literal threat? The way it’s written, you could read this as either a cozy mystery or a full-blown horror story, and that’s the problem with books that rely on *atmosphere* instead of *substance*. But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe Hoffman’s not trying to tell a story so much as she’s trying to *cast a spell*.

The bodega’s run out of my cigarettes, and I’m left wondering if *The Witches of Cambridge* will be the kind of book that lingers like a half-remembered dream—or if it’ll just be another entry in the endless scroll of witchy college romances that feel like they were written by committee.
