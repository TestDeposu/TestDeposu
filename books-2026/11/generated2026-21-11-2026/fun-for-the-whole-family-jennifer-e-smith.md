**Author:** Jennifer E. Smith  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The oven timer is currently screaming at me over a tray of burnt snickerdoodles, which feels entirely appropriate while trying to parse the premise of Jennifer E. Smith’s *Fun for the Whole Family*. Because honestly? The whole premise sounds like a messy batch of dough left out in the August sun. We have four estranged Endicott siblings—Gemma the martyr, Connor the blocked novelist currently weeping into his word processor, Roddy the soccer player risking his marriage for extra turf time, and Jude the famous actress hiding three mysterious secrets behind a blindingly white Hollywood smile—being violently summoned to North Dakota via group text by a sibling they haven't spoken to in years, all because of some dusty childhood nostalgia involving a mercurial mother who dragged them across all fifty states like a bohemian circus act. And look, I usually love a good family reunion drama over a cup of chamomile tea, but the synopsis leans so hard into glossy commercial women's fiction tropes that I am getting whiplash faster than my six-year-old trying to sneak raw chocolate chips out of the pantry. 

Family Skeletons and Stale Road Trip Snacks.

Or maybe it won't be that predictable. 

Actually, scratch that completely. Smith wrote *The Unsinkable Greta James*, which struck a brilliant chord about grief and music, so perhaps this cross-country emotional reckoning will avoid the standard Hallmark-movie pitfalls and actually hurt in the best possible way—think Emily Henry meets *Pineapple Street* by Jenny Jackson, but with significantly more interstate driving. 

The thematic core here revolves around the heavy price of inherited rootlessness versus the claustrophobia of staying put. Gemma gave up her youth to parent the others; Connor is paying for his detachment through professional paralysis; Roddy refuses to put down the cleats. They are all running from the ghost of a mother who treated American geography like a mood ring. Yet the synopsis relies entirely on the crutch of hidden secrets—three of them, apparently, because narrative tension apparently requires a neat little list—rather than letting the psychological friction of adult sibling dynamics do the heavy lifting. Why must every contemporary family novel rely on a ticking-clock secret to force people into a room together? Can't shared trauma and residual resentment be enough to ruin a weekend in North Dakota? 

Needs more salt. 

And more plot substance, frankly. The promotional blurbs promise literary sunshine, but I am looking for a bit of thunderous emotional grit underneath the folksy charm. 

Wait. Did I leave the vanilla extract on the counter where the toddler can reach it?
