**Author:** Nicole Holleman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Oath of the Chosen* is a glittering, jagged promise—like a blade polished to a mirror’s sheen, reflecting just enough light to make you lean in, then slicing when you reach for it. It’s the kind of setup that smells of *The Lies of Locke Lamora* meets *The Priory of the Orange Tree*, but with the visceral, *Game of Thrones*-level brutality of a fantasy where the magic system is less a gift and more a *tax on the guilty*—a cruel, cyclical punishment that ensures no one ever gets clean. And yet, the execution here feels like it’s still in the hands of a writer who’s more interested in *staging* the violence than in making it *mean* anything beyond spectacle.

The opening line—*"Magic is murder."*—is a masterstroke of thematic economy. It’s the kind of line that makes you pause, because it’s not just a rule; it’s a *philosophy*. It’s the kind of philosophy that could fuel an entire subgenre if someone had the guts to lean into it. But here’s the problem: the synopsis doesn’t *do* anything with it. It doesn’t tell us *why* magic is murder. Is it because the act of casting it requires a life? Is it because the kingdom *demands* it? Is it because the magic itself is parasitic, feeding on suffering? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just *drops* the premise like a man dropping a mic in a room full of people who haven’t been told the song yet.

Auri is the protagonist, and she’s framed as this *flawless* instrument of the system—elite, loyal, unquestioning. But then, the moment she’s thrust into the Royal Chosen competition, the synopsis *hints* at something deeper: she has magic of her own. And that’s where the real tension should live. Because if magic is murder, and Auri has it, then she’s either a traitor waiting to happen or a weapon the system doesn’t know how to handle. But the synopsis doesn’t *show* us this tension. It just *tells* us that she’s hiding something, which is the literary equivalent of handing someone a plot twist and calling it a surprise.

The real kicker? The synopsis *never* clarifies what Auri’s magic *does*. Is it the same as the outlawed kind? Is it different? Is it *worse*? The vagueness is maddening. It’s like reading a *Silent Hill* synopsis that doesn’t mention the fog, or a *Dark Souls* game description that doesn’t mention the difficulty. You’re supposed to *infer* the stakes, but here, the stakes are just… floating. And that’s not how you build a story. That’s how you build a *concept*.

Then there’s the romance—or whatever this is with the former Assignment. The synopsis calls him "a man bound to her past, armed with dangerous truths and a dangerously disarming charm." Oh, *please*. This is the kind of writing that makes me want to scream into a pillow. "Dangerously disarming charm"? That’s the kind of line that makes me think of a ghostwriter who’s never read a book with actual tension, only *vibes*. If this is the prose we’re getting, then I can already predict the scenes: slow-burning, *painfully* deliberate, with more emotional whiplash than a *Twilight* fanfic. And the "truths"? What truths? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just *implies* that he’s going to be the love interest, which is the literary equivalent of a movie trailer that ends with a kiss and calls it a day.

The real crime here is the *prophecy*. Oh, *please*. A prophecy. In a world where magic is murder and the system is built on blood, a prophecy is the last thing we need. It’s the kind of thing that turns a sharp, brutal story into a *fantasy soap opera*. I can already see it: Auri and her love interest standing on a cliff, staring into the distance, while some ancient text whispers about "destinies that scream." *Ugh*. This is the kind of thing that makes me want to reach for *The Poppy War* and remind myself that there are still authors who know how to write a war story without turning it into a *Star Wars* fanfic.

But the biggest problem? The synopsis doesn’t *do* anything with the *chaos*. It just *drops* it. One minute, Auri is in the competition; the next, there’s a coordinated attack, and she’s fleeing with the former Assignment. No buildup. No tension. Just *boom*, and suddenly we’re in a chase scene that the synopsis doesn’t even bother to describe. It’s like reading a *Mission: Impossible* movie synopsis that doesn’t mention the explosion. You’re supposed to *feel* the stakes, but here, the stakes are just… there. Like a prop in a room that hasn’t been decorated yet.

The only thing that saves this synopsis from being completely unreadable is the *tone*. It’s got the right mix of *gritty* and *mythic*, the kind of tone that makes you think of *The Blade Itself* or *The First Law*. But it’s not enough. Because a synopsis isn’t just about *what* happens; it’s about *how* it happens. And this one doesn’t do that. It just *lists* the beats, like a grocery list for a story that hasn’t been written yet.

I can already tell this book is going to be a mess. It’s got the bones of something great—*magic as a curse*, *a protagonist with a secret*, *a world built on blood*—but the synopsis doesn’t *do* anything with them. It’s like reading a *Star Wars* script that doesn’t mention the Death Star. You know it’s supposed to be there, but you’re not sure how it’s going to work.

And now I have to fire the ghostwriter who wrote this synopsis. Because this isn’t *Oath of the Chosen*. This is *Oath of the Unreadable*. #FantasyFail #GhostwriterProblem #WhereIsTheMagic
