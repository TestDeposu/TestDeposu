**Author:** T. Kingfisher  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *A Devil of a Crime* is so slick it could’ve been ghostwritten by a committee of *True Detective* writers who’d just finished a bender on cheap whiskey and bad decisions. The premise—retired devil + retired angel = murder squad—is the kind of meta, self-aware joke that either lands with the precision of a well-placed pitchfork or flops like a demonic flat tire. The fact that Kingfisher, whose *The Invisible Life of Addie LaRue* proved she could weave mythic grandeur into a love letter to existential loneliness, is now pivoting toward cozy noir with a supernatural twist is either a bold gambit or a desperate bid for marketability. Either way, it’s a gamble that smells like burnt incense and bad life choices.

The framing device here is pure *Columbo* meets *The Good Place*—a retired devil who’s basically a gardener now, because nothing says "redemption arc" like trading in your eternal damnation for a side hustle in heirloom tomatoes. And then there’s Serafina Fuller, the angel who’s clearly over her celestial duties and just wants to live in a house with a good Wi-Fi signal. The dynamic between them is the kind of forced chemistry that works best when it’s dripping with sarcasm and mutual disdain, like a *Fargo* script written by a couple of exes who still can’t stand each other but have to pretend to be a team for the cameras. The question isn’t whether they’ll solve the case—it’s whether they’ll survive the process without either of them trying to smite the other for "being too human."

Thematically, this is a book that’s playing with the idea of *what happens when you take the supernatural out of the equation*. Fallen and Serafina are both figures who’ve been defined by their roles—one as the embodiment of hell’s wrath, the other as the voice of divine order—and now they’re stuck in a world where their powers are irrelevant, their identities up for grabs. That’s the kind of existential dread that could either be the backbone of a sharp, character-driven mystery or the setup for a book that feels like it’s trying too hard to be *The Night Circus* meets *Knives Out*. The synopsis doesn’t give us enough to tell, but the potential is there: a story about people who’ve been reduced to mortal flaws, forced to rely on each other in a world that doesn’t care about their pasts.

And yet—here’s where the lexical whiplash kicks in—there’s something *deliciously* underwhelming about the idea of a murder mystery where the killer is just some random human who’s been hiding in plain sight. Because let’s be real: if Kingfisher wanted to write a *true* grimdark supernatural thriller, she could’ve gone full *The Only Good Indians* and made the killer a literal demon in disguise. But no, this is a book that’s leaning into the cozy, the familiar, the *comfortable*. And that’s fine—if it’s done right, it could be the kind of thing that makes you forget, for a little while, that the world is a mess of bad decisions and worse people.

But then again, maybe the point is that the supernatural is just another layer of bullshit. Maybe the real horror isn’t the killer—it’s the fact that Fallen and Serafina are just two more people who’ve been gaslit into thinking they’re something they’re not. That’s the kind of thematic depth that could make this book feel like a *Dark Souls* game with a murder mystery plot, if Kingfisher leans into the irony. Or it could just be a book where the real villain is the small-town gossip mill, and the only way to win is to outlast it.

I have a negotiation with my publisher in twenty minutes about whether this book gets a sequel, and I’m already regretting the fact that I don’t have a pitchfork handy.
