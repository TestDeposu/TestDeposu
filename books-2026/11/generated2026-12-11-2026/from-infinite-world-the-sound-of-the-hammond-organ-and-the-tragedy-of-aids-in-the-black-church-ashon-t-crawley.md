The synopsis for *From Infinite World: The Sound of the Hammond Organ and the Tragedy of AIDS in the Black Church* is floating across my desk, dripping in the heavy, self-serious prose we have all grown entirely exhausted by since Baldwin and Lorde perfected the epistolary lament. And yet. Ashon T. Crawley demands attention. Or rather, the marketing copy for Crawley demands that we brace ourselves for another heavy dose of academic trauma-porn disguised as radical joy. 

### The Gospel of Ruin and Reverb

Let us look at what is actually being sold here. A book allegedly arriving in 2026, though the publisher's synopsis borrows heavily—lazily, some might argue—from the exact framing of *The Lonely Letters*. Are we getting a sequel? A theological expansion? Or simply the same intellectual notes recycled under a heavier, more mournful subtitle involving a Hammond organ and a devastating epidemic? Publishers love a brand. They want Crawley to stay in the sanctuary, sweating under the pulpit lights, endlessly theorizing the intersection of blackness, queerness, and Pentecostal rhythm. 

Because God forbid an intellectual write about anything outside the trauma of the pew. 

Actually, strike that. I take it back completely. The Hammond organ is not just a prop; it is the lung of the Black church, breathing life and damnation into the same crowded room. If Crawley wants to tear open the archives and listen to the Leslie speaker bleeding out frequencies while the shadow of the 1980s AIDS crisis decimated the choir loft, who am I to dismiss the repetition? Maybe repeating the grief is the only way to survive the century.

### The Tyranny of Joy-Mandates

The synopsis leans hard on this tired dichotomy between institutional exclusion and revolutionary Black joy. Every contemporary theorist feels legally obligated to quote themselves about joy generating itself, as if optimism can be summoned like a parlor trick through sheer stylistic will. Give me less manifesto, more mess. 

Compare this trajectory to Marlon James or even the dense, demanding theoretical architecture of Saidiya Hartman. They do not beg the reader to feel the transformation; they just detonate the sentence structure and leave you to pick up the shrapnel. Crawley has the academic pedigree to do real damage, to write a book that disrupts how we conceptualize liturgical music and bodily ruin without wrapping it in neat, comforting epistolary bows for university presses. 

Wait. The epistolary form *is* the point. The letter to Moth is what made the previous work breathe. If this new text abandons the intimacy of correspondence for dry historical cataloging of the crisis, it will sink under the weight of its own solemnity. 

My spine aches. The coffee went cold an hour ago, and the pile of unread manuscripts on the floor has officially reached a hazard level that violates city ordinance. I am going to go collapse in my worn-out recliner and attempt to completely ignore the mounting domestic chaos.
