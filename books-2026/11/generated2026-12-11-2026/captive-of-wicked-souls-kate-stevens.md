**Author:** Kate Stevens  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Small-town Montana seems to function as a secular monastery for the emotionally bankrupt, at least according to the literary output of Leeanna Morgan. *Captive of Wicked Souls*—the title is a laughable bit of gothic melodrama that sits entirely at odds with the "sweet" heat level promised—is clearly designed for the reader who finds comfort in the predictable. It proposes a life where the primary conflict is deciding which flannel-clad archetype will provide the most efficient path to a happily-ever-after. There is something profoundly sterile about the way these narratives are packaged; they treat human connection like a series of municipal zoning laws that simply need to be filed correctly to ensure the neighborhood remains aesthetically pleasing, devoid of any genuine grit or the structural decay that actually defines a community. Perhaps there is a certain efficiency in this, I suppose. The synopsis leans heavily on the promise of "second chances" and "tender romance," framing Bozeman as a vacuum where failed relationships and life disappointments are merely ingredients in a recipe for domestic stability, yet I find the relentless optimism exhausting, as if the human condition could be solved by simply standing near a sufficiently large lake until someone with a "big heart" approaches you with a house-hunting checklist.

Actually, the inherent vapidity might be the point. It is a palate cleanser, a literary equivalent of beige wallpaper.

The tropes are weaponized for maximum predictability: the injured athlete, the determined boutique-builder, and the perpetual Christmas miracle. These are not characters; they are placeholders for the reader’s own projection of an ideal, stress-free existence. The thematic focus on "unconditional love" as a reward—rather than a labor—strips away the jagged edges of reality that usually make fiction worth the paper it’s printed on. It aligns neatly with the commercial sensibilities of Robyn Carr, though without the occasional narrative risk that keeps even the most formulaic series from feeling like a training manual for suburban compliance. I suspect this will sell in droves, not because it offers a new perspective, but because it refuses to demand anything intellectual from its audience, providing a comfortable, rhythmic pulse of romantic acquisition that mirrors the assembly-line production of its own sequel lists. It is efficient. It is tidy. It is entirely, depressingly safe.

Passable.

I need to scrub the grass stains out of this soccer uniform before the scent of stale turf and sweat triggers a full-scale sensory collapse in the hallway.
