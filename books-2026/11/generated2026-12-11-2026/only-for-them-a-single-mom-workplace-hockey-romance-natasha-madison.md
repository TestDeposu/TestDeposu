Water is currently leaking through the fabric of this pathetic excuse for a waterproof layer, chilling my collarbones while I stand here staring at a screen, desperately trying to stretch these observations into an acceptable word count so my editor stops emailing me every six minutes. Honestly, why am I even doing this? Natasha Madison is back with *Only For Them*, a title that sounds suspiciously like every other hockey romance currently flooding the market between Colleen Hoover paperbacks and Kendall Ryan airport reads. 

### The Burden of Surviving Men and Pucks

The synopsis leans hard into the classic tropes of female self-preservation. Kennedy declares that the devil has three faces until one fateful night forced her to choose herself over a man—solely for the sake of her daughters. A single mother building an unyielding fortress of survival. Yet, let us be entirely real for a second, or perhaps let me completely contradict my previous skepticism because hockey romance heroines possess a terrifying resilience that somehow always leaves room for a six-foot-four athlete with daddy issues. 

Phoenix represents the other side of this tired coin. Born into hockey royalty as the son of the Dallas Oilers' owner, he desperately needs to escape his lineage. He demands a trade. He wants to prove his worth on new ice. But what does a professional athlete escaping nepotism have in common with an exhausted single mother dodging the ghosts of her past? Proximity. Mandatory, glossy, workplace proximity. 

### The Corporate Blueprint of Romance

The synopsis gives us almost nothing concrete about the actual plot—relying entirely on vague emotional signposts like "fate had other plans" and "I wanted them to be mine." Classic romance marketing. We are forced to project our own exhaustion onto the blank spaces. Madison knows her audience. She understands the aching demographic of readers who want their female protagonists battered by life yet miraculously polished enough to catch the eye of a brooding professional sportsman. 

Actually, scratch that critique entirely. The vagueness is actually a stroke of genius, keeping the emotional stakes nebulous enough for anyone to slip right into Kennedy’s damp, overworked shoes. 

Either way, expect the usual third-act miscommunication before the grand romantic gesture by the boards. The genre demands nothing less. I am just hoping the ice time gets more page space than the domestic musings, though we both know that is a lie. 

The low-frequency rumble of the subway is somehow vibrating straight through the foam of my noise-canceling headphones today.
