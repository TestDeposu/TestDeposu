The subway car’s fluorescent lights buzz like a dying insect, casting a sickly glow over the crowd—someone’s phone screen glows neon blue, a TikTok algorithm feeding them another round of "before and after" transformations that look like Photoshop’s fever dream. You can almost hear the *click* of the ad exec’s brain rewiring itself to sell the next diet pill, the next "glow-up" supplement, the next lie wrapped in a pretty package. Because that’s the thing about *Maintenance Phase*—it’s not just a book about the weight loss industry; it’s a *confession* from someone who spent a decade being the architect of those lies. And the worst part? She didn’t even realize she was writing the script for her own damn life.

Lu Chekowsky—*Mary Manifesto*, the ad genius who could make a toaster look like a love letter—is the kind of character who makes you want to both cheer and scream. She’s the anti-heroine of our era, the woman who spent years convincing the world that self-worth was a product, only to realize too late that she’d been buying the same bullshit. The synopsis doesn’t give us her exit strategy, but it *does* give us the setup: a woman who internalized the very standards she helped monetize, who traded her own voice for the sound of a CEO’s applause. That’s the kind of psychological depth you’d expect from *The New York Times*’s cultural critics, but also from the kind of memoir that makes you feel like you’re eavesdropping on a drunken rant at a bar—*but with footnotes*.

The real genius here is the way the synopsis *hides* its own critique. It doesn’t say, *"This book will expose the lies of the wellness industry."* No, it says something far more dangerous: *"What could possibly go wrong?"*—because the answer, of course, is *everything*, and that’s the point. Chekowsky’s story isn’t just about the ads she made; it’s about the *cost* of being complicit. The way she describes her work—cross-country trips at a moment’s notice, crafting campaigns that turn bodies into commodities—it’s all there in the prose, even if the synopsis doesn’t spell it out. You can *feel* the exhaustion in the way she mentions binging takeout alone, the way she describes her mother’s illness as a complication that never really went away. That’s the kind of emotional undercurrent that makes you think of *Rebecca Solnit*’s essays on resilience, but also the kind of raw, unfiltered voice that belongs in a *BuzzFeed* listicle if it were any good.

And let’s talk about the *tone*—because this isn’t just a manifesto; it’s a *satire*. The way the synopsis frames her as both the villain and the victim is pure *Kathryn Schulz* meets *Jia Tolentino*. She’s the woman who made Michael Jordan’s sneakers *desirable*, who convinced teenage girls that a shirtless heartthrob was worth their emotional labor—only to realize she was just another cog in the machine. The fact that she calls herself *Mary Manifesto* is *chef’s kiss*—it’s the kind of self-aware nickname that suggests she’s already one step ahead of the system, even as she’s still trapped in it. You can hear the sarcasm in the air, the way she describes her life as a series of contradictions: *"I was great at my job because I’d trained for it my whole life. Because I was the one who wrote it."*

The only problem with the synopsis? It’s *too* vague. It doesn’t tell us *how* she broke free—if she did. It doesn’t give us the moment of reckoning, the *aha!* that turns a career into a cautionary tale. But that’s the beauty of it. The best memoirs leave you wanting more, the way a great *New Yorker* profile does. You don’t need every detail; you just need the *vibe*—the sense that this is a woman who’s been *there*, who’s seen the machine from the inside, and who’s not afraid to call it out. And if she does it with the same level of wit and self-deprecation as *Aubrey Gordon*’s *Losing the Plot*, then we’re in for something special.

But here’s the thing: I can’t wait to read it. Because the real question isn’t just *what* she sold—it’s *what she bought*. And that’s the kind of psychological unraveling that makes you think of *Lori Gottlieb*’s *Maybe You Should Talk to Someone*, but with the edge of a *Vogue* cover story gone wrong. The only downside? Now I have to translate some stubborn French existential poetry to unwind from the anticipation. Because sometimes, the best way to process a book you haven’t read yet is to drink something strong and remind yourself that not everything is for sale.
