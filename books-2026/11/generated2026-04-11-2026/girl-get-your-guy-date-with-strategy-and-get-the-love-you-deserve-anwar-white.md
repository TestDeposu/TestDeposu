Hope is a fragile thing, especially when packaged in the glossy, aggressive marketing of modern self-help. Yet I find myself staring at Anwar White’s forthcoming *Girl, Get Your Guy!* with a quiet, aching sort of optimism that feels entirely unearned given the state of contemporary romance literature. We have all waded through the endless swamps of dating manifestos written by men who view human connection through the lens of cold corporate management—talking about funnels, conversion rates, and securing the target like a hostile corporate takeover—and yet White’s promise to shift Black women from the passive position of being chosen to the active throne of the chooser feels oddly revolutionary, or perhaps it just strokes that desperate part of our collective psyche that wants to believe love can be engineered without losing its soul in the process. Mara Brock Akil lends her formidable industry weight to the jacket copy, whispering promises about alchemizing poor relational choices into gold, and I want to believe her. I truly do. Except the synopsis leans heavily on corporate jargon transposed into the bedroom, trading the sterile language of the boardroom for the slightly more glamorous phrasing of an "Olympic Dating podium"—whatever that exhausting spectacle is supposed to entail—and I worry we are simply dressing up our exhaustion in tracksuits.

Actually, scratch that entire critique. 

The framework of a trademarked "7-Date Strategy" is precisely the kind of rigid mechanical scaffolding that kills genuine romance before the appetizers even arrive. 

We are constantly told to build strategies, erect boundaries, and weaponize strategic vulnerability—as if intimacy were a chess match played against an adversary rather than an awkward, beautiful surrender between two bruised humans trying to figure out how to be seen. White wants to dismantle outdated rules, which is commendable, but he immediately replaces them with a different set of rigid guideposts, trading patriarchal traditions for a technocratic blueprint that sounds exhausting to execute on a Tuesday night after working ten hours. 

The synopsis gestures toward lingering wounds and the comforting myth of being "perfectly healed," which actually redeems the project entirely. 

That single concession acknowledges the messy, jagged reality of human grief that most dating gurus gloss over with cheap platitudes and airbrushed optimism. If White actually leans into that messy psychological undercurrent instead of just hiding behind catchy buzzwords, this might transcend the typical genre fare of modern dating guides and offer something enduring. 

Fingers crossed. 

Except I cannot afford to care about dating strategies right now because the maple tree in the backyard has dropped three heavy branches directly over the driveway, and I have to go haul them out of the gutters before the evening storm completely floods the basement.
