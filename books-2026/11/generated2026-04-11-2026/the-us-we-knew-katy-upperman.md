**Author:** Katy Upperman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Us We Knew* reads like a love letter to the kind of YA romance that pretends to be profound but is really just a series of emotional punches to the gut—delivered with the precision of a teenager texting their crush at 2 AM. Celeste’s arc here is the classic "grieving girl gets a second chance at happiness" trope, but the way it’s framed—her grandmother’s death as the catalyst for her self-destruction, then Ty as the savior—feels less like organic storytelling and more like a script cribbed from *The Fault in Our Stars*’ most clichéd moments. The synopsis doesn’t even bother to hide the fact that this is a "secret crush" story with a "brothers as rivals" dynamic, which is fine, but the execution risks feeling like a pastiche of *To All the Boys I’ve Loved Before* meets *The Sun Is Also a Star*, only with the emotional stakes of a bad reality TV show.

The real problem isn’t the plot—it’s the *vagueness*. We’re told Celeste is "falling for" Ty, but we’re not told *why* he’s the one who breaks through her walls. Is it his quiet kindness? His shared grief? The synopsis doesn’t say. It’s all implied, which is a cop-out. Upperman’s previous work—*Everything I Promised You* and *All We Once Had*—suggests she’s capable of deeper psychological nuance, but here, the emotional beats feel like they’re being held together by duct tape and hope. The "phone call that shatters both their worlds" line is so broad it could apply to any YA disaster, from a car accident to a family secret. The synopsis doesn’t even hint at what that call reveals, leaving us with the hollow satisfaction of a cliffhanger that could’ve been avoided with a little more specificity.

And don’t even get me started on the *Ty character*. The synopsis treats him like a walking emotional crutch—"easygoing, considerate, smart"—which is the literary equivalent of describing a character as "nice" and "understanding." Where’s the edge? Where’s the contradiction? The best love interests in YA aren’t just the "right one"; they’re the ones who challenge, who complicate, who make the protagonist question everything. Ty feels like a placeholder, a safe option for Celeste to fall into because he’s not Chase. It’s lazy, and it’s predictable.

But here’s the thing—this book *could* work. If Upperman leans into the fragility of Celeste’s grief and makes Ty’s presence feel earned, not just convenient, then it might avoid becoming another forgettable "grief romance" that gets buried under the weight of its own sentimentality. The synopsis hints at a tragedy coming, but it doesn’t tell us what kind. Is it a death? A betrayal? A revelation? The ambiguity is both its strength and its weakness—it could be gripping, or it could be nothing more than a setup for a sob story that fizzles out by page 200.

Speaking of sob stories, I can’t help but think about how *The Last Cuentista* by Donna Barba Higuera—another book that balances grief and redemption—managed to make its emotional core feel *specific*, not just universal. The difference is that Higuera didn’t rely on a vague "phone call" to upend everything; she built the tension through small, deliberate moments. *The Us We Knew* risks doing the opposite, turning its tragedy into a one-note punchline.

I have to finish painting a portrait of my neighbor’s grieving cat, who stares at me like I’m the one who’s supposed to fix everything. The cat’s name is Whiskers, and he’s judging me. Just like this book’s going to judge me for even thinking it might not be as good as the hype suggests.
