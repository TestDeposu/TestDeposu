Stained paper and old ink. That is what my grandfather always smelled like when he brought home the scrolls from the dusty provincial archives, unspooling centuries of ghosts across our splintered kitchen table while the tea grew cold in the ceramic cups. Tzeyi Koay seems to understand this specific, haunting weight in *A Curse Carved in Ink*. A family bookstore hiding vampiric folklore isn't just a clever hook; it's a brilliant anchor for a blood-soaked inheritance. We are looking at a 2026 debut that dares to mix Chinese mythos with queer romance, and honestly, the publishing market is about time it moved past predictable court intrigue and repetitive fae courts. 

Give me more underworlds. Give me more suffering gods.

My neighbors still let their abominable golden retriever howl at the phantom mail carrier every single morning at precisely 7:14, a caterwauling tragedy that rivals any mythological damnation, testing the absolute limits of my sanity while I try to appreciate literature that actually respects the dead. At least Diyu offers structural integrity and ten brutal layers of divine accountability, unlike this neighborhood association.

### Ink, Blood, and Ten Layers of Bureaucracy

Consider the mechanics of the Jiangshi’s Claw. Possession isn't merely an external monster breaking down the door; it is an internal mutiny, your own red cells turning traitorous and whispering your worst impulses back to you. Koay leans heavily into this psychological claustrophobia. Zhenya murdering her best friend Shuhua right out of the gate is a deliciously ruthless narrative choice. No slow burns. No tedious waiting around for the inciting incident in chapter three. Just immediate, irreversible ruin, followed by a desperate descent into the Chinese underworld. 

And then we meet the immortals. A brooding judge of hell alongside a dangerously charming goddess of death. 

A queer love triangle set against the bureaucratic horrors of the afterlife? Yes. Sign me up immediately. Shelly Parker-Chan walked so Koay could violently sprint through the gates of hell. Fans of *She Who Became the Sun* will recognize this exact brand of razor-sharp ambition, where love and survival are violently at war with dynastic doom. Yet I worry the synopsis is playing its cards too close to its chest. Will the trials through the ten layers become a monotonous video-game checklist of monsters? Or will Koay actually interrogate the heavy philosophical debt of karmic retribution? We shall see. 

I refuse to tolerate another sanitized fantasy where the underworld looks like a mildly inconvenient subterranean lounge. I want suffering. I want bureaucratic dread mixed with pulse-pounding romantic tension that makes my old bones ache with secondhand anxiety. 

I must go now because my grandchildren are screaming in the hallway and I need to spend the next four hours attempting to assemble a profoundly confusing piece of Swedish furniture that appears to have been manufactured without screws or logic.
