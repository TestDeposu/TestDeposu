Every time someone utters the tired phrase "people can change," a little piece of my soul quietly detaches and floats into the ether. Harry Trevaldwyn is bringing us back to the well with *I Can’t Believe I Don’t Have a Boyfriend: More Romantic Tragedies of a Drama King*. Look. We all know how this goes. The synopsis screams rockstar player, arrogant, cocky, the whole exhausting catalog of emotional unavailability wrapped in leather pants. Why do we keep buying the lie that a good kiss can rewrite an entire personality matrix? It never does. Trust me. I am currently staring at a half-empty mug of cold chamomile tea, wondering why anyone ever trusted a man who owns a guitar.

### The Symphony of Bad Decisions

To play devil's advocate, the dual-POV structure promises a chaotic descent into mutual delusion. Piper thinks she can save him. King thinks he is suddenly worthy because a nice girl looked at him with doe eyes. Groundbreaking. Emily Henry and Ali Hazelwood have beaten this specific dead horse into fine powder, yet here we are, eagerly waiting to watch two people ignore every red flag waving furiously in their faces. Ellie, the sensible friend warning Piper away? She is the only voice of reason in this entire fictional universe, and naturally, she will be entirely ignored for the sake of manufactured tension.

### Why Do We Subsidize Our Own Heartbreak?

My radiator has been making this infernal clanking sound since Tuesday. It sounds like a mechanical banshee trapped in the basement, violently shaking its cage every time the thermostat even thinks about shifting. It possesses more emotional depth and consistency than any fictional rockstar who suddenly decides to reform his wicked ways just because he met a girl with a sensible cardigan. My landlord promised he would fix it before the frost set in. He lied. Just like every literary bad boy who swears he is changing for the better, only to ghost you by the third act because the tour called. 

### The Illusion of Redemption

And what about Jack? The protective best friend with the absolute rule about his little sister. The synopsis leans heavily on the tired trope of impending physical violence from the sibling. Oh, please. As if Jack is actually going to do anything other than scowl across a kitchen island for three chapters before giving a reluctant blessing. The stakes are tissue-thin. Yet, Trevaldwyn has a sharp comedic voice that usually saves these paper-thin plots from drowning in their own earnestness. 

Anyway, I walked all the way down to the corner in the freezing drizzle just to pick up the latest issue of *The Paris Review*, only to find the wire rack completely empty because some hoarding academic bought every single copy for a collage project.
