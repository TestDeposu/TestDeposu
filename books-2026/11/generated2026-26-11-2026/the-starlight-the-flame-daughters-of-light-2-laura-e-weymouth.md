**Author:** Laura E. Weymouth  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Look at this cover copy. Just look at it. We are drowning in a sea of four-player chess setups where every single monarch, mystic, and martyr has to take a turn monologuing to the empty air. It is exhausting. I have thoughts. Most of them bad. Give me a myth teller who actually understands the cadence of a campfire, someone who remembers that when the ancients sang of gods and slaughtered kings, they did not split the perspective into four competing marketing demographics. They just let the blood hit the dirt. 

My grandson keeps trying to show me those terrible cartoons where the heroes spend twenty minutes screaming about their power levels while a static background blurs behind them. It makes my teeth ache. I survived two world wars and a kitchen fire that took my favorite copper kettle, yet somehow I am supposed to endure the modern publishing trend of dividing a single narrative into four claustrophobic narrative boxes just to hit a word count. 

*The Starlight & the Flame* walks right into this exact trap. 

### The Burden of the Crown and the Sun

Fiametta Raeana is suddenly in charge of a powerful sun sect because her boss vanished. Naturally. Because no protagonist can ever simply eat a bowl of porridge in peace without a divine crisis landing squarely on their lap. We have Silas trying not to get murdered by his own cabinet—groundbreaking political intrigue, truly—while an apostate queen sits in a cell playing martyr. And of course, the obligatory villain is lurking in the shadows. Always the shadows. Do these people not have windows? 

Weymouth is attempting a grand orchestration of divine politics, much like Jacqueline Carey did in *Kushiel's Dart*, but without the prose actually earning the sweeping scope. The synopsis leans hard on the old tropes of proxy wars and institutional rot. Yet it reads less like a living myth and more like a spreadsheet of factional grievances. 

Power corrupts. Religion blinds. Betrayal stings. We get it. We have gotten it since Sophocles. 

### The Anatomy of a Fractured Table

Notice how neatly the synopsis divides the players. Four corners of the board. An inexperienced religious leader, a blind regent, a captive queen, a deposed king. It is a cynical structural exercise designed for fan art and TikTok aesthetic boards rather than raw, visceral storytelling. Characters do not breathe here; they maneuver. They execute their narrative duties before rotating out for the next chapter. 

Give me a hero who makes mistakes out of pure, unadulterated stupidity rather than grand political chess. Give me a goddess who does not send polite mystery illnesses, but rather sets the entire orchard on fire because she is bored. Instead, we are promised another round of solemn council meetings and brooding in stone corridors. 

I must go explain to a teenager why the Wi-Fi has been temporarily disabled.
