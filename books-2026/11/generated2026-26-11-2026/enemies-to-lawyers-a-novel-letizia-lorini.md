I need these fifty bucks for a textbook on thermodynamics—actual thermodynamics, not the metaphorical kind where two lawyers stare at each other over a stack of deposition transcripts and pretend nobody is sweating through their polyester trousers. Letizia Lorini is giving us *Enemies to Lawyers: A Novel*, which arrives in 2026 to answer a question nobody asked: what if Emily Henry wrote a brief on civil procedure? 

### The Jurisprudence of Smut

Junior associates and fake dating. Name a more predictable corporate synergy. Ivy Isley escapes family court to fight the good fight against a data-leaking dating app called MeetCute—because subtlety is dead and buried six feet under the billable hour—only to be saddled with a co-counsel who has high cheekbones and zero respect for boundaries. Miles Montrose suggests they pretend to be romantically entangled to rattle the opposing counsel, who just happens to be Ivy’s high school sweetheart, Spencer Hale. 

Wait. Let me back up. The synopsis says Miles brought her onto the case *specifically* because Spencer is opposing counsel. That is not a strategic partnership. That is a targeted emotional assassination attempt wrapped in billable increments. 

Actually, scratch that entire premise. It is not an assassination attempt; it is a meticulously engineered marketing ploy designed to appeal to readers who treat romance tropes like a bingo card. 

### Tangents on Tort Law

The blurb promises a breath of fresh air. A breath of fresh air. Right. Because nothing screams atmospheric innovation quite like a mandatory proximity trope inside a corporate law firm. Lorini clearly understands the algorithm. Chelsea Curto calls it the perfect balance of palpable chemistry and workplace dynamics, which is high praise for a book whose entire plot hinges on a logistical choice that would get any real-world attorney disbarred for a glaring conflict of interest. 

If these characters spent half as much time checking precedent as they do locking eyes during late-night case prep, the class action would actually settle before the third act. But logic has no jurisdiction here. We are dealing with high-stakes class action litigation where the only damages being assessed are to the reader's suspension of disbelief.
