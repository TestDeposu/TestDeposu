**Author:** Annabel Monaghan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The radiators in this hallway are currently clanking like a restless skeleton in a closet, demanding attention while the November wind rattles the single-pane glass just enough to remind everyone that winter is coming for our bones. Annabel Monaghan is back with *August After All*, and frankly, I am rubbing my hands together with a quiet, mischievous grin while ignoring the sink full of coffee mugs that have achieved sentience. 

### The Geography of Regret

Montauk is supposed to be a sanctuary of salty air and sun-bleached shingles, yet here it becomes a staging ground for emotional wreckage. The synopsis relies on a delicious, agonizing bit of dramatic irony that Emily Henry fans will recognize instantly—that awful, dizzying vertigo of realizing someone else got the prize you stared at first. But let us be entirely serious for a moment. Eighteen years between a train ride and a divorcee's luxury architectural build? That is not a slow burn; that is a wildfire that smoldered beneath damp leaves for nearly two decades while everyone pretended the forest was safe. 

Or perhaps I am misreading the timeline entirely and we are meant to care deeply about luxury real estate aesthetics while emotional devastation occurs in the kitchen. Actually, scratch that. The architecture angle is merely window dressing—a shiny distraction so we do not notice the characters walking straight into the same trap they built in their twenties. 

### Nostalgia as a Weapon

Monaghan has carved out a distinct niche by writing romances that taste like dry white wine on a hot porch, yet this premise smells faintly of ozone and impending disaster. Summer 2009 meets Summer 2027. Time is a flat, cruel circle engineered by novelists who refuse to let the past stay buried in the sand. And who can blame them? Nostalgia sells better than sex, especially when paired with an artist whose paintings dictate the structural integrity of a house. 

Yet, the synopsis leaves us hanging on the exact mechanics of this betrayal. Tess gets the guy in the first act—or rather, the initial memory—leaving Eve to design the walls around the wreckage a year after the divorce ink dries. It is messy. It is deliciously complicated. And it dares to ask whether the person you loved first is the person you are actually supposed to keep, or just the ghost that haunts every subsequent blueprint. 

I should probably finish grading those manuscripts on the wobbly table before midnight strikes, but staring at the bare branches scraping against the streetlight outside is infinitely more entertaining. Monaghan knows how to write charm without completely sanitizing the ache, which is precisely why this will likely dominate beach bags even if it drops into our laps during the wrong season. 

Midnight approaches, and I am going to wander the city streets at midnight seeking inspiration among the shivering shadows and wet pavement.
