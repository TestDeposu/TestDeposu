**Page Count:** Unknown

The screech of iron on old tracks always hits right at the base of the skull, especially when you are standing shoulder-to-shoulder with strangers who smell faintly of damp wool and existential dread. Yet here I am, thumbing through a digital galley of Sophie Stava’s upcoming *Girls Will Be Girls*, desperately searching for a distraction from the fact that my life currently resembles a poorly lit corridor leading absolutely nowhere. People keep jostling my elbow. Stava clearly wants to capitalize on the glossy, high-society-gone-wrong momentum of *Count My Lies*—which, let us be entirely honest, was an absolute trash-fire masterpiece of suburban paranoia that Hulu is about to completely ruin or somehow elevate into peak television. 

### Suburban Panopticons and Weaponized Gossip

The synopsis leans heavily into the tired trope of the outsider desperate for elite validation—a classic literary mechanism that usually results in predictable gaslighting. But Stava introduces a genuinely sickening conceptual anchor here: the "confessions" game. And honestly? That is peak psychological sadism. Exchanging blackmail disguised as sisterhood is the kind of feral social maneuvering I am entirely here for while swaying rhythmically to the erratic lurch of a deteriorating transit system. 

Mallory Greene is drowning. We all are, really. Between the invisible labor of domestic displacement and husbands who mentally check out to stare at drywall, the modern domestic thriller has essentially replaced the gothic manor with the open-plan kitchen. Stava understands that suburban wealth is a panopticon. Natalie—the magnetic ringleader whose name drips off the page like poisoned honey—does not just invite Mallory into the fold. She apparently traps her in an inescapable spiderweb of pre-orchestrated psychological warfare. 

### The Aesthetics of Deception

What fascinates me about this setup, purely based on the blurb's ominous refusal to provide actual plot details—seriously, the synopsis is vague enough to drive a truck through—is the subversion of female spaces. We are miles away from the domestic bliss peddled by Liane Moriarty or the razor-sharp biting satire of Karin Slaughter. This is straight-up psychological carnage. Stava is operating in that murky, neon-soaked subspace where *The Girls* meets *Big Little Lies*, but with a decidedly more ruthless, knife-to-the-throat velocity. 

And yet, I am bracing myself for the inevitable third-act trope explosion. Will Mallory turn out to be the mastermind all along? Probably. Is her distant husband hiding a basement full of offshore accounts and dead bodies? Absolutely. The genre demands structural predictability even as it promises transgressive thrills. Stava has built a house of cards on a fault line, and I am desperately hoping the roof collapses in a blaze of gloriously messy consequences rather than a neat moral wrap-up. If the ending cops out with a feel-good revelation about female solidarity, I am going to lose my remaining grip on sanity right here between the 4th Street and 7th Avenue corridor. 

The train jerks violently, throwing a guy in a tailored suit directly into my shoulder, and I spill half my lukewarm oat milk latte. God, I need a drink. Or at least a dark room where nobody asks me about my life goals. I have to bolt off this metal tube in two stops anyway, sprinting straight down into a suffocating, smoky basement where a friend's experimental jazz quintet is currently torturing a saxophone and my last remaining brain cells.
