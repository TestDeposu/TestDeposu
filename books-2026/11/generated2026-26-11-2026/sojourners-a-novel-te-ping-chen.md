**Author:** Te-Ping Chen  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The market is currently saturated with historical fiction that reads like a Wikipedia page wearing a cheap floral print dress. Yet *Sojourners* might actually transcend the mundane, assuming the prose possesses enough teeth to cut through the heavy tropical air of 1920s Hawai'i. Te-Ping Chen is attempting something rather ambitious here—juxtaposing the quiet, crushing isolation of a rural general store against the neon-drenched ascent of Honolulu’s nascent tourist trap economy. But let us be entirely serious. The premise relies heavily on a ghost-in-the-machine radio trope that risks sliding straight into melodramatic trash tier if not handled with absolute, ice-cold precision. 

### The Geography of Absence

Consider the structural weight placed upon the elder sister’s disappearance. It is a classic catalytic device, yes, but Chen uses the multi-ethnic landscape—Chinese, Japanese, Portuguese, and Native Hawaiian communities colliding in the shadow of the plantations—as a living, breathing canvas rather than mere set dressing. Or so the back-cover blurb desperately tries to convince us. Critics are always throwing around comparisons to *Moloka'i* or historical epics by Lisa See, though I suspect this novel shares more DNA with the psychological alienation found in Karen Tei Yamashita's early works. Kana’s transition from an idyllic adolescence to a solitary existence running her father's shop is framed as a study in calcified grief. And yet, I am side-eyeing the radio gimmick. Hearing a dead sibling’s voice warbling out of a vacuum tube feels dangerously close to a Lifetime movie adaptation written by an algorithm. 

### Identity on the Margins

Half-Chinese and half-Hawaiian protagonists navigating a rapidly colonizing paradise offer a fascinating lens on displacement. The thematic core here isn't just about finding a missing girl; it’s about the erasure of indigenous spaces by mainland American consumerism. Chen is poking at the raw nerves of historical identity—how a person's heritage becomes commodified just as quickly as a pineapple crop. Still, one worries the narrative will lose its grit once Kana boards that boat to Honolulu. Will the prose maintain its cynical edge, or will it devolve into a predictable coming-of-age tearjerker designed to win Goodreads Choice Awards? The synopsis leaves the ending frustratingly ambiguous, promising a reckoning with "uncomfortable truths" while keeping the actual aesthetic stakes entirely hidden in the fog. 

I detest clumsy symbolism involving radios, yet I am willing to be proven wrong by an author who understands that history is rarely a clean sweep—it's just a messy pile of rubble that people manage to build tourist hotels over.
