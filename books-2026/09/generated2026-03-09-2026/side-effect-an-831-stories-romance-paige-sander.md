The fluorescent hum overhead is starting to sound like a dying transformer—or maybe that’s just my brain vibrating after thirty hours awake staring at spreadsheets of municipal water pipe layouts. Let’s pretend for a second that people actually read books about terminal illness with the same reckless abandon they consume ice cream on a park bench. Paige Sander’s upcoming *Side Effect* tries to fuse the trauma-mining of contemporary memoir with the sweet, cynical architecture of a podcast-era rom-com. Or so the back cover tells us. We are looking at a narrative framework that borrows heavily from Emily Henry’s witty structural scaffolding, yet insists on dragging a heavy sack of medical dread straight through the rom-com turnstile. 

### Microphones and Malignant Cells

Podcasting as a narrative device in fiction has become the new coffee-shop career—a tidy excuse for a protagonist to voice internal monologues aloud for the benefit of an invisible audience. Ruby monetizes her romantic disasters. Yet the real disaster is biological. Late-stage brain cancer. A diagnosis dropped into a hooky synopsis like a clove of garlic in a white wine reduction—sharp, pungent, and impossible to ignore. But wait, let me walk that back. Is it actually impossible to ignore, or is it just another slick marketing gimmick designed to make us cry into our Kindles while waiting for the dawn shift to end? The juxtaposition of a dating-horror-story podcast with mortality rates feels jarring. And I mean that in the worst possible way. 

### The Dreamboat Paradox

Enter Neal. The mysterious dreamboat. Every single romance novel published after 2022 is legally obligated to feature at least one man who possesses a enigmatic smile and a secret that threatens to unravel the third-act HEA. Sander gives us a slow-burn dynamic where emotional armor meets actual, literal expiration dates. Except, how slow can a burn really be when the protagonist’s clock is ticking down like a cheap kitchen timer? The synopsis implies a chess match of mutual avoidance—she pushes people away because of her podcast persona, he presumably pushes people away because he’s hiding his own flavor of baggage. Actually, scratch that, maybe the cynicism is getting the better of me. Maybe the slow burn hits differently when time is a scarce commodity. Writers like Nicola Yoon have walked this tightrope before, balancing terminal diagnoses with fluttering heartbeats. Sander is clearly gunning for that exact sweet spot between grief porn and serotonin hit. 

The mechanics of the premise rely entirely on withholding. We know she’s sick. We know he’s mysterious. We know the podcast will somehow capture the collateral damage of their unfolding intimacy. Whether the prose can sustain this tonal tightrope walk without devolving into maudlin sentimentality remains entirely to be seen—especially since we are relying solely on a three-sentence blurb written by a marketing team hopped up on cold brew and desperation. 

The security guard is pacing past the biography aisle again, flashing his heavy ring of keys like a threat, which means my fifteen minutes of quiet contemplation are officially over and I have to go debate the merits of absurdism with a sketchy stranger in a sticky vinyl booth down at the all-night dive.
