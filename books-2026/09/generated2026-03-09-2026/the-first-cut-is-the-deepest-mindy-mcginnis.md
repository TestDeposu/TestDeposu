**Author:** Mindy McGinnis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Gretchen McMurphy is finally getting her bread up, and honestly? Good for her. Capitalism demands sacrifices, but usually, those sacrifices don't involve an unhinged pop star wielding lethal implements behind a glittering veneer of rhinestone corsets and auto-tune. Mindy McGinnis is dropping *The First Cut Is the Deepest* into our laps, and we are expected to gasp at the revelation that the music industry chews women up and spits them out. Groundbreaking. Truly, an epistemic shift for the ages. 

Yet, the synopsis leans hard into the "girlfriends who murder together stay together" trope that we have consumed ad nauseam ever since *My Year of Rest and Relaxation* spawned a thousand pale, misanthropic imitators. But perhaps—no, actually, let me walk that back entirely. The cynical corporate machinery of pop stardom deserves a much bloodier satirical autopsy than what standard thriller tropes usually allow. 

### The Pathology of the Bridge-and-Chorus

Women in the public eye are perpetually expected to perform a grotesque alchemy—turning their private trauma into marketable, radio-friendly hooks. Fae Deeds—what a magnificent, cartoonish moniker for a psychopath—understands this transaction intimately. She isn't just coping; she is weaponizing the systemic misogyny that built her platinum-selling discography. And Gretchen? Sweet, bingo-hall-playing Gretchen who accidentally soundtracked a fitness influencer's glute-gains—she is walking right into the meat grinder. 

We are looking at a classic bifurcation of female rage. On one side, the commercialized, sanitized pop-anthem grievance designed for TikTok consumption. On the other, the much sharper, feral reality of a woman who has simply had enough. McGinnis is clearly aiming for a neon-drenched pop-noir aesthetic here, drenched in bottled water preferences and glittering delusion. But let us be entirely serious. Will this book actually deconstruct the commodification of female pain, or will it just give us a fun, gory romp where the girlboss gets to commit light homicide between arena tour stops? I am manifesting the former, while realistically preparing for the latter. Think Ottessa Moshfegh writing for a stadium crowd, but messier. 

### The Price of a Stream

The commodification of heartbreak is hardly a new literary landscape. But adding a literal bloodbath to the mix? That is just good business. Fame demands a pound of flesh, and Fae Deeds is simply collecting interest. Gretchen has to decide what she is willing to sacrifice—her morals, her safety, or perhaps just her burgeoning streaming revenue. And honestly, looking at the current state of the Billboard charts, murder might actually be the better career move.
