**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Every third memoir published this decade seems contractually obligated to feature a suburban driveway hiding some tedious, suburban betrayal. *A Family Across Town* arrives in 2026 promising us the moon—or at least a second tract house in the same Los Angeles zip code. Pardon me if I refuse to drop to my knees in gratitude for a premise recycled straight from late-night cable movies. Aynah V. Askanas expects us to gasp at the sheer audacity of a man driving ten minutes east to maintain a parallel household. Groundbreaking. Truly, the cartography of domestic mediocrity has never been mapped with such startling lack of imagination. 

### The Architecture of Banal Deceit

We are subjected to the usual rhetorical hand-wringing in the synopsis. Why did he do it? Why did the mother stay? Oh, spare me the existential interrogation. Men cheat because they are selfish cowards, and mothers stay because of property taxes and emotional inertia. There, I just saved you three hundred pages of navel-gazing prose. But no, we must endure another literary excavation of a father’s double life, as if every second-rate secret deserves a hardcover release. 

Wait. Let me be entirely fair to Askanas for a fraction of a second—actually, no, I take it back completely. The prose is supposedly "darkly funny," which usually translates to privileged millennials making snarky remarks while weeping into their oat milk lattes over parental abandonment. 

### The Cult of Suburban Trauma

We are drowning in an ocean of trauma memoirs written by people who treat a fractured family dinner like a war crime. Compare this to the razor-sharp psychological devastation found in *The Glass Castle* or heavy hitters like Vivian Gornick. Those works earned their cruelty. Here, we get a sanitized suburban domesticity where the most dangerous thing in the pantry is passive-aggression. The synopsis leans heavily on buzzwords—betrayal, dysfunction, psychological cost—without offering a single distinctive narrative hook. It reads like a checklist generated by an algorithm designed to appeal exclusively to book clubs that drink cheap Pinot Grigio and discuss nothing of substance. 

And yet, I will probably read it. 

That is the bitter irony of this entire enterprise. We crave the wreckage, even when we can spot the scaffolding from a mile away. Askanas might surprise us with actual stylistic grit, subverting the tired tropes of the secret-family genre instead of merely wallowing in them. Or she might just deliver three hundred pages of righteous whining. My patience has already evaporated. 

I am going to go stare blankly into the open refrigerator for inspiration.
