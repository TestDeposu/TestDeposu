We are looking at *What Kind of Mother* by Bernadette Murphy and honestly, my blood is boiling over the sheer volume of structural gaslighting women have endured for generations under the guise of holy institutions. Look at the setup. A sixteen-year-old kid—shouldering the emotional wreckage of her own mother's untreated depression and a crumbling household—gets handed a life-altering ultimatum by a society that views her bodily autonomy as public property. 

And then we wonder why she spends forty years carrying the heavy, calcified shame of a closed adoption system built specifically to silence birth mothers. 

People love to talk about adoption like it's some sanitized Hallmark greeting card moment, completely erasing the jagged systemic violence that forces working-class girls to surrender their children because keeping them would require actual social safety nets instead of hollow moralizing. 

Speaking of structural failures, I swear to god, if I have to watch one more episode of *Bluey* where the parents calmly process their toddler's existential dread using artisanal wooden toys while living in a multi-million-dollar Brisbane architectural masterpiece, I am going to throw my entire coffee table through the front window. Why can't a cartoon dad just admit he is hanging on by a thread and screaming into a sofa cushion like the rest of us? Anyway. 

Murphy's framing of this forty-year epistolary quest hits right at the throat of our collective cultural amnesia. 

The prose promises to peel back the layers of Irish Catholic guilt—that heavy, suffocating blanket of inherited trauma that tells young girls their worth is measured entirely by their suffering. 

Expect comparisons to Dani Shapiro's *Inheritance* or Nicole Chung's *All You Can Ever Know*, though Murphy's lens focuses sharply on the weaponization of the maternal instinct. 

Brutal.
