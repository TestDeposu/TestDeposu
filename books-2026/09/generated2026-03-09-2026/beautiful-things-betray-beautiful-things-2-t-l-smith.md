The desk’s surface is a warzone of half-empty coffee cups—some still warm, others cold enough to make you wonder if you’ve been here since last Tuesday. The synopsis for *Beautiful Things Betray* is a knife’s edge: sharp enough to cut, but not quite sharp enough to tell you what it’s cutting into. "I am loyal, but I will betray you."—that’s the kind of line that could belong in a *Gone Girl* afterparty or a *You* binge session, but the rest of it? It’s the kind of thing that makes you lean in, then immediately regret it because you’re already late for something.

The first line is a flex—classic villain monologue, the kind that makes you think you’re in for a full-blown antihero origin story. But then the next lines? "I met her when I was sixteen, she was a breath of fresh air."—suddenly it’s not just a villain, it’s a *broken* villain, the kind who’s been waiting for a decade to drag someone back into the dark. The imagery here is *chef’s kiss*—"her smile could light up a room, making my black heart pitter patter"—it’s the kind of poetic violence that makes you wonder if the author’s been reading too much *The Raven* while chain-smoking in a dimly lit café. And then—*then*—the punch: "I found her again ten years later, with a syringe in her arm." That’s not just a plot beat. That’s a *landmine*.

But here’s the thing—this synopsis is *too* vague. It’s like being handed a single playing card from a deck and being told it’s the entire hand. Is this a revenge thriller? A dark romance? A slow-burn psychological horror? The way it’s written, it could be all three, and none of them. The "angel, but the devil" line? That’s the kind of duality that works in *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo* or *Gone Girl*, but it’s also the kind of thing that gets overused in YA and dark academia until it loses its bite. And yet—*yet*—the way it’s phrased, with that raw, unfiltered voice, makes you think the author knows exactly what they’re doing.

But then—*then*—you realize the synopsis is doing something even more dangerous. It’s not just setting up a story; it’s setting up a *feeling*. The protagonist isn’t just a villain or a victim; they’re a *character* who’s been waiting for a decade to see if their past is worth destroying again. That’s the kind of emotional hook that could make this book feel like a *True Detective* episode written by a poet. But it could also feel like a *Twilight* knockoff if the execution isn’t tight.

And that’s where the real question lies: *Is this going to be the kind of book that makes you forget you’re reading, or the kind that makes you forget you’re alive?* The synopsis suggests the latter, but without more context, it’s impossible to tell. The way it’s written, it could be the next *The Silent Patient*—a psychological unraveling disguised as a love story—or it could be the next *Red, White & Royal Blue*—a dark romance with a killer twist. But given the tone, I’m leaning toward the former.

But—*but*—here’s where I have to pivot. Because the more I think about it, the more I realize this synopsis is *too* reliant on the "found family" trope. The "she was broken and I was glad she was broken" line? That’s the kind of thing that makes you think of *The Last of Us*—the kind of emotional manipulation that works in a survival story but feels exploitative in something like this. And yet—*yet*—the way it’s phrased, with that raw, unfiltered voice, makes you think the author knows exactly how to walk that line.

This altered my brain chemistry.

The desk’s thermostat is suddenly too hot, and you’re too distracted to care. The synopsis is a tease, a promise, a *landmine*—but it’s also a distraction. And that’s the problem with books like this. They make you want to read them before you’ve even finished the synopsis. But will it live up to the hype? Or will it be another one of those books that leaves you feeling like you’ve been played?
