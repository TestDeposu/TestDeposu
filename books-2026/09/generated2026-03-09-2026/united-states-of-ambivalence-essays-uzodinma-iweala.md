Another publisher email, another frantic mandate to squeeze meaning out of a press release while the floorboards shake from the bass downstairs and someone in 4B vacuums at three in the morning. Let us talk about *United States of Ambivalence*. Uzodinma Iweala is dropping a collection of essays in 2026 because apparently, we need a novelist-slash-doctor to diagnose the national psyche while we all collectively stare into the glowing rectangles of our doom. 

The marketing copy leans hard on the heavy-hitting buzzwords — race, civil rights, culture wars, Black Lives Matter, abortion, immigration. Oh joy. Another book telling us that things are complicated. 

### Diagnosing the National Malaise

The synopsis promises piercing clarity. A doctor’s touch for a sick country. But let us be entirely real for a second — I am typing this at a desk littered with cold espresso and half-smoked cigarettes just to hit a word count for an editor who thinks culture is something you buy at an airport bookstore. Do I actually believe a trio of essays penned by someone living abroad can untangle the hyper-capitalist mess of modern America? Please. We are beyond essays. We need an exorcist. 

Yet, Iweala has chops. *Beasts of No Nation* was not exactly a gentle bedtime story. If he brings that same unflinching cruelty to the American obsession with self-mythology, we might actually get something worth reading instead of another centrist lament about how we just need to listen to each other across the aisle. God, the mere thought of bipartisan dialogue makes my teeth itch. 

### The Expat Gaze and Other Convenient Stances

There is always a distinct literary tourism to books written about America by people who currently reside somewhere else. James Baldwin did it with style. Ta-Nehisi Coates circled the globe while interrogating the machinery of citizenship. Iweala positions himself as the insider-outsider — a Black American of Nigerian descent holding the stethoscope to a thrashing patient. 

Expect the prose to be sharp. The synopsis guarantees maturity. But watch out for the inevitable trap of essayistic hand-wringing. Every literary critic in New York is going to call it *essential* and *timely* because those are the only two adjectives allowed in the trades anymore. They will compare it to Joan Didion or Baldwin without pausing to ask if essays can still pierce a culture that has successfully commodified its own collapse. 

Will it capture the doubt and uncertainty? Absolutely. Doubt is very profitable right now. Confusion is a genre. We package our national neurosis into hardcovers and put them on coffee tables next to artisanal candles that smell like cedar and regret. 

I just hope the essays have some actual bite rather than floating away on the safe, lofty clouds of transnational academia. We do not need another sophisticated shrug about the state of our union. We need someone to kick the tires. 

And now the radiator in this pre-war deathtrap is banging like a disgruntled percussionist, meaning I have to spend the next twenty minutes bleeding the valve or freeze to death.
