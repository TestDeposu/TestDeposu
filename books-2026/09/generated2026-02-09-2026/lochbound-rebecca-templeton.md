**Author:** Rebecca Templeton  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Lochbound* is a masterclass in how to sell a gothic fairy tale without actually telling you anything—except that it’s going to be *dark*, which is either a promise or a warning, depending on how much you trust publishers these days. The fact that it’s billed as a "glitteringly dark reimagining" of *The Little Mermaid* is either a stroke of genius or a desperate attempt to ride the coattails of Disney’s endless regurgitation of the same three fairy tales. But let’s be honest—if this were just another *Mermaid* retelling, it would be forgettable. The real hook here is the setting: eighteenth-century Scotland, which immediately elevates it from "another cursed woman" to "another cursed woman in a landscape that will either haunt you or make you want to move to the Highlands." The choice is yours.

The premise hinges on a duality that feels both fresh and familiar—by day, a monster; by night, a woman. It’s the kind of binary that’s been mined ad nauseam in fantasy, but the execution here could either be *Spellbound* meets *Outlander*—if the author has the chops to balance the historical grit with the supernatural dread—or it could be *The Water Horse* with more angst. The problem with synopses like this is that they promise a *game* of impossible attraction between Iris and Henry, but they don’t say whether that attraction is the kind that makes you want to read on or the kind that makes you want to throw the book into the loch. The mention of a "figure from Iris’s past" reappearing is the kind of vague threat that could either be the inciting incident of a brilliant twist or the lazy setup for a deus ex machina. Either way, it’s the kind of thing that makes me wonder if the author has actually written this yet or if they’re just playing with tropes like a child with a box of LEGOs.

But let’s talk about the *real* issue here—the vagueness. The synopsis doesn’t tell us what the curse is, how it works, or why Iris has accepted it for fifty years. Is she resigned? Is she complicit? Is she just tired? The lack of specificity is frustrating because it leaves the reader with more questions than answers, which is either a sign of a brilliant, ambiguous narrative or a sign that the author hasn’t figured out the story yet. Compare this to something like *The Cruel Prince*, where the synopsis drops you into the world with enough detail to make you care immediately. Here, we’re left with a monster, a hunter, and a loch—three things that could go anywhere. The fact that the hunter is described as "rugged, ruthless" is either a red flag for a brooding love interest or a green light for a villain who might actually be the real monster here. The ambiguity is delicious, but it’s also exhausting.

And then there’s the *tone*—gothic, historical, fantasy. It’s the kind of mashup that either works beautifully or falls flat like a poorly baked scone. Think about it: *Outlander* is a masterclass in blending history and romance, but it’s also a time machine that could’ve been called *The Highlander* and still sold the same number of copies. *Spellbound* is a fresh take on fae and magic, but it’s also a book that could’ve been called *The Fae’s Bargain* and still been just as compelling. The difference? The authors of those books had the discipline to make their worlds feel lived-in. This synopsis doesn’t give us that. It gives us a *feeling*—dark, misty, dangerous—but no substance.

But here’s the thing: I’m *intrigued*. Not because the synopsis is detailed, but because it’s *mysterious*. The fact that it doesn’t tell us everything is either a sign of a brilliant, layered story or a sign that the author is too busy playing with words to actually write the book. Either way, I’m curious. I just hope it’s not another *The Secret History* knockoff where the gothic setting is just a backdrop for a bunch of privileged characters arguing about philosophy. Because if that’s the case, I’m going to be very disappointed.

And speaking of *The Secret History*, have you ever noticed how every gothic fantasy these days is either set in a boarding school or a cursed estate? It’s like the genre has a limited palette. *Lochbound* could break that mold by setting its story in the Scottish Highlands, where the real monsters are the weather, the history, and the fact that you can’t just walk away from a curse like that. But then again, maybe the author will just stick to the usual suspects—fae, curses, betrayal—and call it a day. I hope not.

But let’s be real—this book isn’t out yet, and I’m already tired of waiting for it. The synopsis is promising, but it’s also frustratingly vague. It’s like being given a menu with no prices—you know there’s something good on there, but you have no idea what it’ll cost. And right now, the cost of reading a book that might not deliver is too high.
