**Page Count:** Unknown

Steaming espresso and stale airport lounge air make for a dreadful cocktail. Yet here we are, dissecting Natasha Ngan’s *Such Familiar Magic* between delayed flights to Geneva. The publishing machine churns onward, coughing up another iteration of the overworked protagonist seeking solace in domesticity. 

### Ancestral Chores and Corporate Burnout

Laila Chu escapes London to wash dishes for dead people in Silverwood Cove. How utterly riveting. We are asked to swallow the premise that an eight-hundred-year-old magical tradition can be casually shattered on Day One because a modern woman is tired. Burnout is the new aristocracy of suffering. Every protagonist must now be an exhausted shell of a human before the inciting incident even dares to knock. Ngan trades the opulent, sprawling worldbuilding of *Girls of Paper and Fire* for something localized, coastal, and desperately reliant on the cozy fantasy industrial complex that currently plagues every independent bookstore window from Chelsea to Kyoto. 

### The Enmity Index

Enter Anthony Lim. The next-door nemesis. Because two people who grew up sharing a zip code could never simply ignore one another without a mandated trope compelling them toward forced proximity. The synopsis leans heavily on the structural architecture of the forced-cooperation rom-com—a formula popularized by people who think Nora Ephron wrote instruction manuals. They hunt for elusive ingredients. They banter. Sparks fly. One expects the narrative to disintegrate into a predictable sequence of culinary-adjacent magical mishaps that read less like prose and more like an episode of *Top Chef* sponsored by the Wicca supply chain. It has the distinct flavor of the Costa Concordia captain trying to parallel park. 

### The Transience of Spells

The underlying stakes supposedly involve the sanctity of familial lineage and cultural preservation across eight centuries. But let us be entirely serious. The emotional gravity is entirely eclipsed by the looming specter of a ticking clock. Will she fix the potion before her parents return? Will she fall too hard for a man who is only here for the magic? This is the literary equivalent of a Diet Coke consumed while staring blankly at a departures board. It asks for nothing, risks nothing, and dissolves on the tongue before the final page is turned. Give me merciless ambition or give me the recycling bin.
