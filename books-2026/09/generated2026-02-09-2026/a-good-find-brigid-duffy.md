**Author:** Brigid Duffy  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis of *A Good Find* arrives like a well-worn leather-bound volume from a secondhand shop—promising something elegant, but already bearing the faintest scent of dust from a life half-lived. Beverly Atwood, a woman whose grief has settled into the quiet corners of her home like a cat that’s decided to claim the couch, stumbles into an online romance with a man named George. The setup is *so* 2010s, the kind of plot that would make a *Golden Globes* host roll their eyes while sipping a martini. But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t *tell* us what happens next—it just drops us into the wreckage of a woman’s life, where the real drama isn’t the romance but the slow unraveling of her own memory.

The prose here is *deliberately* vague, which is either a sign of brilliance or a cop-out. We’re left to imagine the weight of Bev’s antiques—do they whisper secrets? Do they scream? The synopsis hints at a *Fatal Attraction* meets *Big Little Lies* energy, where the past isn’t just a ghost but a landmine waiting to detonate. And yet, the most interesting line is the one about Bev confronting the "true identity of her suitor." Oh, *please*—this is the kind of reveal that would make a *Scandal* spin doctor blush. But the real question is: does Duffy have the chops to make this more than just another *SilverFox.com* cautionary tale?

The thematic potential is *there*, buried under layers of cliché. Grief as a character? Check. The unreliable narrator trope? Double-check. But where’s the *substance*? Where’s the *bite*? The synopsis reads like a *Hallmark Channel* script that’s been given a single, desperate attempt at sophistication. We’ve seen this movie before—*The Notebook* meets *The Secret History*, but with the emotional depth of a *Netflix original* that gets canceled after two seasons.

The most intriguing part? The way Bev’s antiques become a metaphor for her life. Are they just objects, or are they *people*? The synopsis doesn’t say. And that’s the problem—it’s too busy setting up a mystery to actually *explore* one. Where’s the *Twilight Zone* twist? Where’s the *Lost* level of existential dread? Instead, we get a woman selling her soul for a little love, which is *so* 1980s it’s almost quaint.

The real question is: does Duffy have the audacity to make this more than just another *widow finds love* story? Or is this just another *rom-com* with a side of existential dread, like a *Bridesmaids* script written by someone who’s never actually been lonely?

And then there’s the *George* reveal—because of course, the man who’s not what he seems is the *real* villain here. But where’s the *psychological* depth? Where’s the *Shakespearean* betrayal? The synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at it. It’s all just *so* surface-level, like a *Tinder profile* that’s been copy-pasted from a *romance novel*.

The most frustrating part? The way the synopsis *leaves us hanging*. Is Bev’s daughter the *real* antagonist? Is George a *serial killer* in disguise? Or is this just another *misunderstood romance* that ends with everyone hugging it out? The synopsis doesn’t say. And that’s the problem—it’s too busy *promising* drama to actually *deliver* it.

But let’s be honest: the real question is whether Duffy can turn this into something more than just another *widow’s tale*. Because right now, it feels like a *Netflix* script that’s been given a single, desperate attempt at *literary* gravitas. And that’s *so* not enough.

I must sign divorce papers today. The paperwork is tedious, but at least it’s *not* this vague.
