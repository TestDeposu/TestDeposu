September air smells like damp earth and scorched sugar. The flour is currently clinging to my eyelashes, and I honestly do not care if the counter looks like a war zone because *Oxford Secrets* is waltzing into 2026 like it owns the place. 

### The Illusion of the Ivory Tower

Rachael Davis-Featherstone is back at it, dragging us straight back to the damp stones of academia where people apparently do nothing except murder each other and write essays. And honestly? Good for them. Give me Agatha Christie any day of the week over these modern teenagers whining about their follower counts. But here we are. Eva is dodging feelings for Xander—because nothing says romance like sharing a mutual trauma over a dead friend—while the digital panopticon closes in. 

My seven-year-old just tried to feed a raw potato to the stand mixer, and quite frankly, the kitchen appliance showed better survival instincts than anyone stepping foot on that Oxford campus. 

People always talk about privilege like it is some abstract painting hanging in a gallery nobody visits. But Davis-Featherstone understands the dirty, grease-stained reality of it. When anonymous student gossip forums get hacked, the panic isn't about ethics; it's about exposure. A classic trope, yes. Yet I will devour it like stolen cookie dough eaten straight from the bowl at two in the morning while staring blankly at the wall. 

### The Digital Guillotine

The synopsis relies heavily on vague threats of "deadly secrets" and spilled blood, which is code for "we haven't finished writing the middle chapters yet, but trust us, the vibes are immaculate." Give me a break. Just tell us who pushes whom down the bell tower. But no, we get the whole song and dance about student well-being and digital toxicity. 

Yet there is something undeniably delicious about watching arrogant eighteen-year-olds panic because their burner accounts got leaked. It reminds me of the PTA WhatsApp group drama from last term, except with more tweed and fewer passive-aggressive emojis of wine glasses. 

Davis-Featherstone is chasing the high of *The Secret History* by Donna Tartt, though with a decidedly millennial social media panic attack tacked on for good measure. Will the plot hold together under the weight of its own pretentious terminology? Absolutely not. Do I care? Absolutely not. 

Now I have to go negotiate the Geneva Convention with a four-year-old who is currently treating the bathtub like a maximum-security prison yard.
