**Author:** Alex Sigmore  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The bus’s AC wheezes like a dying animal, and the scent of stale coffee and regret clings to the air—just like the kind of prose that makes you wonder if the author ever actually *lived* in the Pacific Northwest. Because here’s the thing: *Buried Embers* isn’t just another thriller about a fire investigator. It’s a slow-burn (pun absolutely intended) meditation on grief, suspicion, and the way small towns hide their rot behind smiles and pine-scented air. The synopsis leans hard into the "brilliant but troubled" trope, which is fine—we’ve seen it before in books like *The Dry* by Jane Harper, where the protagonist’s trauma is the engine, not just the grease. But where Harper’s protagonist is a cop drowning in bureaucracy, Calla Rowe is a wildfire investigator, which immediately shifts the metaphor from legal corruption to ecological destruction. And that’s where the synopsis stumbles—because it doesn’t *show* us the fire. It tells us there’s a fire. It tells us there’s a web of treachery. It tells us Calla is hiding in a remote Oregon town, which sounds like a cliché until you realize how little we know about *why* she’s there.

The synopsis is frustratingly vague about the mechanics of her trauma. Was her partner’s death a botched investigation? A personal vendetta? The logging company’s involvement? Right now, it reads like a checklist of thriller tropes—arson, disappearances, environmental activism—without the tension of a real mystery. It’s the difference between a *True Detective* episode and a *Dateline* rerun. And yet, the promise of a "shocking twist" lingers, which is either a sign of confidence or a desperate attempt to sell a book that hasn’t quite found its footing yet. Because here’s the thing about wildfire thrillers: they’re either *The Girl on the Train* meets *Firestarter* or they’re nothing. The synopsis doesn’t lean into the science—how does Calla’s expertise in accelerants and burn patterns make her the key to unraveling this? Is she a MacGyver of fire science, or is she just another detective with a fancy degree? The ambiguity is maddening, like trying to read a map in the dark.

But then—*and this is where I pivot*—maybe that’s the point. Maybe the vagueness is intentional, a reflection of Calla’s own fractured state. She’s hiding in Pinecrest, but is she really hiding, or is she just waiting for the next blaze to pull her back in? The synopsis doesn’t answer that, but it doesn’t have to. It’s a setup, not a resolution. And that’s where the real potential lies. If Sigmore—who, by the way, already has a solid reputation with his FBI series—can make us care about Calla’s hesitation, her fear of getting burned again, then this could be something. Because the best thrillers aren’t about the mystery; they’re about the protagonist’s relationship to it. Think *Gone Girl*’s Amy Dunne, but with a chainsaw instead of a pen. The synopsis doesn’t give us that yet, but it’s a start.

And then there’s the environmental angle, which is either a bold stroke or a gimmick. The logging company’s secret old-growth timber operation? That’s not just a plot device—it’s a real-world tension, one that’s been simmering in the Pacific Northwest for decades. If Sigmore can weave that into the thriller without feeling preachy, he’ll have something. But if it’s just another layer of "corrupt industry" tropes, it’ll feel like a cheap nod to current events. The synopsis doesn’t tell us how deep the company’s involvement goes—is it arson, sabotage, or something worse? That’s the kind of ambiguity that makes a thriller *work*, but right now, it’s just another question hanging in the smoke.

I have to stop writing this now because the bus is pulling into a station and I have to go rescue a stack of vinyl records from a damp basement before they turn into moldy relics of my own neglect. But if *Buried Embers* delivers on the promise of a protagonist who’s as complicated as the fires she investigates, it could be the kind of book that lingers like the scent of pine after a burn. Or it could be another forgettable ride through the woods. Only time—and the next blaze—will tell.
