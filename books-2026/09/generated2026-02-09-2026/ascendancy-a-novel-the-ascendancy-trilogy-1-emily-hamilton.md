Emily Hamilton's *Ascendancy* arrives with the heavy, glittering burden of comparison to Tamsyn Muir and Arkady Martine, which usually means the literary marketing machine is trying to sell us a prefabricated revolution. But let us look at the raw structural mechanics of this synopsis. We have Neev—a pilot chipped, monitored, and effectively abandoned by a maternal figure at an academy on Jupiter's moons—and Ida, a sword-wielding product of a transhumanist death cult. A classic case of overpromising. Because how many times must we watch the dutiful institutional cog fall for the feral, leather-clad dissident, only for fate to tear them apart by page fifty just so they can stare at each other across opposing barricades a decade later? 

And speaking of institutional abandonment, my youngest spent forty-five minutes this morning crying because his oatmeal was brown instead of white, completely melting down over a reality he refused to accept, which honestly feels less exhausting than reading another sprawling space opera where the salvation of the entire species apparently hinges on whether two traumatized ex-lovers can communicate their feelings instead of shooting at each other with plasma rifles. Why must every cosmic conflict be a thinly veiled metaphor for bad childhood attachment styles? Just fix the leaky infrastructure of your fictional Jovian moons and stop weaponizing daddy issues against the proletariat.

### The Anatomy of Engineered Longing

The thematic architecture here relies entirely on the worn-out dichotomy between authoritarian surveillance and violent anarchism. The synopsis breathlessly trades in buzzwords: the Neionet, chips in heads, iron grips, and the hollow promise of technology. Yet it refuses to examine the actual mess of institutional dependency. When a child learns never to question the invisible architecture keeping them alive, the trauma isn't just political—it is cellular. Yet Hamilton seems more interested in the "blazing romance" and the "power of the erotic" than the agonizing, tedious reality of unlearning a lifetime of neurological conditioning. Love does not overthrow fascist councils. Concrete does. 

### The Inevitable Betrayal of Happy Endings

Expect a tragic, blood-soaked cliffhanger masquerading as emotional depth. Anyone promising that a queer cyberpunk space opera will unite humanity with a single, sweeping romance is selling a fairy tale to adults who should know better. People who grow up in death cults do not just walk away because a handsome rebel pilot smiles at them; they spend decades flinching at sudden movements in quiet rooms. But the market demands glossy, high-stakes melodrama where a blade to the heart heals all systemic injuries. I refuse to buy into the marketing hype. 

I really must go outside now and untangle the garden hose that has tied itself into a Gordian knot across the back patio, because unlike the characters in this trilogy, at least that catastrophe has a physical logic I can unravel before sunset.
