**Author:** Adele Myers  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Adolescence is a singular state of being—that strange, porous period where one is constantly molded by the hands of others, often under the guise of "improvement" or "social grace." *Belle Haven Finishing School* promises to examine this precise friction, pitting the fragile agency of orphans against the systemic machinery of 1951, a year that smelled of starch and secrets. One wonders if Adele Myers captures the specific, crushing weight of a teacher’s approval—that hollow gold star—or if she leans too heavily into the "gothic mystery" aesthetic that has become so tiresome in contemporary commercial fiction. The dual-timeline structure, jumping from the mid-century to 1987, feels like a deliberate attempt to graft the journalistic rigor of something like *Lessons in Chemistry* onto a darker, more institutional critique. But is a cover-up truly a cover-up if it follows the exact blueprint of every institutional thriller released in the last decade? The synopsis teases a "prominent university professor" behind an unethical experiment, a trope that feels dangerously thin, perhaps even lazy, in an era where we have already been served the grim realities of the Tuskegee Study and MKUltra. Charlotte’s quest for truth in the late eighties serves as the cynical mirror to Birdie’s optimism, yet I worry the narrative might prioritize the "mystery" over the genuinely uncomfortable psychological disintegration of the girls themselves. Why must we always look for a hidden study? Why isn't the banality of the finishing school—the sheer, weaponized dullness of being taught how to walk with a book on one’s head—enough to fuel a tragedy? 

Actually, the mystery might be the saving grace here, provided the prose avoids the cloying sentimentality that often infects Southern historical fiction. 

Are we reading the same book? I suspect the finished product will swing wildly between a blistering critique of mid-century patriarchy and a rather pedestrian mystery involving a lost folder in an archive. It is a bold move to ground this in 1951, a year ripe for the tension between domestic stifling and budding rebellion, yet one fears the inevitable "courageous woman uncovering the truth" beat will overshadow the quieter, more terrifying reality of what it means to lose one's identity to a brand. If Myers can summon the sharp, clinical observations found in Shirley Jackson’s work—rather than the soft-focus nostalgia of a standard historical romance—she might have something worth discussing during my next department meeting. If not, it will be just another artifact for the bargain bin, alongside the endless parade of "forgotten women" stories that claim to empower us while merely recycling the same Victorian-era gender traps. 

Pedestrian.

I must cut this short before the ink dries. I hear a faint, rhythmic smacking sound coming from the living room—the unmistakable signal that my grandson has liberated my favorite fountain pen and is currently testing its flow on his own thumb.
