**Author:** Maulik Pancholy  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Maulik Pancholy is returning with *Will They, Won't They*, and I am already clearing space on my bedside table, hoping against hope that this doesn't crumble under the weight of its own high-concept premise. We are looking at a collision between an earnest documentarian from Queens and a Mumbai nepo baby, which sounds suspiciously like the kind of pitch that gets greenlit in a very expensive Los Angeles boardroom over oat milk lattes. Yet, there is a distinct tender ache underneath the glossy veneer of Bollywood exposure. Ajit Jain wants to dismantle the system with a brutal cinematic exposé. Zain Mansoor just wants to survive the crushing expectations of a generational dynasty. Or at least, that is what the promotional copy would have us believe before the mandatory motorcycle ride changes the trajectory of human history.

Let us be entirely honest with one another for a fleeting second. Enemies-to-lovers stories are notoriously difficult to pull off when the antagonists are forced into proximity by a literal camera lens. The synopsis leans heavily on the tired trope of the righteous artist softening at the exact moment his subject flashes a vulnerable smile, which makes me sigh into my lukewarm Earl Grey. Wait. Let me take that back entirely. Perhaps the forced proximity isn't a crutch at all, but a pressure cooker designed to test the fragile boundaries of artistic integrity against the intoxicating allure of dynastic privilege, forcing these boys to reckon with the mirrors they hold up to each other. 

Predictable.

### The Machinery of Longing

The thematic architecture here relies entirely on the friction between public myth-making and private reality. Pancholy is positioning the glittering chaos of the Mumbai film industry not just as a backdrop, but as a third character in this romance—a monstrous, beautiful entity that demands constant sacrifice from the people feeding its engine. Ajit brings the outsider's sharp, unblinking lens, armed with the protective armor of Queens cynicism and a desperate need for institutional validation. Zain carries the invisible bruises of a bloodline that never allowed him to fail quietly. They are two boys weaponizing cameras and reputations against each other, completely blind to the fact that their mutual antagonism is just another form of intense, terrifying focus. Think *Red, White & Royal Blue* meets *The Sun Is Also a Star*, but filtered through the breathless, high-stakes anxiety of a film set where everyone is watching your every move. We have seen this cinematic waltz before in adult romance, but bringing it into the YA space with queer South Asian protagonists feels long overdue. The tension between wanting to destroy someone and wanting to kiss them in the rain is practically Shakespearean, even if it occasionally traffics in the kind of YA shorthand that makes grown readers roll their eyes into the next dimension. 

Hope.

I am going to hide in my study for exactly ten minutes of uninterrupted silence.
