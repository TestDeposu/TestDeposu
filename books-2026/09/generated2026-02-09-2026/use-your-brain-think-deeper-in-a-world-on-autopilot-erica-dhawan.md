Staring at this blinding white document feels like trying to paint with a vacuum. I am supposed to string together a few hundred words about *Use Your Brain* by Erica Dhawan — a title that sounds less like literature and more like a warning label on a microwave — just to pad out my word count and scrape together enough cash for my landlord. Brilliant. Let us pretend I care about corporate leadership handbooks when my canvas has been blank for three weeks and my coffee tastes like burnt ash. 

### The Cult of the Prompt

The synopsis leans hard into our collective intellectual decay. We are outsourcing our minds to machines that do not care. Groundbreaking stuff. We are trading the friction of original thought for the slick, greasy convenience of automated summaries. Dhawan apparently wants us to pick up new tools and then put them down — revolutionary advice that has somehow become a marketable commodity in boardrooms. If you need a globally recognized leadership expert to tell you not to let an algorithm write your emails, you are already too far gone. 

Yet the premise touches a raw nerve. Look at Cal Newport’s *Digital Minimalism* or Sherry Turkle’s work on how technology erodes our capacity for solitude. We are terrified of quiet. And so we fill the void with prompts, filters, and synthetic confidence. The book promises common-sense habits to protect our judgment. But can a habit fix a systemic numbness? Doubtful. 

### Marketing the Antidote

What fascinates me — or what would fascinate me if my emotions were not entirely fried — is the irony of packaging anti-technology advice into a marketable commodity designed to feed the very machine it critiques. People will buy this book on an e-reader, highlight a few pithy sentences about touching grass, and then immediately return to scrolling. Dhawan frames the loss of critical thinking as a personal failure of discipline rather than an inevitable consequence of late-stage digital dependency. It is much easier to sell a field guide for sharpening creativity than it is to admit the system is rigged to keep us docile. 

We are raising children who freeze at open-ended questions. That sentence in the blurb actually hit something. It is the only part of the synopsis that carries genuine weight — the generational atrophy of simply sitting in the discomfort of not knowing. Everything else is standard corporate self-help boilerplate designed to make middle managers feel productive while they read during quarterly reviews. 

I need to write two hundred more words just to satisfy the editors who think every cultural symptom can be cured by a five-step habit loop. It is exhausting. We decorate our digital cages with self-help paperbacks and call it resistance. 

I must attend a terribly pretentious avant-garde film screening where people stare at a blank screen for two hours and call it a profound commentary on modern alienation, which honestly sounds preferable to reading another paragraph about catching yourself on autopilot.
