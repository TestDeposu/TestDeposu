**Author:** J.M. Voris  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

September always smells like roasted coffee beans and damp wool coats, especially when you are sitting by the window watching the neighborhood teenagers argue about college applications while you desperately try to unpack a synopsis that sounds like a massive pitch meeting at a streaming service where executives just threw Greek mythology into a blender with a history textbook and hoped the glass wouldn't crack. J.M. Voris gives us *Into the Burning Deep*, a sapphic reimagining of Orpheus and Eurydice set on a quarantined steamship called *The Lyre*, and honestly, I am already exhausted by the sheer mathematical probability of these tropes colliding in a way that either completely reinvents speculative fiction or crashes terribly against the rocks of YA commercialism. Adrian Nix is an oil-splattered mechanic dodging dark magic from a ruined homeland—because no protagonist is allowed to just have a normal job anymore without carrying the literal geopolitical trauma of an entire continent on their grease-stained shoulders—and then along comes Ophelia Jones, a rebellious socialite betrothed to a wealthy governor who exists purely to be the sun, the moon, and the tragic catalyst for a maritime rescue mission. And why do characters in these books always have terrifying dreams of sinister voices whispering in the bilges? Can we not just have two people fall in love over burnt toast and bad ship rations without a ghostly vessel luring someone across the waves with a siren song of vague, unspecified stakes? I said earlier that this premise feels tired, but wait, actually, the claustrophobia of a floating quarantine zone offers an genuinely brilliant container for romantic tension, trapping two entirely different social strata together until the pressure cooker literally explodes into the supernatural.

Vulnerable.

The synopsis leans heavily on mood—oil-splattered hands, haunted corners, burning deeps—without actually telling us how the magic functions, which usually means the world-building is going to rely entirely on vibes and emotional resonance rather than hard mechanical rules, a frustrating trend I also noticed in recent releases by Tamsyn Muir and Shelley Parker-Chan where aesthetic completely overpowers internal logic. 

Now if you will excuse me, I need to put on a heavier cardigan and walk three blocks down the hill before the local artisanal cafe closes because I desperately need a decent cup of matcha in this utterly gentrified neighborhood.
