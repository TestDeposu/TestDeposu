**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Amsterdam. The sheer banality of a "creative" life in the Netherlands—cycling past canals while nursing a hollowed-out sense of self—is almost too perfectly curated to be true. Genevieve Hudson’s *Headfirst* arrives like a sudden, humid gust of air from the American South, threatening to disrupt the sanitized, European aestheticism that people of a certain tax bracket seem to prize above oxygen. We are promised a portrait of Reed, a woman who has traded the jagged edges of her identity for the soft, beige velvet of a domestic "goal." It’s an old story, really. The classic trajectory of the expatriate who realizes that running across an ocean doesn't actually stop the internal monologue from screaming.

### The Geography of Regret

Charleston serves as the inevitable pressure cooker here. It is a city that reeks of humidity and unresolved history, the perfect backdrop for a collision with a past lover. Hudson’s previous work—that magnetic, Kristen Stewart-coded prose—suggests we should expect a jagged exploration of desire that refuses to apologize for its own messy, high-key toxicity. But wait, perhaps the setting is irrelevant. Maybe the geography doesn't matter; it is the internal architecture of the protagonist that dictates the rot. Actually, the setting is the only thing that matters, as if the physical humidity itself forces the emotional repression to evaporate. 

The synopsis teeters on the edge of a trope I have seen performed a thousand times at gallery openings in SoHo: the "should I stay or should I go" pivot. It is low-stakes existentialism masquerading as a grand life crisis. I find the premise mildly infuriating yet undeniably alluring. It’s the literary equivalent of a Birkin bag—frivolous, overpriced, and I absolutely need to possess it immediately. Will Reed actually choose between her paralyzing anxiety and her latent, feral desires? I doubt it. If she makes a clear choice, she loses the ambiguity that makes her interesting. If she doesn't, she’s just another vacuous character lost in a bourgeois fever dream. 

Hudson is positioning herself in the league of writers like Raven Leilani or maybe a stripped-back, queer-coded Ottessa Moshfegh. *Headfirst* seems poised to deconstruct the "good life" until it looks like a pile of damp receipts. I suspect the novel will be less about the actual romance—the "one who got away" is usually a hollow vessel for the protagonist's projected ego—and more about the performative nature of adulthood. It is a bold, sus flex for an author to center a narrative on the cowardice of comfort. 

The prose will likely be sharp, slicing through the pretension of ad-agency aesthetics like a blade through silk. I anticipate a reckoning that feels less like catharsis and more like a migraine. One doesn't read this for the plot; one reads it to see how many social hierarchies the author can dismantle before the final page.
