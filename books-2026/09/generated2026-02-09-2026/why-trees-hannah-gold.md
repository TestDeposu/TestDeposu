**Author:** Hannah Gold  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Why Trees* is so deliberately vague it’s almost a performance in itself—like a character in one of its own stories refusing to name the thing they’re holding, just to see if you’ll ask. And you will ask. You *always* ask. The blurb’s own contradictions—“brilliant, unexpected writer” vs. “almost irritatingly funny”—feel like a writer’s note slipped into the manuscript: *Don’t trust the hype, but don’t ignore it either.* It’s the kind of synopsis that makes you wonder if Gold’s first collection is a trick, a dare, or just another New York City character trying to outmaneuver the reader before the first page even turns.

The tone here is *Rejection* meets *Bad Behavior*, but with the kind of precision that suggests Gold’s actually read the footnotes of those books—like she’s standing in the back of a room where Tulathimutte and Gaitskill are arguing about who gets to be the funnier one, and she’s taking notes. The synopsis leans into the city as a character, but not in the usual way. It’s not about skyscrapers or subway grinds; it’s about the *performances* of survival. A night blogger using her boyfriend as a “writing chair”—that’s not just a quirk, that’s a metaphor for how we all prop each other up in this city, even when we’re collapsing under the weight of our own absurdity. And the Ellis Island encampment? That’s not a setting, that’s a pressure test. The journalist isn’t just chasing a story; they’re chasing the last shred of relevance in a world where even the news is dying.

The real question is whether Gold can deliver on the promise of *Why Trees* being “genre-bending.” The synopsis doesn’t give us enough to say for sure—it’s more of a mood board than a blueprint. But the way it oscillates between the clinical (“financial and emotional insecurity”) and the visceral (“pure chaos”) suggests a writer who understands that New York isn’t just a place; it’s a mood disorder. The characters here aren’t suffering from depression—they’re suffering from *propinquity*, that inescapable proximity that turns strangers into acquaintances and acquaintances into enemies. And the “gigawattage of wit” Tulathimutte mentions? That’s not just humor; it’s the kind of sharpness that could cut through the city’s noise like a scalpel.

Yet the synopsis’s biggest flaw is its refusal to commit. It’s like a character who keeps changing their mind about whether they’re an artist or a grifter—you can’t tell which one they’ll be by the end. The “almost irritatingly funny” line is either a backhanded compliment or a warning: this book might be so sharp it’ll leave a paper cut on your soul. And the “people suffering extreme emotional states” line? That’s not just a theme, that’s a challenge. Gold’s got to prove she can make the city’s chaos feel intimate, not just observational.

I’m betting she does. But only if she stops treating the synopsis like a press release and starts treating it like a story itself—one where the real punchline is that no one knows what’s coming next. Because that’s the thing about New York: it’s not just a place, it’s a bet you can’t afford to lose.
