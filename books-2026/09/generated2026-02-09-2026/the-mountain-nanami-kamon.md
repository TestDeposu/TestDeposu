**Author:** Nanami Kamon  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Staring at the flickering fluorescent tube above this sticky library carrel while a fluorescent-vested city worker aggressively leaf-blows the pathetic September drizzle outside, my mind drifts to Nanami Kamon’s upcoming *The Mountain*. God, the autumn air smells like damp wool and municipal defeat. But let us discuss the literary meat here. 

### The Haunting of the Professional Woman

Minami is a horror writer suffering from the most profoundly gendered curse known to modern capitalism: she has a blank document and a deadline. And what does she do? She answers a middle-of-the-night distress call from an old female friend, Asako—because patriarchy taught us that women must perform unpaid emotional labor even when dodging specters from a cursed sawmill. Classic. 

Kampon’s premise relies heavily on the tired trope of the haunted female collective—think Shirley Jackson meets Ring-era hyper-fixations, except translated for an English-speaking audience finally ready to look past Murakami's manic pixie dream ghosts. Yet, I am side-eyeing the marketing copy. The synopsis gives us zero actual narrative teeth, relying entirely on the generic promise that "the mountain is calling them back." Wow. Groundbreaking. Tell me more about the rocks, corporate publishing. 

### Folk Horror and the Male Gaze on Nature

Japan’s rich lineage of yama-uba folklore—mountain hags, wild women living at the fringes of civilized patriarchal safety—deserves a radical feminist deconstruction. Will Kamon deliver this? Or will we just get generic wet-haired phantoms crawling out of corporate water coolers? The juxtaposition of a professional horror writer hunting for inspiration by exploiting her female peer's trauma is delicious. It exposes the grift of the trauma economy. We monetize our collective suffering, wrap it in a pretty paperback cover, and sell it to suburban ghouls looking for a thrill before bedtime. 

Minami thinks she is too smart to get dragged in. She sits in her ivory tower of supernatural expertise until the phantoms come for her, too. Slay, queen, get terrorized by indigenous mountain curses. The epistolary trigger—an email out of the blue—is aggressively mundane. It grounds the supernatural dread in the absolute worst part of contemporary existence: digital correspondence that requires a emotional response. 

Expect *The Mountain* to either completely redefine J-horror translation standards or completely lean into the exhausted tropes of cursed real estate. I am betting five crisp dollars it does the latter, wrapped in some gorgeous minimalist cover art that looks great on an Instagram grid. 

Anyway, I need to close this laptop immediately and aggressively march down to the municipal park before dusk so I can forage for edible chanterelles before the city sprays them with pesticides.
