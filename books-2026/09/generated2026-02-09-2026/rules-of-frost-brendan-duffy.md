Isolation is the ultimate litmus test for human hierarchy. *Rules of Frost* dangles the bait of a winter-locked boarding school, promising a claustrophobic descent into the brittle, performative power dynamics that define these echo chambers. Brendan Duffy seems intent on weaponizing the "snowed-in" trope, yet the synopsis leans heavily on the generic promise of a hidden game. Are we truly expected to tremble at the prospect of teenagers—already the most vicious, ritual-obsessed architects of social exclusion—playing with fire? 

### The Architecture of Enforced Proximity

The narrative hook relies on the classic teacher-as-authority figure, yet Jon Sayers is clearly poised for a spectacular failure of governance. Boarding school fiction thrives on the lie that adults hold the keys to the kingdom. In reality, they are merely observers to the localized feudalism of dormitories. If the "secret game" involves anything less than a total subversion of the faculty’s pathetic attempts at holiday cheer, the book will collapse under its own weight. I am bracing for a narrative that mistakes atmospheric gloom for genuine structural critique. 

Do you remember *The Secret History*? Donna Tartt managed to make the pretension of academic elitism feel like a slow-motion car crash. *Rules of Frost* smells like a derivative cousin, aiming for that same icy, intellectual rot. If Duffy wants to survive the comparisons, he needs to avoid the tired "teacher with a dark past" cliché. Give us a system—not a man—that breaks. Give us the cold, hard logic of why kids find solace in ritualized violence when the lights go out. 

Honestly, it’s no different than watching that wretched *Caillou* series where the parents just smile while the child exerts his tiny, tyrannical will over the household, demanding cookies while the world burns. At least in cartoons, the screaming stops when you turn off the screen; here, we are trapped in the pedagogical equivalent of a fever dream about compliance and ritual. Back to the freezing pond: I suspect the boy at the center of this will matter less than the way the remaining students decide to rewrite the social contract in his absence.

I predict this will be the year’s most frustrating exercise in ambiguity. The synopsis avoids specificity with the grace of a politician dodging a tax audit. "Something else entirely"? Spare me. If it’s a ghost story, I’m out. If it’s a brutal, psychological evisceration of the boarding school caste system, then perhaps there is a flicker of utility in the prose. 

I’m currently staring at the water stains on my walls, which look suspiciously like a map of the campus Duffy is building. I have to go deal with my landlord about the persistent leak in the ceiling before the plaster actually gives way.
