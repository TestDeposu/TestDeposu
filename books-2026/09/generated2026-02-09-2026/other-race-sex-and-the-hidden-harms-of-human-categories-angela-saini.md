The gymnasium radiator is hissing against the September chill outside, and I am currently staring at a purple neon permission slip for a field trip to the sewage treatment plant that a twelve-year-old just tried to forge with a scented gel pen. Honestly, the cursive loop on the 'M' looked like a drowning squid. But returning to Angela Saini’s upcoming *Other*, the synopsis outlines a thesis that feels remarkably pressing while I sit here watching fluorescent lights flicker over folding chairs, wondering how we ever convinced ourselves that stuffing human beings into rigid administrative boxes was a stroke of genius rather than a bureaucratic trap. We spend our entire lives handing over demographic data to doctors, state registries, and algorithms under the comforting delusion that checking a neat little demographic box helps dismantle inequality, when Saini is gearing up to argue that these exact categories are quietly being weaponized by bad actors from Washington to Budapest to enforce gerrymandering, restrict bodily autonomy, and resurrect archaic caste systems just in time for the 2027 census rollout. 

It reminds me of that dreadful young adult dystopian trilogy—you know the one, where teenagers are violently sorted into factions based on whether they are brave or good at math, as if human personality can be neatly sliced up like a cafeteria rectangle of lukewarm pizza—and frankly, this book looks poised to do what spec-fic has been warning about for a decade, except with actual receipts from Palantir and global biometric databases. This is why we can't have nice things. We take an idealistic concept from the sixties meant to guarantee civil rights, hand the data collection keys over to opaque tech monopolies and rogue race-science researchers hunting for spurious genetic correlations, and then act entirely shocked when the machinery gets twisted back into an instrument of exclusion. Saini is not writing a comforting self-help manifesto about embracing our differences; she is offering a sharp, cold-water interrogation of classification itself, urging us to peer past the state-mandated checkboxes and recognize the immense hidden harms of letting institutions define who we are allowed to be before we even open our mouths.

Exhausting.

Please remind me why I agreed to stay late on a Tuesday evening when I still have to go home and explain implicit differentiation to a teenager who thinks calculus is just an aggressive form of ancient Greek torture.
