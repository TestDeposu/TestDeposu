September afternoons demand a certain caliber of vintage, perhaps an aged Rioja that tastes of scorched earth and old leather before the evening chill sets in. *All These Deep Arroyos* arrives on the horizon with precisely that sort of dry, punishing terroir. Matt Baca constructs a literary landscape out of the New Mexico high desert—sun-bleached adobe, prickly yuccas, and the inescapable weight of inheritance. Early blurbs from Rachel Khong and Karen Joy Fowler already lean heavily on the emotional sweep of the thing, but let us be entirely frank about what we actually know. We know nothing of the prose style beyond a few curated jacket quotes. We know only the skeletal framework of a tragedy involving adobe houses, twin brothers, a Laguna Pueblo aspiring scientist named Cristina, and a federal prison sentence. 

### The Geography of Resentment

Dual narratives involving wayward brothers always risk dissolving into cheap melodrama—Cain and Abel with better lighting. Yet the synopsis hints at something structurally more interesting: the geographical determinism of the *desierto alto*. 

And perhaps I was entirely wrong to doubt the twin dynamic. 

Actually, twin tropes are utterly exhausted and lazy. Writers lean on biological mirrors when they cannot be bothered to invent interiority. 

Wait. Let us reconsider the architecture of a federal penitentiary juxtaposed against the vast, indifferent emptiness of New Mexico. Baca is playing with containment versus expansion. Robin is locked in a box because of his brother’s malfeasance, while Cristina builds a life in college laboratories, miles away from the dust. The premise is painfully contrived. Yet the inclusion of a mysterious benefactor working within the judge's chambers introduces a gothic machinery that elevates the narrative above standard contemporary regionalism. It reads less like standard literary fiction and more like Cormac McCarthy filtered through a lens of legal suspense. 

### The Cholla and the Courtroom

The thematic core here rests on debt. Not financial debt, but the crushing, inescapable currency of having been saved. When Robin and Cristina rescue a mysterious third party in their youth, they set a domino effect into motion that reaches into a federal judge's chambers seven years later. 

Literary fiction loves a secret savior. We have seen this brand of atmospheric fatalism before in the works of Denis Johnson or Louise Erdrich, where the landscape is an active antagonist rather than a passive backdrop. 

September brings a sobering clarity to our reading stacks, sweeping away the frivolous beach reads of July. This novel promises zero comfort. It trades in parched earth, institutional gray, and the bitter pill of collateral damage. Whether Baca can sustain the tension across an entire novel without collapsing into western pastiche remains to be seen. But at the very least, the man understands how to frame a desert horizon.
