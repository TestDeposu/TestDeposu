The Gothic revival brickwork of this quad is positively rotting with academic pretension today, which makes it the ideal spot to dissect Radha Agrawal’s upcoming *How to Make a Friend*. Another manual telling the digital generation how to touch grass. Yawn. We are drowning in prescriptive treatises marketed as manifestos, written by people who treat human connection like a startup pitch deck. 

### The Commodification of the Unplanned

And what a premise we have here. Friendship as an inefficient practice. Groundbreaking sociology, really. Comparable to whatever pop-psychology dreck Malcolm Gladwell or Brené Brown churn out when they need a new vacation home. The synopsis leans hard into the tired aesthetic of anti-capitalist romanticism—because nothing screams authentic bonding quite like buying a hardcover book to teach you how to ride the subway for a short hug. 

Yet the intellectual dishonesty baked into these blurbs is truly breathtaking. Agrawal wants us to reject toxic individualism while simultaneously utilizing capitalist toolkits—simple scripts, enrollment metrics, and vibe preservation protocols—to engineer organic warmth. Make it make sense. We are meant to swallow the myth that awkwardness is some sort of sacred gateway to intimacy, when half the time, awkwardness is just bad posture and social illiteracy. 

### Architectural Delusions of Grandeur

Consider the terminology. Community architect. Good grief. Do we need blueprints to sit on a stoop and bitch about the administration? This is peak millennial optimization masquerading as a soulful retreat from screens. Instead of letting relationships wither or bloom organically—the absolute horror of social Darwinism—we must now audit our connections for productivity versus meaning. 

Predicting how this text will perform is child's play. It will undoubtedly dominate the bestseller lists for weeks, scooped up by lonely corporate drones in Tribeca who desperately need a script to talk to their barista. It is absolute brain-rot dressed up in cashmere wellness rhetoric.
