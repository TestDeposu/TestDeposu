**Author:** Pablo Katchadjian  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *An Opportunity* is so deliberately opaque it feels like a spell itself—one that promises to unravel only if you’re willing to chase the shadows it casts. There’s a certain thrill in reading about a man who believes he’s been cursed with indecision, because what’s more human than the terror of paralysis? Yet the way Katchadjian frames it—this "invisible monster" sapping energy, the "secret literary police"—it’s less a horror story and more a fever dream of bureaucratic absurdity, the kind only a Latin American surrealist could conjure. The mention of a "gig reporting from the frontlines of a far-flung war" makes me wonder if this is a novel about the performative nature of witnessing, the way even the most urgent truths get mediated into something else. It’s the kind of premise that could easily collapse into self-parody, but the praise from Schweblin and Aira suggests Katchadjian has the precision to make it feel like a ritual rather than a joke.

But here’s where the synopsis fails—it doesn’t tell us *how* the narrator’s quest becomes a meditation on storytelling. Is it through fragmented narratives? A narrator who keeps slipping into unreliable modes? Or is it something more visceral, like the way the "monster" might manifest in the gaps between sentences? The lack of specificity is maddening, but also tantalizing. It’s the same kind of controlled ambiguity you’d find in *The Night Circus* or *The Library at Mount Char*—books that let the reader’s imagination do the heavy lifting, even as they demand you suspend disbelief. Yet unlike those, *An Opportunity* seems to lean into the idea that storytelling itself is a kind of enchantment, a spell that can bind as easily as it can free.

And then there’s the translation—Heather Cleary’s name is a guarantee of lyrical precision, but also of a certain tonal rigor. Katchadjian’s work often dances between the grotesque and the tender, so if this novel is as "hallucinogenic" as Rimsky claims, it might feel like being trapped in a dream where the walls are made of ink. The "secret literary police" trope is particularly intriguing—does it reference actual censorship, or is it a metaphor for the self-imposed constraints of art? Either way, it’s the kind of detail that could make this book feel like a love letter to the act of creation, even as it’s being policed.

But wait—if the narrator is so indecisive, how does he even *begin* to break the spell? The synopsis doesn’t say. And that’s the problem. It’s not that the premise is weak; it’s that it’s too *open*. There’s no anchor, no clear throughline beyond the idea of "freedom at any cost," which feels less like a thematic anchor and more like a tagline for a cult movie. Compare it to *The Secret History* or *The Savage Detectives*—books where the mystery of the protagonist’s descent is as compelling as the descent itself. Here, the descent is just… a premise.

Yet—here’s the pivot—I might be overthinking it. Maybe the beauty of this book is exactly that it refuses to be pinned down. Maybe the "invisible monster" isn’t just a metaphor for indecision but for the act of writing itself, the way a story can consume you until you’re nothing but a vessel. If that’s the case, then the synopsis is doing its job: it’s not giving you answers, it’s giving you the illusion of a quest. And in a world where so many books feel like they’ve been pre-packaged for algorithmic consumption, that’s a rare and dangerous thing.

But then again, if the narrator is so trapped by his own indecision, how does he ever *actually* break free? The synopsis doesn’t say. And that’s the real question—does this book dare to answer it, or is it just another exercise in the illusion of choice?

I need to mediate a brutal sibling rivalry over the remote before I lose my mind entirely.
