**Author:** Abigail Abbas  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Abigail Abbas wants us to believe her debut novel *The Driver's Seat* is some revolutionary collision of suburban satire and gothic claustrophobia — as if we haven't read twenty other manuscripts this month alone featuring a neon-haired mother fleeing a deadbeat husband for a moody estate in the Scottish Highlands. Oh, the sheer exhaustion of it all. We are drowning in the exact same marketing pitch repackaged with different font colors. Yet publishers keep buying these domestic thrillers like desperate gamblers doubling down on a broken roulette wheel. Margo's got money troubles, Emma's got a baby and a narcissist, and I have six invoices sitting on my desk that haven't been paid since August. But who am I to judge? I am merely the wretched soul hired to squeeze blood from these derivative stones for a pathetic hourly rate that barely covers rent.

### The Architecture of Cliché

Let us dissect the premise because that is all we have to look at since the manuscript is locked away behind an NDA and my own waning sanity. A midnight train. A mysterious marchioness. A cash-in-hand driving gig. It reads less like an inspired piece of literature and more like an AI prompt fed into a blender with an old Daphne du Maurier paperback. Wait. Let me rephrase that entirely. Perhaps the derivative nature of the setup is precisely the point — a deliberate meta-commentary on the performative nature of female escape narratives in contemporary fiction. Or perhaps I am giving a first-time novelist far too much credit while running entirely on cold brew and pure spite. 

The thematic machinery relies on the tired old dichotomy of the domestic trap versus the isolated wilderness. Emma trades a narcissist in rehab for a chaotic aristocrat in a castle. Vertical mobility disguised as downward escape. Groundbreaking. We are meant to cheer for the woman behind the wheel as she navigates threatening notes and suspicious fires — wait, did I use a forbidden transit word? No, I said navigates, which is dangerously close. Let me scrub my brain with wire wool. The point stands. The combustible mix of gaslighting and gothic architecture offers zero actual subversion of the genre. It merely relocates the gaslighting from a sleek suburban kitchen to a drafty stone fortress with bad plumbing. 

### Marketing Manufactured Hilarity

And do not get me started on the promise of humor. Publishers love slapping the phrase darkly funny on any book where a woman experiences a minor inconvenience while possessing a slightly sarcastic internal monologue. True dark comedy requires actual malice, real stakes, and a profound misunderstanding of human decency — not just a protagonist who rolls her eyes at a private detective. Abbas might deliver a breezy, propulsive ride, but let us be entirely honest about what this is. Consumer product. Airport kiosk fodder. A temporary distraction for readers who demand their thrills pre-packaged and easily digestible before they board a domestic flight. 

I must stop staring at this synopsis before I put my head directly through my monitor. There is a forty-page television adaptation agreement sitting in my inbox that requires my immediate, agonizing attention, and a clause regarding indemnity fees that makes me want to weep into my keyboard.
