**Author:** Camila Sosa Villada  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-04 APL

My heart is still hammering against my ribs—that wonderful, terrifying hum of pure caffeine and eight straight hours of staring at corporate spreadsheets while keeping three hundred rogue inventory line items from descending into absolute administrative chaos. God, I love the absolute rush of closing a quarter out. Yet here I am, staring down the barrel of Camila Sosa Villada’s upcoming novel, *A Domestication*, through the haze of a publisher's breathless French synopsis. And honestly? I am ready to fight anyone who dismisses it. 

### The Illusion of the Stage and the Trap of Blood

We get it. The world loves an archetype. An actress—specifically the most famous trans actress on the planet—is someone the public invents, consumes, and discards. But what happens when the illusion demands a domestic reality? The synopsis tells us she decides to build a family against all common sense. Good. Fiction needs more terrible, beautiful, irrational decisions. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that entirely. Actually, scratch the praise about irrationality—the synopsis is frustratingly cagey. It relies on that tired French literary trope of throwing a bunch of high-concept ingredients into a bowl and calling it art. Jean Cocteau? A perilous return to the natal village? Violent, tearing love? It sounds like a checklist generated by an algorithm designed to win European literary prizes. 

Yet I am fiercely, irrationally loyal to Sosa Villada’s prose. After *The Bad Girls*, which punched me directly in the gut with its feral, unapologetic brilliance, she can write a grocery list and I will call it a masterwork. 

### Dismantling the Myth of the Hearth

The core of this book—or at least what the marketing copy promises us between breathless bouts of praise—is a direct assault on the sanctity of the traditional family. And thank heavens for that. We are drowning in domestic misery memoirs written by people who think putting a microwave on the counter is a rebellion. Sosa Villada is different. She slices open the anatomy of an atypical couple with the precision of a surgeon who lost her license years ago and doesn't care. 

Except the synopsis also tries to claim this is "deeply universal." 

No. Stop right there. The moment a brilliant, transgressive writer gets labeled as "universal," the publishing industry is trying to sand down her edges for the suburban book clubs. I refuse to accept it. This book better not be universal. It better be violently specific, jagged, and entirely unpalatable to anyone who hasn't survived the margins. If it smells even faintly of Elizabeth Strout comfort food, I am throwing it across the room. It needs to read like a knife fight in the dark, closer to *My Work* by Olga Ravn or the raw fury of Virginie Despentes, rather than some cozy meditation on parenthood. 

I don't need another tender story about finding oneself. I want the wreckage. 

I have to close this document right now because I promised my daughter I would bake three dozen cupcakes for the school bake sale by tomorrow morning and the oven is currently cold.
