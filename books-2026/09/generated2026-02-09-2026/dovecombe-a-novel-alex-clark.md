The light is failing, casting long, bruised shadows across the rows of late-season kale, and the frost hasn’t quite bitten yet, though the wind has that sharp, metallic tang of early September. I sit here with a damp galley of *Dovecombe* on my lap, wondering why we remain so obsessed with the idea that the natural world is out to punish our domestic failures. The synopsis promises a crumbling manor, which is a tired trope, yet Clark seems intent on weaponizing the landscape itself. If the oaks are truly sentient, they are likely just annoyed by the human noise pollution. There is a tiresome recurrence in the genre where woodland—traditionally a source of biological complexity and resilience—is reduced to a prop, a jagged set piece for Victorian-adjacent melodrama. We want the trees to be malignant because it is easier to fear an angry forest than it is to admit that our own patterns of obsession and erasure are what ruin the soil. The prose reportedly leans into the evocative, which usually translates to an over-reliance on adjectives that describe dampness, but I suspect the actual mechanics of the story will be brittle, prone to cracking under the weight of its own gothic posturing. It’s the sort of book that wants to be the next *Rebecca* or perhaps something from the Sarah Waters back-catalog, yet it risks losing itself in the fog of its own vagueness. Is the arson a structural mystery or just a pathetic fallacy draped in kerosene? I’m leaning toward the latter. I suppose I should admire the commitment to the bit—the brooding groundskeeper, the ruthless matriarch, the whole gothic apparatus. Actually, forget that. The atmospheric tension might be the only thing holding this narrative together, and perhaps the deliberate opacity is exactly what modern readers crave in a market flooded with overly-explicated thrillers.

Sentient trees.

The premise hinges on the idea that the land remembers, a conceit I find deeply romantic and entirely scientifically illiterate, though it certainly makes for an arresting metaphor in a fiction setting. If the house is truly a character, I hope Clark avoids the trap of making it merely a vessel for human secrets; I want the rot to be structural, caused by actual neglect rather than some ethereal curse. Then again, if the protagonist is the most dangerous element, as the blurb suggests, the gothic setting is just a mirror, not a participant. It’s an interesting pivot, moving from a hauntological mystery to a character-driven thriller, though I worry the shift will leave the environmental themes feeling abandoned in the shrubbery. The industry loves a “twisted” mystery, but I’m tired of the twisting. Give me something rooted. Give me something that understands the difference between a forest and a backdrop.

Must get the charcoal going before the neighbors arrive and the backyard turns into a chaotic mess of over-seasoned meat and loud opinions.
