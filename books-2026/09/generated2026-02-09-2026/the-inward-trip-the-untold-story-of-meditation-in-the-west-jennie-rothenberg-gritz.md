**Author:** Jennie Rothenberg Gritz  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Oh, the way this book’s synopsis reads like a corporate wellness brochure—slick, sanitized, the kind of thing you’d find in a Whole Foods magazine tucked between articles on "gut health" and "the benefits of cold showers." *The Inward Trip* promises to trace meditation’s journey from "the first yogis and Zen masters" to today’s "mindfulness era," but the real story here isn’t just about how Eastern practices landed in America—it’s about how America turned them into a lifestyle accessory. The problem with this kind of historical retelling is that it risks reducing centuries of spiritual discipline to a series of Instagram-worthy milestones: Swami Vivekananda at the 1893 World’s Parliament of Religions, then Thich Nhat Hanh in the ‘60s, then the Silicon Valley mindfulness boom of the 2010s. Where’s the mess? Where’s the resistance? Where’s the moment when someone said, "No, this isn’t just relaxation—it’s a rebellion"?

And yet—here’s the thing—this book might still be useful. Not as a rigorous history, but as a cultural autopsy. Gritz, a journalist who’s clearly spent time in labs studying the science of meditation, seems to understand that the real magic isn’t in the technique itself but in the way it’s been repackaged. She mentions "mind-wandering" as a creative force, which is delightfully counterintuitive—because in an era where focus is everything, the idea that daydreaming might be an asset is almost revolutionary. But the synopsis doesn’t tell us how she’ll handle the tension between the spiritual roots of these practices and their modern, often commodified forms. Will she celebrate the democratization of meditation, or will she expose the ways it’s been stripped of its radical edge? The way the synopsis leans into the "science-backed tools for self-improvement" framing makes me worry she’ll default to the latter—because that’s the safer bet, and safer bets don’t sell books the way cautionary tales do.

Speaking of safer bets, I can’t help but think of *The Spirituality of Yoga* by Georg Feuerstein, which actually did the deep cultural work this one seems to be promising. Feuerstein didn’t just trace yoga’s history—he dissected how it became a Western obsession, complete with all the pitfalls of appropriation and commercialization. But Feuerstein was writing in the ‘90s, when the yoga industry was still in its infancy. Gritz, if she’s half as sharp, might have something to say about how meditation has evolved since then—how it’s become not just a wellness trend but a corporate perk, a parenting hack, a way to "hack your happiness." The question is, will she treat it like a phenomenon worth celebrating, or will she treat it like a cautionary tale?

And then there’s the practical guide at the end. Oh, the practical guide. Because nothing says "groundbreaking" like a how-to section that feels like it was cribbed from a Headspace app. Hardly groundbreaking.

But let’s be honest—this book isn’t going to be *The Om Factory* by Mark Epstein, which actually grappled with the psychological and existential dimensions of meditation in a way that felt less like a self-help manual and more like a philosophical inquiry. Gritz’s approach seems more journalistic, which is fine—better than most of the fluff out there—but it also means we might not get the kind of deep, uncomfortable conversations about what meditation *really* does to people. Does it heal? Does it harm? Does it just make us better consumers? The synopsis doesn’t say.

I also can’t stop thinking about how this book might handle the fact that meditation, in its modern form, is often marketed as a solution to the very problems it’s supposed to help us transcend. Anxiety? Meditate. Depression? Breathe. Burnout? Just close your eyes and think happy thoughts. It’s a system that turns spiritual practice into a Band-Aid for capitalism’s wounds. And yet—here’s the thing—I’d read a book that acknowledged that tension. I’d read a book that didn’t just trace the history of meditation but asked why we need it so badly in the first place.

But the synopsis doesn’t say any of that. It just says "essential read for anyone embarking on their own journey inward." Which is fine, I guess, if you’re looking for a feel-good narrative about how America got its zen. But if you’re looking for something that actually challenges the way we think about meditation, you might be disappointed. Or maybe not. Maybe Gritz will surprise us. Maybe she’ll show us that the inward trip isn’t just about relaxation—it’s about resistance. It’s about asking why we’re so desperate to quiet our minds in the first place.

I need to unclog the shower drain before the entire second floor floods.
