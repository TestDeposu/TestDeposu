**Author:** Kate Fagan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Cinema is a graveyard of half-baked vanity projects and directors desperate to scrub their own fingerprints off a flop. Kate Fagan’s upcoming *Instant Classic* suggests we are about to witness the literary equivalent of a desperate third-act pivot. Marion May, our weary auteur, hunting for a win in the dusty, half-forgotten archives of women’s basketball. It is a premise that hums with the kind of earnest, aggressive nostalgia that makes me want to pull the fire alarm. 

### The Erasure of the Jump Shot

We are sold a story about Harper Kidd, a fifties phenom whose career was sacrificed at the altar of the era’s suffocating conformity. It is a familiar archetype—the fallen athlete, the erased history, the weight of the past pressing down on the present. Is it profound? Or is it just another exercise in exhuming the dead to satisfy our current hunger for vintage grit? Fagan clearly wants us to see the connective tissue between a modern filmmaker’s malaise and the silenced cries of a mid-century woman. Yet, the synopsis treats this link with a heavy hand, promising a mirror held up to the soul. I find the metaphor of the court as a site of self-discovery a bit too clean, almost antiseptic. The game is rarely that forgiving. 

Actually, wait. Perhaps the antiseptic nature is the point. Maybe the beauty lies in the sterility of the archive versus the chaotic sweat of the paint. No, that is wrong—the sweat is exactly what the story needs, and if Fagan keeps the prose too polished, the whole thing will slide right off the shelf.

The narrative architecture reminds me of Taylor Jenkins Reid’s *Daisy Jones & The Six*, but stripped of the rock-and-roll decadence and replaced with the screech of sneakers on hardwood. It is a gamble, certainly. One that risks collapsing into a Hallmark-adjacent meditation on identity if it fails to ground the WNBA’s sharp, modern intensity against the muted, sepia-toned struggle of the fifties. Does the world need another book about a character finding their voice through someone else’s biography? Probably not. Do I secretly hope the prose is biting enough to keep me awake? Yes.

If this lands, it lands because it captures the specific, sharp loneliness of being a woman in a room where the rules were written by men who never touched a ball. If it misses, it will be because it traded the harsh, ugly reality of lost legacy for a tidy emotional payoff. I suspect Fagan will aim for the latter—a crowd-pleasing, cinematic epiphany—when I am praying for the former. A bit of bitterness would go a long way here. 

I am already running behind. The taxi is waiting, or it should be, and there is a debate starting in twenty minutes at a smoky basement club about whether art can ever truly exist outside of its own decay.
