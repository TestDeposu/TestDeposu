Fairview, Connecticut. A picturesque cage where the lawns are manicured to a sociopathic degree and the local girl's autonomy is treated like a public utility. Wendy Walker’s *All is Not Forgotten* drops us squarely into the kind of suburban rot that Gillian Flynn and Paula Hawkins mapped out with such gleeful malice. But wait—actually, let me walk that back entirely, because Walker sidesteps psychological noir cliché by introducing a terrifying bio-ethical question: what happens when science itself conspires to rob a young woman of her own narrative? 

### The Pharmacological Erasure of Female Agency

Let us examine the sheer audacity of the premise. Jenny Kramer is brutally assaulted, and the immediate medical response is a controversial pharmacological intervention designed to wipe the slate clean. They call it healing. I call it state-sanctioned gaslighting in a Petri dish. Walker manages to weaponize bodily autonomy, or rather, the systematic dismantling of it. Yet, the emotional memory refuses to stay in its lane. It leaks out like toxic waste through the floorboards of a McMansion. The girl cannot remember her attacker, yet her body keeps the score in a way that makes you want to throw your coffee mug at the wall. Bitch, please. The audacity of assuming a traumatized teenager can just pop a pill and forget a violent violation is peak patriarchal nonsense. 

### Fractured Households and Performative Perfection

Tom Kramer is obsessed with justice, which is just a socially acceptable mask for male vengeance and bruised paternal ego. Charlotte, on the other hand, is too busy maintaining the aesthetic integrity of her curated domestic hellscape to notice the structural collapse happening underneath her feet. This is where the book actually transcends cheap procedural thrills. Walker exposes the fault lines of a community built on keeping up appearances—a theme we have seen explored by Liane Moriarty, but executed here with a scalpel rather than a sledgehammer. The narrative thrives on the friction between what is publicly performed and what is privately festering. 

The synopsis leaves us hanging on whether the prose can sustain the psychological weight of its own premise. Plot-wise, we get the standard thriller beats—the attacker is still lurking in the shadows, the town is harboring secrets—which feels slightly pedestrian for such an otherwise fascinating bio-political setup. I am surprisingly generous today, so I will forgive the trope-heavy trappings because Walker usually knows how to twist the knife in the final act. We are looking at a masterclass in suspense, provided she does not cop out with a neat, tidy resolution that wraps up the patriarchal violence with a neat little bow. 

I am going to put on a Coltrane vinyl and forget about this book.
