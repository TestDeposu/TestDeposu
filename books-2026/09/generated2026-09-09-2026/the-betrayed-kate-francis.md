**Author:** Kate Francis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Another glossy brochure arrived in the mail this morning, promising a weekend retreat for "troubled adolescents" who just need a little discipline to straighten out their jagged edges—because nothing screams rehabilitation quite like tearing a kid out of their bed at three in the morning by masked mercenaries and shipping them off to the woods to sleep on a cot. Kate Francis is leaning heavily into this exact brand of institutional paranoia with *The Betrayed*, a 2026 release that uses the well-worn YA thriller trope of the predatory summer camp and strips it down to pure psychological isolation. 

And let us be honest, the plot outlined in this synopsis is as predictable as the chilly September wind currently rattling the loose shingles off my porch. We have Liv Walker, the mandatory betrayed protagonist whose own flesh and blood traded her trust for a glossy pamphlet. Yet the real structural annoyance here isn't the kidnapping—it is the absurd reliance on parlor games turning fatal. Remember when books like *One of Us Is Lying* or Karen McManus's entire bibliography actually grounded their high school tension in social media gossip and genuine teenage malice instead of turning summer camp into a cheap slasher flick where people die because they played Werewolf by the campfire? Honestly, speaking of overhyped media, my youngest has been watching this utterly mindless cartoon about a yellow sponge for three hours straight, and the existential dread I feel listening to those relentless, synthesized chuckles from the living room is somehow still more coherent than the logic of sending a teenager to a brutal survival camp just because she rolled her eyes at dinner. 

Trust issues.

Thematic analysis of this kind of fiction usually tries to dress itself up as a profound commentary on adolescent alienation and the systemic failure of the nuclear family. But when the baseline premise relies on sadistic counselors and a mysterious traitor mimicking a party game, any genuine exploration of adolescent trust evaporates into hollow genre mechanics. Francis clearly wants to invoke the claustrophobia of Lord of the Flies mixed with contemporary boarding school thrillers, but without a page count or any original character beats beyond the synopsis clichés, it reads like an algorithm generated it in a boardroom. 

Hard pass. 

I need to go outside and fire up the grill before the neighborhood barbecue descends into absolute anarchy.
