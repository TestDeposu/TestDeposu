**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Skara is a nice enough sounding fiction, yet another Scottish estate buckling under the grand tradition of aristocratic downsizing. Eleanor Buchanan brings us back to the well in *The Moonstone Sister*. A ring. Four lost siblings. Naturally. Because nothing says domestic stability quite like a piece of jewelry acting as a psychological anchor for an entire bloodline. 

Let us discuss the geography of grief. Or rather, the marketing department's version of it. We are promised the French Riviera in 1931. Cannes, specifically. Before the tax shelters ruined the view and the umbrellas turned uniform. Lily Blackmore gets shipped off on the Blue Train—how terribly *Orient Express* without the corpses—leaving behind the wind-swept Scottish heather for the sun-drenched hedonism of the Mediterranean. 

### The Chronological Shuffle

And then, modern day. Clara Gibson. A woman whose life apparently requires an administrative reboot the moment her grandmother kicks the bucket. An unthinkable betrayal! What is it this time? An unexpected codicil leaving the ancestral portrait to a stray cat? An anonymous Swiss bank account funded by bootlegged claret? I guess we're doing this. The contemporary protagonist always needs a catalyst involving a deceased relative with a shadowy past and a convenient penchant for nineteenth-century photography. The parallel is about as subtle as the Costa Concordia docking in a paddle-boat pond. 

One looks at these blurbs and sighs. The comparison to Lucinda Riley is thrown around like confetti at a third marriage. People want the Seven Sisters formula replicated until the oceans dry up. They want the generational trauma delivered via heirloom accessories and dual-timeline narrative gymnastics. 

### The Hereditary Hardware

Jewelry as a custody arrangement. Imagine telling a border guard that your emotional stability is entirely dependent on an antique gemstone gifted by an aunt you have never met. It has the distinct energy of the Duke of Windsor's auction at Sotheby's—expensive, slightly desperate, and historically dubious. Clara stumbles upon a mysterious photographer from 1932 who haunts her research. Naturally. Because archival work in this genre never involves actual microfilm or eye strain; it always leads directly to a handsome, enigmatic stranger or a long-lost diary bound in morocco leather. 

The prose will no doubt sweep us away. That is the contractual obligation of the genre. Sweeping. Immersive. Unputdownable. Words deployed with the mechanical precision of a Swiss timepiece to mask the fact that Lily and Clara are essentially the same protagonist wearing different decades. One wears a silk slip on the Croisette while watching the fascist storm clouds gather over Europe with quiet stoicism; the other discovers a digital footprint that upends her engagement to a man named something aggressively beige like Julian. 

Give me the peace of a quiet backwater instead. Give me memoirs written by retired undersecretaries who spend four hundred pages detailing grain quotas in the Balkans. Instead, we get the blue train, the moonstone, and the inevitable journey home. 

The washing machine just flooded the laundry room.
