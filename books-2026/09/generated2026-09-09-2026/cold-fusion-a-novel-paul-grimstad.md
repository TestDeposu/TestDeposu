**Page Count:** Unknown

The unnamed freshman arrives on campus like a ghost in a flannel shirt, his grief so thick it could be the air itself. And yet—*and yet*—this is the kind of story where the ghost gets to wander through the autumn leaves, muttering about cold fusion while his brain unravels like a cheap sweater left in the rain. Paul Grimstad’s *Cold Fusion* isn’t just a coming-of-age novel; it’s a fever dream masquerading as one, the kind where the protagonist’s hallucinations feel more real than the world around him, and the world around him is already a mess of dive bars and abandoned churches.

The synopsis is deliberately vague, which is either a sign of genius or a cop-out—because if you don’t know what cold fusion actually means here, you’re left wondering if it’s a metaphor, a literal obsession, or just another layer of the protagonist’s unraveling mind. But that’s the point, isn’t it? The 1990s midwestern campus setting is a stage for the absurd, where the only thing more unreliable than the protagonist’s perception is the narrative itself. It’s *Wonder Boys* if John Williams had been replaced by a guy who’d just lost his father and started hallucinating in a hotel room—less melancholy, more manic, with the kind of dark humor that makes you laugh until you realize you’re laughing at a guy who might be losing his mind.

And the mother! Oh, the mother. She’s not just a wealthy enabler; she’s a plot device with a side of existential dread, sending her son on a quest that feels less like a search and more like a punishment. It’s the kind of dynamic that makes you wonder if the real cold fusion is the mother’s own delusion—that she thinks she can control her son’s grief by turning it into a mission. Meanwhile, the elusive student he’s tracking down is just another eccentric in a line of them, because in this world, everyone’s a fringe intellectual or a drug dealer, and the line between the two is thinner than the protagonist’s grip on reality.

The synopsis mentions a "mysterious hallucinatory illness with an even stranger home remedy"—which is either a brilliant setup for a psychological thriller or the kind of vague premise that makes you wonder if Grimstad is just too lazy to commit. But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the illness isn’t a plot device at all, but a metaphor for the way we all construct narratives to make sense of chaos. And if so, then *Cold Fusion* isn’t just a novel; it’s a mirror held up to the reader, asking them to question whether they’re the protagonist or just another hallucination in his story.

But let’s talk about the real crime here: the fact that we’re still reading about grieving college students in the 1990s like it’s some kind of fresh take. Where’s the originality? Where’s the twist? Because right now, this feels like *The Secret History* if it had been written by a guy who’d just watched too many *Twin Peaks* reruns and decided to channel David Lynch’s paranoia. And don’t even get me started on the fact that every other "darkly funny fever dream" novel seems to be trying to outdo the last one, like we’re all just waiting for the next *Idiots* or *Stoner* to come along and save us from the banality of existence.

Yet—yet—there’s something undeniably compelling about the idea of a protagonist who can’t tell if he’s losing his mind or just losing the battle against the absurdity of life. It’s the kind of premise that could either be brilliant or pretentious, depending on how it’s executed. And if Grimstad pulls it off, then *Cold Fusion* might just be the next great absurdist novel, a darkly comic meditation on the fragility of perception in a world that’s already unraveling.

But I’m getting ahead of myself. The real question is: can this book live up to the hype, or is it just another overhyped fever dream waiting to collapse under its own weight? Because let’s be honest, the literary world is full of books that promise more than they deliver, and *Cold Fusion* is no different. It’s either going to be a masterpiece or a mess, and right now, I’m leaning toward the latter.
