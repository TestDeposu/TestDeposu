There is a particular kind of heartbreak that comes from reading the back of a book about human suffering while holding a ridiculously overpriced latte, wondering if the foam on top was beaten by hands that actually saw a living wage today, or if we are all just complicit cogs in a very shiny, very cruel machine that grinds ordinary people into dust just so someone in Palm Beach can buy another yacht. 

Sandy Tolan is stepping into the ring with *Bittersweet Empire*, a sprawling, five-years-in-the-making investigative deep-dive that promises to rip the pristine white wrapper right off the global sugar trade and expose the dynastic rot beneath. And by rot, I mean the kind of multi-generational, heavily lobbied, government-subsidized feudalism that makes *Succession* look like a heartwarming sitcom about quirky billionaires. 

We are promised the Fanjul family—a name most people outside the commodities market won't recognize until Tolan drags them blinking into the harsh Florida sun—and their glittering domain of Domino Sugar and Florida Crystals. 

Wait. Actually, let me walk that back entirely. 

Maybe the Fanjuls aren't the secretive shadow-masters the synopsis makes them out to be at all. Power usually screams rather than whispers in this country, hiding behind campaign contributions and Palm Beach galas rather than dark alleys, and perhaps Tolan is overstating the secrecy just to sell hardcovers to people who already pat themselves on the back for buying fair-trade chocolate. 

Brutal. 

Because beneath the glossy marketing copy about stolen land in Cuba, migrant cane workers trapped in modern debt bondage, and poisoned Everglades ecosystems, lies a familiar journalistic gamble: can a sweeping, multi-century indictment of capitalism actually move the needle, or is it just another devastating doorstop for our coffee tables that we weep over before falling asleep to a true-crime podcast? 

We have seen this before. Matthew Desmond does it with housing. Patrick Radden Keefe does it with the Sacklers. 

Tolan is aiming for that exact shelf of righteous, investigative fury, tracing a path from northern Spain to the Caribbean to the White House itself, using five years of relentless on-the-ground reporting that apparently already packs enough punch to successfully block sugar imports from the Dominican Republic. That is not just writing history. That is weaponizing it. 

Power. 

And yet, I worry about the balance. Can a book juggle the macro-economics of the U.S. sugar program alongside the micro-tragedies of offshore workers earning four dollars a day without losing its narrative soul in the ledgers? 

We shall see when the pages finally land. 

Right now, I need to go argue with a barista about the ethical sourcing of their coffee beans.
