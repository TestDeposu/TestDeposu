**Author:** Arriel Vinson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Seventeen is an age where the human brain is essentially a live grenade wrapped in bad decisions and hormones, yet publishers insist on handing teenagers these impossibly poetic burdens. *If This World Were Mine* by Arriel Vinson arrives with all the rhythmic markers of a verse novel trying very hard to break my heart before third period. The synopsis gives us Sky Simms, a majorette whose life hits a brutal stumbling block after a family tragedy shatters her collegiate marching band fantasies. Naturally, she is forced into close proximity with the annoying drumline captain named Meech. Cue the slow-burn friction. 

I am already exhausted by the sheer predictability of the forced-proximity trope. Can two teenagers in YA fiction *ever* be assigned to work together without discovering they secretly heal each other's generational trauma? It is enough to make a person want to throw a red pen across a linoleum floor. Why must every extracurricular activity double as a grief-processing facility? Give me a protagonist who just wants to twirl a baton because it looks cool, not because she is carrying the weight of a Greek tragedy on her sequined shoulders. 

Speaking of unnecessary suffering, I spent twenty minutes this morning trying to pry a dried-up glue stick out of a mechanical keyboard while three children argued about whether a hot dog constitutes a taco. That level of daily chaos makes the tidy emotional arcs of modern verse novels feel terribly quaint. At least a rogue glue stick has the decency to ruin your day without offering a poignant metaphor about letting go of the past. 

Vinson won a Coretta Scott King/John Steptoe award, which means the writing will undoubtedly be sharper than a freshly sharpened No. 2 pencil. Yet I worry the verse format will lean too heavily into the kind of sparse, fragmented styling popularized by Elizabeth Acevedo or Jason Reynolds. Every contemporary author writing in verse right now feels obligated to make their line breaks do heavy lifting for emotions the narrative fails to earn. 

The synopsis hints that Sky might "fumble her future" if she keeps running from her grief. What a terrifyingly adult anxiety to project onto a senior in high school who probably still forgets to bring her gym uniform on Thursdays. We are asking teenagers to master emotional regulation before they even understand how compounding interest works. 

My red ink is ready anyway. 

Now if you will excuse me, I need to stare blankly at a calculus assignment involving limits and derivatives that I do not understand at all.
