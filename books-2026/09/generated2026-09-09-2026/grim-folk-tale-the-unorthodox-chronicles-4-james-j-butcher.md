**Author:** James J. Butcher  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Grimsby*—this fourth installment in Butcher’s *Unorthodox Chronicles*—is a masterclass in how to make a magical bureaucracy feel like a dying institution, its bones creaking under the weight of its own bureaucracy. Grimshaw Griswald Grimsby, a junior Auditor, is thrust into a crisis that smells of something older than the Department itself: a plague among children, the kind of horror that doesn’t just kill but *unmakes*. The fact that Butcher’s chosen to frame this as a public health crisis—with all the red tape, the slow-moving investigations, the bureaucratic finger-pointing—is either a stroke of genius or a deliberate provocation, depending on whether you’re a fan of *The Dresden Files*’ more overtly heroic tone or the kind of reader who still seethes at the idea of a government agency failing to act in time.

But here’s the thing: the synopsis is *deliberately* vague. It doesn’t tell you what the illness is, only that it’s magical and that it’s spreading. It doesn’t explain why Boston, of all places, is the epicenter—unless you’ve been following the series closely, you’re left with the same unsettling sense of dread that comes from knowing something terrible is happening, but not knowing why. This is the kind of ambiguity that works best when paired with a writer who trusts the reader to fill in the blanks with their own fears. Think of it like the opening act of a horror movie where the camera lingers on a child’s bedroom, the shadows stretching just a little too long—you don’t need to see the monster to know it’s there. Butcher’s done this before, of course. The *Unorthodox Chronicles* have always had a knack for making the mundane feel like a pressure cooker about to explode, but this time, the stakes feel *personal*, which is either a bold shift or a risky one, depending on whether you believe in the character’s growth or not.

And then there’s the matter of the "city of monsters beneath Boston." Oh, this is where things get interesting—or at least, where they get *troubling*. Butcher has a habit of dropping readers into liminal spaces, places where the rules of magic and reality bend in ways that feel both thrilling and deeply unsettling. But the synopsis doesn’t specify what these monsters are, only that they’re "ancient and strange." This is the kind of hand-waving that could either pay off spectacularly or devolve into the kind of generic "undead horde" nonsense that makes you wonder why you bothered reading the series in the first place. Compare this to *The Locked Tomb* or *The Crimson Conspiracy*—Butcher’s early works where the monsters were tied to specific, eerie lore, not just vague "evil things that go bump in the night." If this book leans too hard into the "mythic horror" angle without grounding it in something tangible, it risks feeling like a pastiche of *The Witcher*’s monster catalog rather than a true evolution of the series.

But let’s talk about Grimsby for a second. The synopsis positions him as a junior Auditor, which means he’s not the hero of the story—he’s the *reluctant* hero, the one who’s still learning the ropes, still carrying the weight of his past mistakes. This is a character arc that Butcher has explored before, but the way it’s framed here—with Grimsby burdened by "his own troubles, both past and present"—suggests a deeper psychological layer than we’ve seen in previous books. It’s almost as if Butcher is finally giving us the emotional core of the series, the kind of character study that makes you root for a protagonist not just because of what they do, but because of who they are. And yet—here’s the pivot—if this book leans too hard into the "tragic underdog" trope, it risks feeling like a carbon copy of *The Last Jedi*’s emotional whiplash, where the character’s struggles become the sole focus at the expense of the plot. Butcher’s strength has always been in balancing action with introspection, so if he pulls this off, it could be his most mature work yet.

But then there’s the matter of Mayflower, the grizzled veteran Huntsman. The synopsis pairs them together like a classic cop-and-detective duo, which is either a smart move or a lazy one, depending on whether you believe in their dynamic. Mayflower’s role here feels like it could either elevate Grimsby’s arc or become a crutch, the kind of "wise old mentor" trope that either adds depth or feels like an afterthought. Compare this to *The Dresden Files*, where Harry’s relationships with his allies were always central to the story—Butcher never made them feel like plot devices. If Mayflower’s presence here is anything less than organic, it could undercut the entire book.

And now, because I can’t help myself, a tangent: I just watched an episode of *Bluey* where the character Chilli gets into a fight with her little sister because she’s "too old for this stuff." It was so painfully on-the-nose that I wanted to scream, "Just let her be a kid, for God’s sake!" But here’s the thing—Grimsby’s struggle with his own past, with the weight of responsibility he didn’t ask for, is the kind of emotional beat that *Bluey* could never hope to capture. It’s the difference between a cartoon that’s trying too hard to be profound and a series that actually *is* profound, even if it’s just through omission. Butcher doesn’t need to spell out the stakes; he just needs to let the reader feel them.

Back to the book: the mention of "the Elsewhere" is the kind of vague, ominous phrasing that could either be a brilliant setup for a sequel or a lazy way to avoid explaining anything. If Butcher’s going to drop us into another layer of reality, he’d better make it count. The *Unorthodox Chronicles* have always been about the tension between the mundane and the magical, but if this book leans too hard into the "cosmic horror" angle without giving us something tangible to latch onto, it risks feeling like a pastiche of *Annihilation* without the same level of intellectual rigor.

Butcher’s done this before. He’s written books where the stakes felt personal, where the magic felt real, where the characters felt like they were struggling against forces they couldn’t understand. If *Grimsby* delivers on even half of what the synopsis promises, it could be his best work yet. But if it’s just another bureaucratic thriller with a side of monster-of-the-week, it’ll feel like a missed opportunity.
