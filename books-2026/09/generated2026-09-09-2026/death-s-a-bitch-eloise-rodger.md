The autumn air here against the old brick of Quadrangle Three carries the faint, decaying scent of damp beech leaves and expensive espresso, a combination that invariably puts one in the mood to eviscerate a debut novel. The syllabus for the upcoming season groans under the weight of another twenty-something trying to monetize clinical melancholy. Eloise Rodger’s *Death’s a Bitch* arrives with the heavy marketing machinery of an Oxford Street Christmas window, breathlessly compared to Sally Rooney and Oyinkan Braithwaite in the same press release. The audacity. To yoke Rooney’s agonizingly flat affectless bourgeoisie to Braithwaite’s punchy Lagos noir is like casting Chloë Sevigny in a direct-to-video Uwe Boll adaptation. 

### The Commodification of the Crypt

We are supposed to swoon over eighteen-year-old Aggie, counting her sister’s hair-curls like a suburban obsessive-compulsive, only to have the Grim Reaper materialize not as a medieval skeleton, but as an exhausted mother in a bespoke blazer. Groundbreaking. Truly, this is the kind of high-concept magical realism dreamed up by marketing committees who spent their gap years scrolling TikTok in Berlin. The Faustian bargain has always been a fragile literary vessel, but handing it over to a protagonist who treats dying like a neglected laundry pile reduces existential dread to a sitcom premise. 

### Siblinghood as Branding

The synopsis leans heavily on the notion of siblinghood as a battleground, yet it wraps the horror of psychiatric facilities and eating disorders in the pastel-hued packaging of Gen Z quips. Stick-on flowers on padded table corners. How quaint. One can already picture the pristine first-edition copies of *Death's a Bitch* sitting untouched on the marble coffee tables of Brooklyn loft-dwellers who wouldn't know a real psychiatric ward if it bit them on their leather loafers. Rodger has apparently bypassed the messy work of interiority in favor of snappy dialogue and a high-concept premise that peaked the moment it was pitched over oat milk lattes. Literature used to demand silence. Now it demands a viral hook about domesticity with the underworld. 

### The Inevitable Fall

Let us be entirely honest about the trajectory here. The book will sweep the literary prizes because publishers are terrified of anyone over thirty who writes a complete sentence. Critics will praise the "raw vulnerability" with the same breathless desperation they brought to every mediocre campus novel since Donna Tartt took her final bow. Yet beneath the shiny jacket design lies the hollow ring of an author who read two contemporary bestsellers and decided she could synthesize them with a single Wikipedia search on Greek mythology. It is the literary equivalent of the Fyre Festival cheese sandwiches—all aesthetic, zero substance, leaving one profoundly resentful of the trees sacrificed to print it. 

I have a high-stakes negotiation with my publisher in twenty minutes.
