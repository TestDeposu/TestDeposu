**Author:** Audrey J. Cole  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Echo Ridge* reads like a love letter to the kind of family saga that gets buried under the weight of its own nostalgia—unless, of course, it’s handled with the precision of a scalpel. Three generations of Holloway women, a convenience store in Rabbittown, and the quiet violence of change. It’s the kind of setup that could either collapse into sentimentality or soar into something resembling *The Sweet Hereafter* meets *The Loved Ones*—but with the grit of a working-class neighborhood that’s been forgotten by the rest of the world.

But I’m getting ahead of myself.

The problem with this synopsis isn’t that it’s vague—it’s that it’s *too* specific in its vagueness. We get the convenience store, the neighborhood, the three women. That’s a lot of ground to cover without a single concrete detail to anchor it. Where’s the tension? The synopsis doesn’t say. Is this a story about resilience? A story about decay? A story about the way women hold communities together like fraying thread? The language leans into the communal—*"the bonds that break and the ties that bind"*—but without a clear emotional or narrative throughline, it risks becoming a catalog of observations rather than a living thing.

And yet—there’s something compelling in that ambiguity. It’s the kind of setup that could work if the prose has the weight to carry it. Think *The House of the Spirits* but with the economic realism of *The Goldfinch*. The Holloway women aren’t just characters; they’re a pressure point, a family where every decision—every love, every loss—feels like a negotiation with the neighborhood itself. But negotiation implies movement, and the synopsis doesn’t suggest that. It just *is*.

Then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe *Echo Ridge* is a book about stagnation, about the way people get stuck in their roles—Trudy Morgan-Cole as the narrator, the Holloways as the store, Rabbittown as the backdrop that never changes. If so, it’s a bold choice. Most family sagas are about progression, about how people escape their pasts. But what if the past is the only thing holding them together? What if the convenience store isn’t just a job but a fortress?

I’m not sure. The synopsis doesn’t tell us. It just *hints*.

And that’s where the real work begins. Because if *Echo Ridge* is going to avoid the trap of being a quaint, nostalgic exercise, it’ll need to dig deeper than surface-level community bonds. It’ll need to ask: What happens when the ties that bind start to rot from the inside? What happens when the women who keep the neighborhood alive realize they’re the ones being worn down?

But then—what if the answer is simpler? What if the story is just about the store? The way it’s always been, the way it always will be. The way the Holloways are just another generation of people who show up, do their work, and let the world move on without them.

I don’t know. I haven’t read it. But I can’t help but wonder if this is the kind of book that will feel like a punch in the gut when it lands—because it’s not about grand gestures, but about the quiet, relentless work of keeping things together, even when they’re falling apart.
