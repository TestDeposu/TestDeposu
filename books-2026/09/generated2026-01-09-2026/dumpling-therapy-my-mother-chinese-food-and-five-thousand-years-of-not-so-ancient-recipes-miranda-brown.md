Butter in the coffee. A mother who refuses to march to the rigid drumbeat of culinary tradition. Miranda Brown is preparing to break our hearts with *Dumpling Therapy*, an upcoming memoir that sounds less like a cookbook and more like a gentle, aching epistolary collision with the ghosts who feed us. 

### The Kitchen Where History Bleeds

Memory tastes like scorched sugar and unexpected lard. We spend our youth craving the standardized happiness of the families next door—the identical dumplings, the predictable brown gravy, the quiet shame of a mother who dares to be eccentric in a foreign land. Then the mother dies. And suddenly, every weird, butter-soaked recipe she left behind becomes a sacred scroll. 

Wait. Did I say the mother dies in the first chapter? The synopsis implies it happens somewhere in the middle, but honestly, memoirs about dead mothers always start with the rattling of pots in an empty kitchen anyway. Speaking of rattling pots, why do people insist on writing historical fiction where every single woman in the 1800s is secretly a master herbalist and an early feminist? It drives me completely mad. I tried to read a historical romance last week about a duke who invented the steam engine while sighing over a milkmaid, and I nearly threw my cold-press juice across the living room. Give me messy, flawed human beings over sanitized historical archetypes any day. 

Or perhaps I am entirely wrong about the historical scope here. Maybe the book barely touches the ancient past and stays entirely glued to the stovetop of 1980s San Francisco. 

### The Lineage of Chop Suey

There is something devastatingly romantic about tracing a dumpling across two millennia only to realize it has an immigrant story of its own. Food has no pure bloodlines. It migrates, it marries strange ingredients, it adapts just to survive the winter. Brown looks at chop suey—that universally scorned artifact of diaspora survival—and finds a lineage. 

Yet, I worry the culinary metaphor might stretch too thin across five thousand years. Can a single bowl of braised Cantonese tofu really hold the weight of maternal estrangement and ancient geopolitical shifts? 

Perhaps it can. Love makes fools of historians and poets alike. We hunt for secret letters simply because we cannot bear the thought that the people who raised us were ever just ordinary strangers. They leave us butter in the coffee and expect us to decode the grease stains. 

The absurd cost of braces these days is giving me a massive headache.
