**Author:** Hannah Morrissey  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Scream City* is a masterclass in how not to waste a good premise—because what it *could* be is a brutal, genre-defying descent into the kind of psychological horror that makes you question whether the real monster was ever the one in the alley. Instead, it’s a synopsis that leans so hard into the "copycat killer" trope that it risks becoming a pastiche of *Se7en* meets *Zodiac*, but with the emotional depth of a *True Detective* episode that got cut short by a budget meeting. The problem isn’t that the idea is weak—it’s that the execution feels like it’s being held together by duct tape and a prayer to whatever god watches over Milwaukee’s Walker’s Point.

Alice Foster, CCK’s granddaughter, is the kind of character who should be fascinating—trapped in the shadow of her grandfather’s legacy, running a bookstore that’s somehow both a sanctuary and a time bomb. But the synopsis reduces her to a plot device, a "suspect" who exists primarily to make Rex Hale’s life miserable. And Rex? Oh, Rex is the everyman cop who’s already been broken by the system, which is fine, but the way the synopsis frames him—divorced, suspended, evicted—it’s like the author is trying to cram every cliché of the "burned-out detective" into one paragraph. Where’s the nuance? Where’s the *humanity*? This feels like a script for a TV show where the writers hit "save" after the third take and called it a day.

But here’s where the synopsis *almost* works—because the idea of a copycat killer in a city still scarred by its own history is rich with potential. Milwaukee’s Cream City Killer was real, and the way the synopsis drops that name like a punchline—*"Scream City"*—it’s a nod to the way trauma lingers, how cities become named by their violence. That’s the kind of thematic weight that could make this book more than just another thriller. But instead, it’s treated like a gimmick, a way to sell the idea that "this time, it’s personal" because the killer is mimicking a past crime. It’s the literary equivalent of a TikTok trend—short-lived, surface-level, and entirely forgettable unless someone slaps a viral hashtag on it.

And speaking of trends—why does every thriller these days have to be a "copycat" story? It’s like the publishing industry decided that if *Se7en* worked, then *Se7en 2.0* must be even better, even if it’s just a rehash of the same tired mechanics. The synopsis doesn’t even bother to hide that this is a formula: a disgraced cop, a suspect with a dark family history, a killer who’s just another ghost in the machine. It’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if the author even *read* the books they’re trying to emulate, or if they just Googled "psychological thriller tropes" and picked the ones that sounded the most dramatic.

But let’s talk about the real crime here—the way the synopsis treats Walker’s Point like a character itself, a neighborhood doomed by gentrification and the weight of its past. That’s the part that *could* be compelling, if only the author didn’t reduce it to a backdrop. Gentrification isn’t just a setting; it’s a force that erases people, and the synopsis hints at that tension—Rex’s eviction, Alice’s bookstore surviving where others haven’t—but it never digs into why that matters. Is this about displacement? About the way history gets rewritten? Or is it just another excuse to throw in a few urban decay aesthetics before the final act?

The most infuriating part? The synopsis doesn’t even *try* to sell the emotional core. It’s all about the murders, the suspects, the "path of destruction"—but where’s the heart? Where’s the moment that makes you care about Alice or Rex beyond their roles in the investigation? This feels like a book that was written by committee, where the only thing that matters is the next body drop, not the next tear.

And don’t even get me started on the fact that this is supposed to be a *new* novel in 2026, when the market is already saturated with reboots and rehashes. The synopsis reads like it was cobbled together from a list of "things that sell": serial killer, copycat, haunted bookstore, disgraced cop, gentrification subplot. It’s the literary equivalent of a fast-food meal—familiar, predictable, and entirely forgettable unless you’re starving.

But fine, let’s pretend this is actually something. Let’s pretend that Alice and Rex will somehow find a way to trust each other, that they’ll dig up the past and uncover the truth before the killer silences them both. Let’s pretend that *Scream City* will be the kind of book that lingers, that makes you think about the weight of history and the way violence echoes through generations. Because right now, it’s just another synopsis that’s too busy selling the idea of a thriller to actually *be* one.
