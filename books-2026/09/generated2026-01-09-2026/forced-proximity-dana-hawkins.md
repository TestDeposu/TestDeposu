**Author:** Dana Hawkins  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-08-27 APL

The provenance of a piece dictates its entire aesthetic value—or at least that is what I remind myself when staring at a provenance trail as thin as tracing paper. Dana Hawkins’s upcoming *Forced Proximity* arrives with a scaffolding built entirely from marketable tropes and glittering reader testimonials, leaving the actual texture of the prose frustratingly opaque. We are handed a blueprint instead of a completed canvas. 

### The Architecture of Friction

Consider the central conceit. An elite personal security operative juxtaposed against a recalcitrant A-list actor. It is a compositional setup as old as the salon, yet the friction relies on familiar strokes. Lexi Morgan is sketched with the broad charcoal strokes of physical competence and professional disdain. Justin Greenwood is the vanity project she must manage. 

And yet, the synopsis promises a slow-burn combustion. Or perhaps a mild simmer, given the genre's constraints. 

Actually, let me retract that sweeping dismissal of the burn rate. Hawkins has proven her mettle in crafting queer contemporary narratives—works like *Not in the Plan* carry a distinct emotional cadence that suggests the interpersonal dynamics here might possess genuine bite beneath the glossy veneer. 

### The Palette of Peril

A stalker enters the frame to accelerate the mandatory physical proximity. This is narrative convenience disguised as high stakes. A tired trope, frankly. One expects more psychological acuity from a creator whose previous character arcs have earned such fervent praise in niche literary circles. But commercial romance requires a looming threat to force the protagonists into each other's personal space, converting professional detachment into domestic intimacy whether they consented to the layout or not. 

The aesthetic tension here is obvious. High-society glamor meets the utilitarian grit of a bodyguard who thinks she's just babysitting. It is low-hanging fruit for character contrast. The juxtaposition of luxury trailers and menacing anonymous texts provides a jarring chiaroscuro effect, though one worries the execution might lean too heavily into superficial cinematic cues—pure slop for the masses who want their emotional beats telegraphed from three chapters away. 

### The Market Frame

Kate Spencer would likely devour this in a single afternoon on her patio, while Emily Henry fans will spot the structural scaffolding from a mile away. The pedigree matches the current contemporary market demand for forced proximity aesthetics. 

My literary agent just called on the secure line with international translation rights for my obscure monograph on 18th-century forgery, so I must abruptly depart this critique before I dissect the publisher's marketing copy any further.
