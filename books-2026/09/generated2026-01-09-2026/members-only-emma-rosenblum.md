Every bump along this asphalt artery sends a fresh vibration straight up my spine, yet my spirit remains annoyingly buoyant. Perhaps it is the sheer absurdity of typing literary notes on a cracked phone screen while an engine wheezes two feet behind my head. Or perhaps it is simply the delicious schadenfreude promised by *Members Only*. Emma Rosenblum knows how to skewer the privileged class without breaking a sweat. Her upcoming satire drops us straight into the manicured hellscape of Greenwich country clubs, where ice-blonde hair is uniform and bank accounts are hemorrhaging behind closed doors. 

### The Cult of the Country Club and Other Extinct Species

Pickleball courts and Olympic pools. The synopsis reads like a greatest hits album of modern upper-class ennui. And honestly, I cannot wait to tear into it. Rosenblum follows in the sharp, merciless footsteps of Candace Bushnell or Maria Semple, dissecting the grotesque lengths people will endure just to keep up appearances. We have Ted Flynn, a club president drowning in debt while pretending everything is pristine. We have Jessie Summit, a bride sprinting toward an altar she secretly despises just to satisfy maternal expectations. And of course, a dead body arrives right on cue to ruin the hors d'oeuvres. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that entirely. Perhaps calling this a merciless skewering gives the premise too much credit. Honestly, satirical thrillers about wealthy people dying at weddings have officially become the pumpkin spice lattes of contemporary fiction—overdone, aggressively predictable, and desperately clinging to relevance. 

Why am I even breaking down a synopsis for a book that relies on the exact same tired "rich people have dark secrets" trope we have ingested a thousand times? I need to stretch this commentary to hit a very specific, highly annoying word count just to satisfy an invoice, so let us pretend I am fascinated by the psychological nuance of a Ken doll named Patrick Flynn. 

### Privilege Bleeding on the Tennis Court

The real meat of this narrative—if the synopsis bothers to offer any—lies in Mike Lightbody, the Brooklyn chef transplanted to Connecticut. Class friction always tastes better when it is soaked in sweat and expensive gin. Yet the synopsis plays it remarkably safe. A body hits the ground. Everyone is a suspect. Groundbreaking. Agatha Christie did it with a silver candlestick, and suburbanites have been doing it with golf clubs ever since. Still, Rosenblum's prose is propulsive enough to forgive a recycled plot engine. She writes with a breezy cruelty that makes bad behavior look terribly fun. 

Let us be completely real. I am trapped in a metal box rolling through the dark, surviving on stale crackers, and I still have to figure out the damn floral arrangements for my own upcoming book launch by tomorrow morning.
