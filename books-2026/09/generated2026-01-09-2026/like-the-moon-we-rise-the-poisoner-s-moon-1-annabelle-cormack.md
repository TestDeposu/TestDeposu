**Author:** Annabelle Cormack  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Like the Moon We Rise* is a glittering mess of political intrigue and witchy revenge fantasies—like if *The Poisoner’s Daughter* by Kristin Harmel met up with *The Cruel Prince* by Holly Black in a back-alley deal gone wrong. The premise is *so* ripe for a slow-burn courtroom drama where the real poison is the kind that seeps into the bones of the reader’s patience—because the setup is *too* neat, *too* conveniently tied to prophecy, and *too* eager to throw Auriel into a role that feels less like a subversive act and more like a poorly written fanfic trope.

But let’s cut to the chase: the book’s biggest sin is its *vagueness*—not in the way of a mystery, but in the way of a plot that’s been sketched in Sharpie on a napkin. We have a poison maiden, a prince with a grudge, a friend who’s *definitely* hiding something, and a witchcraft that’s either a gift or a curse depending on who’s paying the rent. The problem? None of this feels like it’s been *thought through*. The Empress isn’t just a villain—she’s a *blank slate*, a placeholder for whatever political allegory Cormack wants to slap on later. And Ambrose? He’s not a prince, he’s a *checklist*: young, vengeful, *obviously* hiding something. The synopsis treats him like a character in a YA romance where the guy’s backstory is just “he’s hot and he’s got issues.”

The real gold here is in the *tone*—a mix of *The Lies of Locke Lamora*’s sharp wit and *The Cruel Prince*’s courtly tension, but with the emotional temperature cranked up to “slow-burn until someone snaps.” Auriel’s position as a poisoner isn’t just a job; it’s a *metaphor*—one that could’ve been *so* much sharper if the synopsis didn’t reduce her to “forced to kill for a living.” That’s the kind of line that makes you wonder if the author’s ever read *The Poisoner’s Handbook* by Deborah Blum, where the real horror isn’t the killing, but the *science* of it, the *precision*, the way poison becomes a language all its own. Instead, we get “sweetest poisons,” which is *so* 2010s fantasy—like if a TikTok trend about “dark academia” got pregnant with a court intrigue novel.

Faolan’s role is the most interesting part of the synopsis, not because of what he *does*, but because of what he *implies*. He’s the wild card, the one who’s been in on the plan from the beginning—except the synopsis doesn’t tell us *why*. Is he a traitor? A lover? A *business partner*? The ambiguity is *deliberate*, but it’s also *dangerous*—because if Cormack can’t commit to his role, the reader’s going to be left flailing like Auriel in a room full of knives. And don’t even get me started on the “dark presence within her.” That’s *so* vague it could mean anything—is it a literal demon? A metaphor for trauma? A *plot device*? The synopsis treats it like a punchline, but in a book this layered, it should feel like a *threat*.

The biggest red flag? The way the synopsis *assumes* the reader will buy into the prophecy nonsense. Prophecies are *dead* in modern fantasy—unless they’re subverted, unless they’re *lies*, unless they’re *tools*. But here, they’re just *there*, like a deus ex machina with a crown. It’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if Cormack’s read enough *actual* political history—because prophecies don’t *save* people, they *destroy* them. Look at *The Lies of Locke Lamora*: the real magic isn’t in the prophecy, it’s in the *people* who believe in it. Here, the prophecy feels like a *cheat code*, and that’s a problem.

But—*and this is a big but*—if Cormack can pull off the balance between Auriel’s internal struggle and the courtly machinations, this could be *something*. The synopsis is *too* eager to hand Auriel a mission, but if she’s allowed to *question* it, if the court feels like a *prison* rather than a stage, then maybe the book will live up to the hype. Right now, it’s just *waiting* to see if the author can turn “sweetest poisons” into something more than a gimmick.

My prediction? This is going to be the kind of book that *divides* readers—either they’ll love the courtly tension and the witchy undertones, or they’ll be left scratching their heads at the *lazy* execution of the prophecy. It’s *so* close to being a gem, but the synopsis makes it feel like it’s still in the rough-cut stage. And in fantasy, rough cuts are *fine*—as long as they’re not the final product.
