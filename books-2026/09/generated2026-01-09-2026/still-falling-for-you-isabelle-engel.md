**Author:** Isabelle Engel  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The manuscript industry loves a good physical metaphor for emotional vulnerability. And *Still Falling for You* leans so hard into the gravity motif that it practically demands a helmet. Isabelle Engel returns in 2026 with a premise that trades on the cinematic illusion of danger to mask the much safer, predictable mechanics of the modern rom-com. 

### The Physics of Melodrama

We are promised a second-chance narrative wrapped in the high-octane aesthetic of a Wild West vampire movie—a genre mashup that sounds either brilliantly unhinged or aggressively focus-grouped. Respectfully, what? But let us look past the ridiculous premise of a cinematic vampire shootout. The core engine here relies on the tired trope of forced proximity via professional sabotage. Mavis and Fox cannot simply talk to each other like adults who endured a near-fatal accident. No. They must dodge falling sandbags and stunt mishaps while nursing lingering resentment. 

Actually, scratch that—maybe the forced sabotage angle is the only thing keeping the narrative pulse from flatlining. Without an external antagonist poking holes in the production, these two would likely spend three hundred pages staring longingly across a craft services table. 

### Breaking the Stunt Rigging

We need to talk about the meta-textual exhaustion of this assignment. Why am I sitting here dissecting the marketing copy of a book that does not even have a finalized page count yet, purely to satisfy an arbitrary word count for a deadline that looms entirely too close? The synopsis leans heavily on comparative titles like *The Fall Guy* and Ava Wilder's *Will They or Won’t They*, begging the reader to accept its pedigree before a single chapter is consumed. 

The thematic undercurrent attempts to deconstruct the archetype of the fearless protagonist. When the physical body is compromised—as Mavis's was in that devastating breakup-adjacent accident—the emotional armor shatters. Yet, the synopsis treats this trauma as a mere stepping stone toward a cute comeback. The danger is commercialized. The bruises are glossed over with sizzling chemistry. 

Engel wants us to believe that love is the most hazardous drop of all. Yet, every plot point outlined suggests the exact opposite. Love here is cushioned by mats, choreographed by coordinators, and guaranteed a happy ending by the final act break. There is no real peril in a genre bound by contractual obligation. 

Time to pack up these stray thoughts. I have to rush across town to join a protest against the demolition of a historic neighborhood theater before the wrecking balls arrive.
