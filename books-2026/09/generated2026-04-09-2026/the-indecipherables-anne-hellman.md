**Author:** Anne Hellman  
**Page Count:** Unknown

San Francisco concrete gives way to the sun-bleached expanse of Joshua Tree, and suddenly we are supposed to shudder at a fresh coat of old paint. Anne Hellman’s *The Indecipherables* arrives with heavy baggage—praising blurbs invoking Hitchcock and Paul Tremblay that practically beg for a spot on a dark, mahogany-paneled shelf between rare editions of Shirley Jackson. Yet, how exhausting it is to watch contemporary horror lean so heavily on cinematic nostalgia as a crutch. Do we really need another homage to *Vertigo* when the original staircase still spins quite well on its own? 

### The Geometry of Vanity

Twins on a road trip. A mysterious illness. Doppelgängers snapping polaroids of crimes not yet committed. It carries the distinct aroma of the Fire Island ferry disaster—all frantic motion and expensive styling masking a hollowness at the core. Or perhaps I misjudged the entire enterprise. Maybe the derivative nature of the premise is precisely the point, a deliberate pastiche designed to mock our endless appetite for mirrored reflections and psychological tropes we discarded back when the second season of *Twin Peaks* went entirely off the rails. 

And yet. The synopsis promises a fever dream where boundaries dissolve. Ava and Brin drive through an unwavering fog—a magnificent metaphor for the haze that settles over any manuscript relying too heavily on uncanny clichés to do the heavy lifting. But let us be entirely fair to the architecture of the thing. The mechanics of the plot, however familiar, offer a deliciously cruel geometry. Four identical women playing a cat-and-mouse game across a sun-scorched highway. It reads less like a narrative and more like an installation piece at Art Basel that has gone horribly, delightfully wrong. 

### Mirrors Without Glass

Consider the thematic weight of the secret. We are told the horror arises from what we conceal from those we love. How utterly quaint. As if the human psyche is a Victorian parlor hiding a scandalous oil painting in the attic. The best literature does not merely hide secrets; it rots beneath them while the tea gets cold. Hellman seems determined to drag us through the psychological mud of estranged siblings, trading subtle dread for prophetic hallucinations and supernatural interference. 

Actually, scratch that entire line of reasoning. The supernatural element ruins the exquisite claustrophobia of the doppelgänger motif. The moment a shadow twin stops being a manifestation of repressed guilt and starts acting like a paranormal nuisance, the art loses its edge. Real terror is entirely internal. It lives in the brushstrokes we refuse to acknowledge, not in a mechanic holding up a photograph of a car you supposedly drove yesterday. 

Still, one cannot dismiss a debut that dares to traffic in paranoia this thick. The Pacific Coast Highway is a gorgeous, treacherous spine for a descent into madness. If the prose matches the pitch of the marketing copy—heady, creepy, and relentlessly jagged—we might just have a minor artifact worth displaying behind UV-filtered glass. Or we might have an overly ambitious exercise in mimicry that collapses under the weight of its own mirrors. 

Time to leave the gallery lights burning and tend to pressing matters. I need to hand-bind a chapbook for a friend's upcoming release.
