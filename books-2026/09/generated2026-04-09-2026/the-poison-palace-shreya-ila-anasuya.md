**Author:** Shreya Ila Anasuya  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis of *The Poison Palace* arrives like a poorly sealed letter from a distant empire—promising intrigue, but already smelling faintly of the same old dust. A courtesan-turned-assassin, a cult of snake-goddesses, and Alexander the Great—oh, how *quaint*. It’s the kind of premise that makes you wonder if the author has ever read anything beyond the most reductive historical romances, the ones that mistake "exoticism" for depth. The idea of a young woman trained in poison and politics is so tired it’s almost a joke, like if *The Song of Achilles* had been written by a committee of overcaffeinated undergraduates who thought "Greek mythology" was just a synonym for "dramatic lighting."

But let’s not pretend this isn’t a deliberate gambit. The synopsis leans hard into the "forbidden love" trope, which is fine—if you’re writing *The Mists of Avalon* in 1983. Here, it feels like a lazy shortcut, as if the author is banking on the assumption that any story with two women in a historical setting must inherently be "queer" and therefore "revolutionary." Meanwhile, the political stakes are reduced to a single, vague mission: stop Alexander. No nuance. No exploration of why Magadh would resist him beyond the cliché of "conqueror vs. indigenous culture." It’s all surface, like a painting with no depth—just a few bold strokes of "snake goddess" and "poison" to make it feel *spicy*.

And then there’s the cult of the Vishkanya. Oh, the Vishkanya—how original. A secret society of women who worship snakes and brew potions? It’s the kind of thing that would make *The Da Vinci Code* look like a masterclass in originality. The synopsis doesn’t even bother to suggest what makes this cult distinct beyond its aesthetic. Is it a matriarchy? A resistance movement? A cult of *actual* snake-worshippers? Or is it just a convenient plot device to give Eka and Ira something to do while they fawn over each other? The vagueness is almost insulting. It’s like being handed a menu where every dish is labeled "Surprise" and the chef is too lazy to describe the ingredients.

Yet—here’s where I pivot, because I can’t help myself. The synopsis *does* have one thing going for it: it’s unapologetically *bad* in a way that might actually be interesting. There’s a kind of brutal honesty to it, like a film that knows it’s a B-movie but doesn’t care. It’s not trying to be *The Handmaid’s Tale*—it’s trying to be *The Mummy* meets *The Craft*, and that’s a choice, even if it’s a bad one. The problem isn’t that it’s derivative; the problem is that it’s derivative *without* the self-awareness to make it fun. Where’s the irony? Where’s the wink to the reader? Instead, it just *is*, like a poorly executed costume party where everyone’s dressed as a "dark fantasy queen" but no one’s actually playing a role.

I am looking respectfully. I’m looking at this synopsis and wondering if the author has ever read *The Jewel in the Crown* or *The Palace of Illusions*. Both books took historical settings and wove them into something rich, layered, and *alive*. Instead, we get a story that feels like it was cobbled together from Wikipedia pages and a Pinterest board of "historical romance aesthetics." The courtesan training, the snake cult, the Alexander subplot—it’s all there, but none of it *means* anything. It’s like a puzzle where every piece is labeled "mystery" and "intrigue," but the box doesn’t come with instructions.

And yet—there’s a perverse thrill in the way it’s written. The synopsis is so *confidently* mediocre that it almost makes you want to read it, just to see how badly it falls apart. It’s the literary equivalent of a bad Tinder date who’s so sure of themselves that you can’t help but be intrigued. But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe *The Poison Palace* is meant to be a quick, forgettable read, a historical fantasy for people who want to feel clever without actually having to think. And if that’s the case, then I suppose it’s not *entirely* without merit. It’s just not the kind of merit that lasts.

I need to go yell at my gardener about the hydrangeas. They’re overgrown, just like this synopsis.
