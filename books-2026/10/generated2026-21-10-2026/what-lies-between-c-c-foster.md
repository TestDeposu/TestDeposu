**Author:** C.C. Foster  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Rain taps softly against the wrought-iron balcony outside, matching the dull ache behind my left eye and the quiet exhaustion that settles into one's bones after decades of acquiring things that ultimately mean nothing. Callidor sounds less like a city and more like a high-end tax evasion scheme managed by ghosts. And now we are expected to applaud a debut gothic fantasy about a decade spent in an asylum for the Tainted because Edgar Allan Poe is somehow considered fresh intellectual property in the year of our Lord two thousand and twenty-six. 

### The Architecture of Redundancy

Ianira Henbane deserves better than this narrative scaffolding. A woman immured for ten years—a charmingly archaic word that smells of damp velvet and cheap publicity stunts—emerges only to walk straight into a locked-room death game. It is the literary equivalent of the Costa Concordia captain steering the vessel back toward the rocks just to see if the second collision makes better copy. Lucien Blackwood hosts a banquet, poisons his guests, and demands a week-long trial of wits. Groundbreaking. One expects the caterer to hand out artisanal cyanide biscuits while a string quartet plays Vivaldi poorly. 

Call Greenwood to task for this. C.C. Foster borrows the structural skeletons of *The Tell-Tale Heart* and dresses them up in the moth-eaten furs of second-chance romance tropes. 

### The Pathology of Bad Exes

Callidor houses greedy residents. Lucien Blackwood is a murderer. Ianira is desperate. And yet, the synopsis insists on leaning into the oldest, most tedious crutch in contemporary fiction: the rekindling of adolescent passion while literal toxins dissolve the guests' internal organs. Nothing says eternal devotion quite like a slow-acting poison and a ticking clock. This dynamic feels less like forbidden yearning and more like the disastrous 1997 charity auction where someone accidentally bought three Tuscan olive groves instead of a single lithograph. 

Yet the prose promises lyricism. Lyrical prose usually means adjectives have been systematically weaponized against the reader. 

### The Inevitable Aftermath

The moral panic over toxic literary behavior will inevitably follow this release, as hordes of humorless internet commentators clutch their pearls over an antihero who dispenses lethal chemicals with canapés. They miss the point entirely. The true horror is not the magic gained through death or the institutionalization of the insane. The horror is the absolute lack of imagination in modern packaging. We have traded psychological depth for a bulleted list of tropes on the back cover. Death games. Creepy manors. Second chances. Why not simply stamp *Content Warning: Characters Make Poor Decisions* across the spine and save us the trouble of opening the dust jacket? 

My architect is downstairs, pacing the parquet with those infuriating blueprints for the south-facing terrace, and I must go explain to him why load-bearing walls are non-negotiable before the light completely fails.
