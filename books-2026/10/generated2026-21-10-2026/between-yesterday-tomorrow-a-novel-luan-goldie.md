Another summer heatwave, another predictable literary exercise in manufactured pathos designed to wring tears from the commuter train demographic. We are expected to greet Luan Goldie’s forthcoming *Between Yesterday & Tomorrow* with the usual breathless reverence, as if trauma recovery and forced proximity were brand-new literary inventions rather than the well-worn cogs of the contemporary mass-market machinery.

### The Architecture of Manufactured Resilience

The synopsis reads like an algorithm regurgitating every grief trope accumulated over the last decade. Zoe is broken—obviously—because no protagonist in modern fiction is allowed to possess an unbroken bone in their psychological anatomy. She is the mandatory former "party girl," a shorthand writers use when they cannot be bothered to develop actual interiority. Enter Cian, physically sidelined by a climbing accident, because apparently able-bodied individuals cannot possibly engage in deep emotional dialogue without a medical prop keeping them horizontal. They hide from the heat. They argue about Studio Ghibli. The sheer banality of it all is staggering. 

Yet, let us not be entirely dismissive of Goldie’s prose capabilities. Her previous work demonstrated a certain quiet competence, a domestic realism that occasionally rises above the sludge of standard book club fare. But this plot summary leans dangerously hard into the aesthetic of the airport kiosk. It smells of vanilla lattes and cheap tissues. 

Wait. Let me reconsider my absolute disdain for the premise. Perhaps the forced confinement of a summer heatwave offers a claustrophobic intensity that could elevate the cliché. Actually, no. The mention of Zoe needing to "take a leap of faith into the unknown" triggers immediate somatic exhaustion. It is mid-cult lit-fic trash masquerading as emotional depth. 

### The Commodification of Wounded Psyches

We are drowning in a sea of trauma-lit, a relentless market saturation where characters exist merely to heal one another through witty banter and shared vulnerability. It is absolute brain-rot for people who buy mass-produced scented candles in bulk. One longs for the austere cruelty of Joan Didion or the unapologetic bleakness of early Ian McEwan, rather than this sanitized pap where emotional growth is telegraphed via animated film debates. 

Goldie’s publisher is undoubtedly positioning this for the same audience that wept over *Lessons in Chemistry*—a comparison explicitly invited by the pull quote—though one suspects the execution here will lack even that book's mildly subversive bite. This novel will sell three hundred thousand copies by August, littering the sunbeds of every resort from Ibiza to Margate.
