**Author:** Sara Hashem  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Merciful Carnage* arrives like a poorly wrapped present—all glittering promise and a suspiciously loose bow. One minute you’re being sold a *dark fantasy duology* with the gravitas of a *Game of Thrones* prequel, the next you’re staring at a plot that could’ve been cribbed from a *Twilight* fanfiction draft. The idea of a "deathless healer" is so broad it might as well be a placeholder for "someone who does magic but isn’t a mage." And let’s not pretend the "soulmates-to-enemies" tagline isn’t just a desperate attempt to slap a trendy label on what feels like a *Bridgerton* meets *The Last of Us* mashup—because, honestly, where else would you even find that combo?

Tala’s backstory is the kind of setup that makes you wonder if the author was too busy Googling "how to make a character relatable" instead of writing. A deathless healer? Sure, why not? But the *real* question is: why does she *care* about proving herself? Because, let’s be real, if you can’t die, you’ve already won the lottery of existence. The banishment to Axsanda reads like a plot device pulled from a *Dune* knockoff, where the desert is just a fancy way of saying "Tala needs to leave her village so we can set up a romance." And the scorpions? Oh, the *scorpions*—because nothing says "I’m a fantasy novel" like giving your protagonist a pet sidekick that’s basically a furry, venomous ex.

Selim, the immortal High Judge, is the kind of character who would make a *Bond villain* jealous—chillingly beautiful, emotionally detached, and clearly the kind of guy who would’ve been a *Game of Thrones* side character if he’d just stopped being so *monolithic*. The synopsis sells him as a man who’s "centuries without his emotions," which is either a brilliant way to frame his character or a lazy excuse to avoid giving him any depth. Because let’s face it, if you’re immortal and your only priority is "maintaining sacred order," you’re basically a *God Emperor* with a spreadsheet. And the fact that he’s "hunting for an answer to her existence" is either a *very* roundabout way of saying "he’s attracted to her" or a *very* lazy way of avoiding actual character development.

The plague? The dark omens? The "catastrophic consequences" of their attraction? It’s all so vague it might as well be a *Choose Your Own Adventure* book where the author forgot to write the endings. The thematic potential here is *staggering*—immortality, power dynamics, the cost of healing, the ethics of survival—but the execution feels like it’s still in the "rough draft" phase. And the fact that this is being pitched as a *duology* makes me wonder if Hashem is banking on the "slow burn" trope, because right now, it just feels like a *lot* of setup for *nothing*.

The real kicker? The synopsis doesn’t even *try* to hide the fact that this is a *romance-first* fantasy. The "attraction" is treated like a *given*, not a *discovery*, and the "catastrophic consequences" line is just a fancy way of saying "they’ll fight, they’ll kiss, the world will end." It’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if the author was too busy watching *The Vampire Diaries* reruns to actually think about what makes a fantasy novel *good*.

But here’s the thing—if Hashem pulls this off, it could be *something*. The bones are there: a deathless healer, an immortal judge, a plague, a romance, a duology. It’s the kind of setup that could’ve been *great* if it weren’t so *predictable*. And if she leans into the *psychological* weight of immortality—if she makes Selim’s detachment *earned*, if she makes Tala’s deathlessness *haunting*—then maybe, just maybe, this could be more than just another *dark fantasy* with a pretty cover.

But right now? It’s just another book waiting for its author to stop treating it like a *TikTok trend* and start treating it like *literature*.
