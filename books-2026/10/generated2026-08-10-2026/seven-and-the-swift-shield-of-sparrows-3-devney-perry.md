Flour is currently dusting the kitchen counter because the toddler insisted on dumping half a bag into the sink while my back was turned for literally three seconds, which is just fantastic timing for trying to parse the microscopic scraps of information Devney Perry has deigned to give us about *Seven and the Swift*, the supposed final installment of the *Shield of Sparrows* trilogy. 

Wait. Actually, let me walk that back entirely—maybe sparse marketing is brilliant for an epic slow-burn romantasy, or maybe it just means the publisher has nothing concrete to show us yet because the manuscript is currently living in a state of quantum uncertainty between three different hard drives. 

And yet, we are supposed to write an entire anticipatory preview based on a blurb shorter than a grocery list. 

Trilogy conclusions are notoriously tricky beasts. One second you are enjoying a lovely, slow-burn fantasy romance reminiscent of Jennifer L. Armentrout or Sarah J. Maas, and the next you are drowning in a sea of invented glossary terms and map-page-flipping just to remember who the secondary antagonists are. Perry made her absolute fortune writing small-town contemporary romances filled with bearded men who fix trucks and grumpy heroines who run bakeries—which, ironically, is roughly my actual life minus the brooding alphas—so watching her pivot into high fantasy with *Shield of Sparrows* and *Rites of the Starling* has been fascinating to observe from the sidelines of the mixing bowl. 

Exhausting.

The thematic core here clearly hinges on avian motifs, obviously, given the titles, but without a single plot sentence for *Seven and the Swift*, we are left staring blindly into the void of 2026. Will the slow-burn finally ignite into something resembling pacing, or will the characters spend another four hundred pages staring soulfully across castle courtyards while various birds fly overhead to symbolize their internal emotional trauma? 

Probably both. 

Authors love a high-stakes trilogy closer, but romance-heavy fantasy often crumbles under the weight of its own world-building by book three. 

Fingers crossed the editor brought a heavy red pen. 

Anyway, I have to go stop the eight-year-old from attempting a Chopin étude at deafening volume on the upright in the parlor.
