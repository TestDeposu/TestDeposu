**Author:** Taylor Jenkins Reid  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Last Days of Vic & Coco* reads like a love letter to the kind of women who’ve been erased from history—except Reid’s never written one of those. She’s written about the women who *wrote* themselves into the margins, the ones who knew how to disappear before they were gone. Vic and Coco aren’t just hustlers; they’re a pressure test for the idea of sisterhood under capitalism, a duet of trust and betrayal that feels like it could’ve been written by a younger, grittier Patricia Highsmith, if Highsmith had spent more time in a pool hall than a Swiss alpine chalet.

The synopsis leans into the *Daisy Jones & The Six* formula—big payoff, big risk, big emotional whiplash—but where *Daisy* was a band’s last stand, this is a con’s last roll. The structure is classic Reid: a slow burn of tension, a promise of heist-movie spectacle, and a lingering question about whether the real score was ever the money. The oil crisis of 1974 isn’t just a backdrop; it’s a metaphor for the way Vic and Coco’s world is collapsing around them, just like the economy. And yet—here’s the twist—they’re not victims. They’re the ones holding the deck. That’s the kind of subversion Reid does best, the way she takes a trope (the female duo, the final heist) and asks: *What if the real con is believing they’re ever safe?*

But—and this is where the synopsis stumbles—where’s the *humanity*? Reid’s best work (*The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo*) thrives on the tension between performance and truth. Here, Vic and Coco are defined by their roles: Vic the strategist, Coco the charmer. No backstories, no cracks in the armor. It’s like reading a *Heat* script where the characters haven’t yet realized they’re in a movie. The synopsis hints at a fracture—“they want different things”—but doesn’t dare explore it. Where’s the moment when Vic realizes Coco’s been lying about her past? Where’s the scene where Coco admits she’s not just playing the game, she’s *living* in it? Reid’s strength is making us care about the lies before we care about the people. This synopsis doesn’t even *try*.

And speaking of trying—why does every book about women in the ’70s have to be a *Bonnie and Clyde* reboot? *The Last Days of Vic & Coco* feels like it’s competing with *Thelma & Louise* for the “women who drive fast and get caught” trophy, but where’s the originality? Where’s the Reid twist that makes us see this as something more than a period piece with a bigger car? The synopsis doesn’t even mention the *desert highways*—just the *seedy motels and dive bars*, which is fine, but it’s the kind of setting that could’ve been lifted from a *True Detective* pilot if it weren’t for the women. And women, in Reid’s hands, are usually more than just scenery.

But fine, let’s grant the synopsis its due: it’s got the Reidian DNA. The con structure, the unreliable narrator vibe, the promise of a payday that’s either liberation or ruin. It’s the kind of book that’ll make you want to high-five a stranger in a bookstore while also wondering if you’re the mark. And yet—why does it feel so *safe*? Reid’s best work (*Malibu Rising*) is about the cost of fame, the way power corrupts even the most charismatic. Here, the stakes feel personal but not *existential*. Vic and Coco are playing with fire, but the fire isn’t burning yet.

And then there’s the title—*The Last Days*—which could mean anything. Is it the last days of their friendship? Their hustle? Their youth? The synopsis doesn’t say. It’s the kind of ambiguity Reid loves, but it’s also the kind that makes you wonder if the book will deliver. Because the last thing we need is another Reid novel where the payoff is just another love letter to the idea of rebellion without the reckoning.

But hey, at least it’s not like that *Bluey* episode where the dog keeps stealing the mail. At least.
