**Page Count:** Unknown

Another fictional universe where collateral damage involves literal soul-brokerage instead of a botched corporate acquisition. Oh, look, Cortney L. Winn is dragging us back into whatever dark, brooding sandbox *Vesselless* crawled out of. *Merciless* promises us a glorious parade of psychological torture, Stockholm syndrome adjacent bargaining, and enough interpersonal backstabbing to make a board of directors look like a kindergarten playgroup. The synopsis reads like a fever dream penned by someone who unironically listens to industrial goth at three in the morning while staring at an empty balance sheet. And honestly? I respect the hustle.

### The Economics of Suffering

We have Nizzara, who traded her literal soul—an asset valuation I can only describe as catastrophic—to save Dagen, only to get instantly side-swiped by a traitor and gift-wrapped to Queen Nexia. Classic high-fantasy asset forfeiture. She’s stripped of power, playing 4D chess with a captor who probably drinks black coffee and despises joy. Meanwhile, Lo is drowning in buyer’s remorse back home, hunting magic rings while some random potion-maker rolls up to demand a life debt payable in matrimonial collateral. It is melodramatic, exhausting, and dripping with the kind of high-stakes transactional angst that hits different when you are burning through another bottle of twenty-year-old Scotch while the autumn wind howls against the penthouse glass. 

### Tropes for the Doomed

Let's dissect the literary DNA here. We are leaning heavily into the "forced proximity with the person who ruined your life" trope, which is basically just corporate restructuring with more swords. Winn is weaponizing emotional debt like a hedge fund manager shorting a failing biotech stock. Dagen's sudden character shift—going from devoted paramour to frosty ice cube because of some mysterious stranger's shadow—is deliciously petty. Yet, one has to wonder if this entire plot is just an elaborate metaphor for a prenuptial agreement gone nuclear. 

I expect this thing to read like Sarah J. Maas mated with a particularly bleak session of contract law. It will probably sell a million copies to people who think crying over fictional characters builds character. Maybe it does. 

I am closing this tab and heading over to browse Tuscan real estate listings to cleanse my palate of all this peasant drama.
