**Author:** Alyssa Cole  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Steam hisses from the commercial wand behind me, drowning out the eighth rendition of an acoustic indie cover playlist that I swear is designed to slowly erode the sanity of anyone earning minimum wage while holding a master's degree. Not that I'm keeping track. Yet here we are, staring down Alyssa Cole’s upcoming *It Takes a Village*, a spiritual successor to *When No One Is Watching*. Another slick, high-concept thriller capitalizing on structural rot and corporate gaslighting. Because apparently, we all need another fictional reminder that the housing crisis is actually a shadowy conspiracy rather than the slow, grinding machinery of late-stage capitalism. Groundbreaking.

The premise practically writes itself. Eboni Goodman trades her hard-earned Master’s in Social Work and a soul-crushing grocery delivery route for an administrator gig in a shiny, experimental South Jersey utopian village for the unhoused. Color me shocked when the locals hate them and the pristine philanthropic facade starts shedding paint to reveal literal tech glitches and buried town secrets. But wait. Let me back up and correct myself. Maybe the synopsis-writers just leaned too hard on the buzzwords because they couldn't think of anything else to put in the pitch. The marketing copy reads like a pitch deck generated by an algorithm fed exclusively on Jordan Peele movies and prestige paperback thrillers. 

Actually, let's break the fourth wall for a second because I am legitimately staring at an empty word document trying to stretch this out just to make rent this week, wondering why I spend my life analyzing plot synopses about fictional gentrification nightmares when the real nightmare is standing right here wiping oat milk off a reclaimed wood counter. 

Anyway. The thematic weight here rests entirely on the juxtaposition of grassroots optimism against systemic cruelty. Cole proved with *When No One Is Watching* that she knows how to weaponize real estate data and historical erasure into genuine dread. The dread of the HOA meeting. The terror of the property tax assessment. Yet I worry the corporate thriller genre has become too predictable. We get it. Rich people are monsters and benevolent social programs are secretly human testing grounds. Can we please get a horror novel about something mundane, like an editorial board that actually knows what a semicolon does? 

Enter Michael. The broody security guard. Every single thriller needs a brooding local with a mysterious past to lean against a chain-link fence and smoke a cigarette while the protagonist uncovers a municipal cover-up. It is a legal requirement at this point, enshrined in some sacred covenant known only to literary agents. 

Expect the usual suspects. A dash of Shirley Jacksonian paranoia mixed with modern corporate surveillance dread. It will sit comfortably on airport bookstore tables right next to Riley Sager and Grady Hendrix. Whether it actually delivers anything resembling a pulse-pounding climax or just devolves into a three-hundred-page lecture on zoning laws remains entirely to be seen based on this aggressively vague plot summary. But I suppose we will all pre-order it anyway. 

I have to go write a scathing editorial on the commercialization of modern art before the afternoon rush starts and someone asks me for a decat macchiato with four pumps of vanilla.
