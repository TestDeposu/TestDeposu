Uncle Frank is currently clearing his throat for what promises to be the seventh rendition of a war story involving a faulty toaster in Frankfurt, while out in the hallway, someone’s toddler is weaponizing a plastic drum kit against the wainscoting. Yet I find myself mentally escaping to a desolate stretch of Irish coastline because Catherine Walsh has decided to torture us all again with *Only One Island*. 

### The Geography of Grudges

Three thousand miles. That is the exact distance required to successfully evade your former partner, according to the laws of romantic comedy geography. But Walsh wisely short-circuits this tired trope before the ink even dries. Grace thinks she is trading the concrete chaos of Toronto for a windswept solitude—complete with a solitary donkey and zero electricity—only to discover that her fellow caretaker is the exact human being she crossed an ocean to forget. Nathan. The name alone sounds like damp tweed and unresolved text message receipts. 

Except the synopsis plays it remarkably safe. No electricity? A donkey? Fine. We get it. It’s rustic charm designed to force two people into terrifyingly close proximity until their unresolved baggage combusts. But let us be entirely honest here—or wait, let me actually retract that sweeping generalization about narrative safety because the forced proximity trope on a literal rock in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean is the exact kind of claustrophobic pressure cooker that separates brilliant contemporary romance from absolute garbage. 

### Clashing in the Cold

This isn't Emily Henry territory where everyone communicates with sparkling wit while wearing impeccably curated knitwear. Walsh usually leans into the messy, stubborn, unglamorous friction of ordinary people who refuse to admit they were wrong. Or maybe she doesn't. The official blurb relies heavily on the "win the breakup" directive—a delightfully petty motivator that instantly elevates the stakes from mere pining to absolute warfare. Outlasting Nathan becomes an Olympic sport fueled by stale tea and damp mattresses. 

And who can blame them? There is something deliciously punishing about the remote island setup. It strips away modern distractions—the glowing screens, the easy exits, the polite coffee shop breakups—and leaves only the raw, jagged edges of two stubborn egos. Yet I worry the execution might lean too heavily into predictable slapstick with the aforementioned donkey. Actually, strike that. If the donkey doesn't become the primary emotional anchor and unbiased judge of their failing communication skills, the entire publishing industry has failed us. 

Nathan and Grace are destined to clash spectacularly, but the real tension lies in the silence between the arguments. When the generator is dead and the Atlantic wind is howling against the stone walls of the guesthouse, clever retorts lose their edge. 

Uncle Frank has officially moved on to complaining about property taxes, and the volume of that piano practice down the hall is threatening to crack the plaster directly above my head.
