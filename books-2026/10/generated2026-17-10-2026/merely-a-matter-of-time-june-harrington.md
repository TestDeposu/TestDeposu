Flour is currently dusting my countertops like a light December snowfall, and the youngest is screaming about a blue crayon lodged in the radiator while I try to parse the mechanics of temporal displacement in June Harrington’s upcoming *Merely a Matter of Time*. 

Why do contemporary speculative writers insist on sending overworked academics into the Regency era? It is a tired parlor game. Give me a locked-room poison mystery in a Victorian vicarage any day over another pair of bickering scientists accidentally detonating their funding while wearing sensible shoes. 

### The Chronology of Yearning

Lucy Bright and Francis de Montmort are stranded two hundred years in the past. They possess an arcane heirloom and a broken machine. They must avoid ancestors. Groundbreaking. But let us look closer at the philosophical weight here. Determinism versus free will tucked inside a cable-knit cardigan? Please. It sounds like Connie Willis and Jane Austen were tossed into a blender with a very expensive chemistry textbook. 

And speaking of flour, the third batch of shortbread is burning because I spent ten minutes staring at the phrase "he falls first and he falls harder" on my screen. Romance marketing has become aggressively literal. Do we need a bulleted list to tell us that opposites attract? Back in my day, we had to infer emotional devastation from a lingering glance across a damp drawing room. Now the synopsis reads like a clinical intake form for chronic pining. 

### The Physics of Banter

The publisher promises slow-burn banter. Slow-burn usually means three hundred pages of two brilliant idiots refusing to acknowledge their own cardiovascular responses. It drives me mad. My six-year-old just tried to bake a plastic dinosaur into the sourdough starter. That makes more sense than sending an infernal sprite into a nineteenth-century horserace. 

Harrington is leaning hard into the cozy speculative trend. Think *Emily Wilde’s Encyclopedia of Faeries* meets the *Bridgerton* writers' room. Yet I worry the magic will serve merely as theatrical set-dressing for a standard academic-rivals-to-lovers arc. Science deserves better than being an excuse for forced proximity. 

I predict this book will either be a masterclass in witty restraint or an exhausting exercise in modern millennials dropping twenty-first-century irony into muddy Georgian lanes. My money is on the latter. Yet I will buy it anyway. 

I am operating on three hours of sleep and pure espresso.
