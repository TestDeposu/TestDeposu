**Author:** Cebo Campbell  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Fresh calfskin bindings ought to smell of sulfur and old money, not the stale desperation of a marketing department trying desperately to recapture the lightning of Colson Whitehead’s early output. Cebo Campbell returns in 2026 with *Wells Without Water*, a sophomore effort that currently relies entirely on a publisher’s plot sketch to carry its hefty ideological ambitions. But let us be entirely frank about what we are looking at here. A fifteen-year-old walking into a police station with murder on the mind feels less like authentic Southern gothic and more like an episode of *Succession* directed by someone who only reads literary theory via Twitter threads. 

### The Weight of Ancestral Rot

The synopsis leans heavily on the usual suspects of contemporary literary horror—generational trauma, decaying municipal blocks, and supernatural possessions that double as metaphors for systemic oppression. We have Carmichael Franklin, a cynical legal investigator summoned back to the crumbling enclave of Loblolly Grove. A murdered caretaker named Ma Esther. A Bishop brother dealing in demonic possession. It is the literary equivalent of the Costa Concordia captaining itself onto the rocks of predictable allegorical ambition. 

Campbell attempts to marry the rhythmic cadence of a Black sermon with the hallucinatory surrealism of the American South. Yet, translating the magical roots of Nat Turner’s rebellion into a cosmic horror framework is a high-wire act that demands absolute stylistic precision. Slip even an inch, and you land squarely in the realm of high-concept pastiche. 

### The Perils of the Heavy-Handed Metaphor

Art dies the moment it hands the reader a flashlight and points directly at the symbolism. When a synopsis explicitly boasts about bringing to light the cosmic horror of slavery and the weaponization of religion, my antique spectacles slide down my nose in sheer exhaustion. Why trust the intelligence of the audience when you can simply emboss the ideological thesis statement directly onto the dust jacket? Give me ambiguity, or give me the shredder. 

One suspects this novel will be aggressively championed by corporate book clubs that treat literature as moral cod liver oil. They will swallow it quickly, wince, and pretend they enjoyed the flavor. I intend to inspect the first-edition run with a magnifying glass, check the paper weight, and perhaps find some solace in the typeface. 

I am going to pour a glass of fifteen-year-old scotch and log off.
