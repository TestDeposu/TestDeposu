**Page Count:** Unknown

The soil out by the south pasture is holding too much water this spring, turning every footprint into a muddy basin that collects gray water and dead leaves. *Murder Most Cryptic* arrives on the publishing scene bearing a similar sort of heavy, waterlogged groundwork—relying on the tired trope of the dysfunctional support group where every single member conveniently possesses a quirky, pigeonholed archetype. We have the bossy former teacher, the crossword-loving retired cop, the socially awkward bus driver, and the mandatory yoga enthusiast. Because apparently, no modern cozy mystery can exist without ticking off a demographic spreadsheet. 

Alys Cummings sets her debut in the UK, anchoring the narrative around a protagonist whose messy alcoholism is billed as an unreliable lens. Yet, I take it back—maybe that unreliability is precisely what saves the enterprise from sinking entirely into the mire of predictable genre conventions. Or perhaps it is just another lazy narrative crutch designed to obscure a thin plot. 

The compost heap smells particularly foul this morning because the raccoons got into the vegetable scraps again, tearing open the biodegradable canvas sacks I spent three hours stitching together last Sunday. Speaking of pests tearing things apart, modern publishing houses are currently churning out these gimmick-laden amateur sleuth novels faster than a factory farm produces phosphate runoff. It reminds me of those ghastly children's cartoons where the cute woodland animals solve crimes by baking pies, except here the woodland animals are miserable Britons exchanging passive-aggressive glances over lukewarm instant coffee in a damp church basement. 

Yet, beneath the glossy marketing promises of clever easter eggs and crossword jargon, the thematic undercurrent touches on something genuinely fractured: the desperate human need for collective dignity when individual agency has completely rotted away. Nina wants to give a dead stranger his dignity back, even as she loses her own grip on reality. 

### Ink, Sludge, and the Unreliable Page

The thematic exploration of addiction intertwined with structured wordplay offers a fascinating structural parallel. Crosswords demand order, symmetry, and definitive answers within rigid black-and-white grids. Addiction and sudden murder break those exact boxes. Cummings utilizes the cryptic clue as a literal map for human chaos. I predict this will either read like a sharp, witty homage to Richard Osman's *The Thursday Murder Club* or devolve into an insufferable exercise in British twee preciousness that makes one long for the sweet release of a chemical pesticide. 

The synopsis leans heavily on the gimmick of the hidden clue, yet tells us almost nothing about the systemic pressures of the newsroom where Nina and the victim supposedly crossed paths. We are left squinting at a blank grid, waiting for the author to fill in the blanks. 

I need to go power-wash the driveway, which is my only solace.
