One simply grows weary of the contemporary obsession with temporal gymnastics. Every third manuscript arriving from the midtown houses insists upon fracturing the linear sequence of human existence, as though the straightforward unfolding of a life lacks sufficient drama without a party bus full of chronological phantoms. Julie Buxbaum’s upcoming *All Together Now* commits this exact narrative sin with breathless, unrelenting cheer. 

We are asked to swallow a premise so aggressively whimsical it reads like a rejected episode of *Succession* directed by Baz Luhrmann during a fever dream. 

### The Council of Eighteen-Year-Old Bangs

Hazel Stein wakes up every decade on a vehicle featuring a stripper pole to commune with her past and future iterations. Eight-year-old Hazel is folding napkins. Forty-nine-year-old Hazel is bald and gaunt. Seventy-seven-year-old Hazel possesses a gash on her forehead. And eighty-year-old Duke Ellington writing the *Sacred Concert* in a municipal swimming pool makes about as much sense as this particular seating chart. 

Consider the sheer logistical nightmare of conversing with one’s own previous insecurities while wearing threadbare clothes. The synopsis promises a dazzling journey through three great loves, the sandwich years, and the seismic shock of unexpected grief. But it relies entirely on the sort of manufactured conceit that treats emotional maturation like a series of costume changes in a provincial dressing room. 

Alison Espach and Emma Straub might pull off this brand of suburban domestic whimsy with a straight face. Buxbaum, judging purely by this text-blurb architectural blueprint, leans heavily into the kind of high-concept gimmickry designed exclusively for book clubs that require a Chardonnay vintage just to get through the prologue. 

### Chronological Insolence

What do we actually owe the people we used to be? Nothing. Absolutely nothing at all. We owe them the courtesy of forgetting our catastrophic bangs and moving briskly past the twenty-eight-year-old phase without demanding an audience on a neon-lit transit vehicle. The conceit assumes a perpetual dialogue with the self is a blessing. It sounds like an absolute auditory torture chamber. 

One shudders to imagine forty-nine-year-old Hazel staring down the terrified eight-year-old across a purple argyle carpet. It feels less like a profound meditation on mortality and more like the plot of a terrible high-concept studio comedy from nineteen-ninety-eight starring Meg Ryan that everyone collectively agreed to bury in a salt mine. 

Fate is sealed. Or it is not. Either way, the synopsis offers precious little breathing room for actual human observation, preferring instead a frantic assembly line of generational self-actualization. One prefers literature that trusts the quiet passage of time rather than hauling every single decade of a woman's wardrobe into a single cramped interior. 

The persistent draft coming from my poorly insulated window is unbearable.
