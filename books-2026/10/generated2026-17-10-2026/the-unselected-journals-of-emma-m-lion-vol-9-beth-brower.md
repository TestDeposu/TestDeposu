The synopsis for *The Unselected Journals of Emma M. Lion: Vol. 9* is so deliberately opaque it might as well be a Victorian-era cipher—except the cipher is just a wink. The year is 1884, the setting is St. Crispian’s, and Emma M. Lion, that arch, self-incriminating diarist, is back. But what, exactly, is she unselecting this time? The answer, of course, is everything. Because the brilliance of this series—if the first volumes are any indication—lies in its refusal to be pinned down. Emma’s journals are less a chronicle and more a performance, a series of asides from a woman who knows she’s being watched, by history, by her own conscience, and by the reader who’s already in on the joke.

The comparisons to Austen, Wilde, and Wodehouse aren’t just marketing fluff; they’re a clue. Emma’s voice is a hybrid of sharp social satire and absurdist whimsy, the kind of wit that could make a P.G. Wodehouse character weep with laughter before realizing they’ve been had. But where Wodehouse’s humor is often gentle, Emma’s is a scalpel—dry, precise, and occasionally cruel. The "Unselected" in the title isn’t just a nod to the journals’ raw, unfiltered nature; it’s a meta-commentary on the act of selection itself. Who gets to decide what’s worth preserving? Emma, of course, but only because she’s already decided she’s the most interesting person in the room. And yet—there’s a vulnerability there, buried beneath the sarcasm. The journals aren’t just confessions; they’re a defense mechanism.

The vagueness of the synopsis is intentional, really. It’s the same kind of vagueness that makes *The Secret History* by Donna Tartt so frustratingly addictive—you don’t need to know the plot to know you’ll be obsessed. Emma’s world is one of rigid social hierarchies, where every glance, every word, is a landmine. The theatricality of it all—costume protocol, persona, the very idea of a "journal" as a performative act—feels like a love letter to the excesses of the era, but also a critique. Emma isn’t just observing; she’s participating, and the reader is complicit. That’s the real genius of the series: it turns the act of reading into a kind of collusion.

But let’s talk about something entirely unrelated, because I refuse to be reasonable. I spent an hour yesterday watching *Bluey* with my daughter, and I swear, that show is the most aggressively anti-Victorian thing ever produced. No corsets, no stifling propriety, just a bunch of dogs running around a backyard like they’ve never heard of shame. Meanwhile, here we are, in 2024, still trying to figure out how to raise children without turning them into little automatons. It’s infuriating. And yet, *Bluey* is the kind of thing that makes me miss the days when children’s literature was at least *trying* to be morally ambiguous. Now it’s all just… warmth. No edges. No Emma M. Lion-level of existential dread disguised as a bedtime story. But I digress.

The point is, *The Unselected Journals* thrives on that tension between warmth and edge. Emma’s world is one of sharp contrasts—elegance and decay, wit and melancholy, the public persona and the private self. The fact that we’re getting a new volume in 2026 is a testament to how well Brower balances these elements. The synopsis doesn’t give us much, but it doesn’t need to. The real magic is in the gaps, in the implication that Emma’s next entry will be just as revelatory as the last. And if the first volumes are any indication, we’re in for a treat.

I have a PTA meeting to attend, and I already regret agreeing to it. The thought of sitting through another discussion about whether the school should serve gluten-free cupcakes on field trip days is enough to make me want to burn the entire volume of Emma’s journals—just to see what she’d say about it.
