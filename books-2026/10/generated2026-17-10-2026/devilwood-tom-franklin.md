**Page Count:** Unknown

My roommate is currently screaming at a Wi-Fi router because his paleobotanical simulation crashed for the fourth consecutive night, which means I am staring at yet another piece of promotional literature about rural Alabama while living off instant ramen that costs less than fifty cents. Tom Franklin is back with *Devilwood*, and if I have to read one more marketing blurb about blood-crazed cats and shadowy woods before my student loan refund clears, I am going to throw my laptop out the third-floor window. 

### The Pathology of Rural Noir

There is an exhausting literary obsession with treating the American South as a monolithic gothic petting zoo. Every single time, we get the same tropes recycled like cheap plastics—shady undertakers, charismatic preachers who shout about the Devil, and some poor sap with a ridiculous nickname like “The Cow Man.” Actually, wait. Let me walk that back. Maybe the regional specificity is precisely the point here. Franklin wrote *Crooked Letter, Crooked Letter*, which genuinely worked because the pacing respected the reader's intelligence instead of leaning entirely on feral white cats delivering grotesque offerings to a dead woman's door. Still. The synopsis reads like a checklist pulled from a southern-gothic-generator plugged into a dusty generator in 1994. 

### Predators Versus Metaphors

The central tension of *Devilwood* supposedly hinges on the porous boundary between actual biological predators and human malice. Except the synopsis immediately ruins its own philosophical ambiguity by telling us precisely what we already know — that not all monsters are supernatural. Oh, really? Do tell. Because for a second I thought the local undertaker was actually a shape-shifting entity from an extra-dimensional void. The intellectual laziness of spelling out the thematic thesis right on the back cover drives me insane. If you are going to write a book about the sins of the past refusing to stay buried—and trust me, they never do, they practically pay rent at this point—at least let the ecosystem make some biological sense. Do you know how much caloric intake a blood-crazed cat needs to survive in rural Alabama? More than a dead hermit can provide, that is for sure. 

### Anticipating the Blood-Soaked Soil

The prose will probably be sharp. Franklin can write a sentence, nobody is disputing that part. But I am so tired of plots driven exclusively by things lurking just inside the tree line. Give me a narrative where the horror is just bad municipal zoning laws or lack of proper public funding for local libraries. That would terrify me far more than a charismatic preacher talking about the abyss. Yet here we are, bracing for another masterpiece of atmospheric misery where the setting acts as a character because the actual humans are too busy carrying generational trauma and hiding secrets close to their chests. I cannot afford coffee today, which means my cynicism is running at peak capacity. 

I am going to put on a battered Tom Waits record and forget this narrative.
