**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-24 APL

Double espresso shots at four in the morning do not foster patience for vague fantasy tropes. Yet here we are, examining Rachel Greenlaw’s upcoming *The Divines*. The synopsis for this second installment of the *Killmarth Trials* promises escalating stakes, dead mentors, and gods who are apparently waking up cranky. 

### The Geography of Resentment and Recovery

The premise leans heavily on the architecture of grief. Sophia is mourning. Fine. We have all mourned something—perhaps my last trust fund disbursement or the demise of actual literary subtlety in YA fantasy. The synopsis tells us the Great Hunt has begun. It does not tell us why we should care beyond the standard-issue death toll. We get the usual genre boilerplate: escalating attacks by the Cold Ones, a frantic hunt for an antidote, and a sudden revelation that the Crown is lying. Shocking. Next, you will tell me politicians are corrupt. 

Greenlaw is recycling the structural beats of Leigh Bardugo without the linguistic grit. Or perhaps a Diet Coke version of Sarah J. Maas, minus the three-page descriptions of leather corsetry. 

### Epistemological Crises in the Crown

Let us focus on the institutional betrayal. The synopsis mentions uncovering disturbing secrets kept by the Crown. This is the structural equivalent of the Fyre Festival cheese sandwich—an overhyped disaster we all saw coming from a mile away. Trusting authority figures in a fantasy sequel is about as wise as investing in crypto during a congressional hearing. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that. Trusting the Crown is actually the only rational option if the alternative is another four hundred pages of protagonists whining about trust issues while the world burns. 

Actually, scratch that entire thought. The real issue isn't institutional corruption; it's the sheer exhaustion of gods biding their time. 

### The Divine Appetite

The theological implications here are deliciously bleak. Old Gods coming back from retirement because they are hungry? Sounds less like epic mythology and more like the catering budget at Davos falling through. But Greenlaw wants us to tremble at the wrath of forgotten deities. The prose of the synopsis suggests a relentless march toward God-like proportions of magic, which usually translates to forty consecutive chapters of glowing hands and screaming into the void. 

Can we please retire the phrase "steamy, propulsive"? It reads like the jacket copy of a romance novel desperately trying to moonlight as high epic fantasy.
