**Author:** Jayci Lee  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The flour is currently coating every single surface of my kitchen because the eight-year-old insisted on cracking eggs directly into the mixing bowl without supervision, and frankly, *Not My Type* by Jayci Lee looks like the exact kind of high-octane emotional rescue I need once this sugar-fueled chaos finally subsides. 

### Koreatown Coffee and Class Struggle

We are looking at yet another *Pride and Prejudice* spin-off, which honestly makes me roll my eyes so hard I might pull a muscle, but putting it in Los Angeles' Koreatown gives it a fresh pulse that standard regency pastiche completely lacks. Jane Austen would probably approve of the capitalist critique here. Or she would hate it. Who cares. The synopsis leans hard into the classic enemies-to-lovers architecture—librarian versus corporate CEO—and while that trope is older than my grandmother's sourdough starter, Jayci Lee usually delivers enough sharp banter to keep things afloat. 

And let us be real for a second. If I have to read one more billionaire romance where the hero looks like a Greek god and acts like a damp sponge, I am going to throw my rolling pin through the drywall. 

Speaking of structural integrity, why do middle-grade chapter books feature such aggressively annoying talking animals lately? My youngest has been forcing me to listen to an audiobook about a squirrel detective who solves crimes using acorns, and the audio production is so shrill it makes my teeth vibrate while I am trying to measure out vanilla extract. At least Jayci Lee writes adults who presumably possess the capacity for basic eye contact and synchronized breathing. 

### The Slow Burn We Actually Deserve

The promise of K-drama-level yearning is doing some heavy lifting in this synopsis. Give me the lingering glances. Give me the hand-flexing. Give me the sheer agony of two stubborn people refusing to admit they are obsessed with each other while standing too close in a tight hallway. Chloe Gong and Emily Wibberley mastered this exact brand of delicious, slow-burn tension, and Lee has the chops to pull it off without making the characters sound like teenagers texting on a bus. 

Yet, I remain deeply suspicious of vague synopses. "Their lives continue to intersect in unexpected ways." Wow. Groundbreaking. Do they bump into each other at a organic grocery store? Do their dogs get their leashes tangled? Tell me something real, blurb-writers. I want grit. I want messy character flaws that cannot be solved by a nice dinner and a shared look across a dimly lit room. 

The thematic core here relies on shedding expectations, which is rich coming from someone whose entire daily schedule is dictated by three small humans demanding carbohydrate-heavy snacks every forty-five minutes. But hey, reading about fictional people breaking free from familial obligation is cheaper than therapy, and definitely less messy than this counter top. 

I need to go frost three dozen cupcakes by tomorrow morning or the school bake sale is going to feature my tear-stained panic disguised as vanilla buttercream.
