**Author:** Kelley Armstrong  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The first thing that strikes you about *Last Resort at Haven’s Rock* is how it leans into the kind of structural laziness that makes you wonder if the author ever read *The Girl with All the Gifts*—because, like that book’s infamous reveal, this one seems to think it can get away with burying its stakes in a town that doesn’t even exist yet. Haven’s Rock, Yukon. Population: zero. A place for people to disappear. A fresh start. And yet—here we are, already in the weeds of a mystery that feels less like a detective story and more like a real estate pitch with a body count.

The synopsis does what so many crime novels do these days: it frames itself as a bold reinvention, a spinoff with "new dangers," as if the original *Rockton* novels weren’t already drowning in their own brand of existential dread. But the problem isn’t that this is a sequel—it’s that it’s a sequel that hasn’t bothered to outgrow its own clichés. The couple, Casey and Eric, are back, but their dynamic feels less like a partnership and more like a corporate retreat where the only rule is "don’t talk to strangers (or the forest)." And the forest, of course, is the perfect stand-in for the unknown, the thing that lurks just beyond the headlights of the genre’s usual tropes. But why bother with originality when you can just swap out the setting and call it a day?

The real sin here is the way the synopsis treats the reader like an idiot. Two construction workers break the rules—*stay out of the forest*—and go missing. A body is found. Mystery ensues. It’s the kind of plot that would make *True Detective*’s Rust Cohle roll his eyes so hard he’d dislocate his jaw. The forest isn’t just a setting; it’s a metaphor for the genre’s own self-imposed limitations. And yet, the synopsis doesn’t even bother to hint at what makes this forest different from the last one. Is it haunted? Is it a front for something worse? Or is it just another excuse to have Casey and Eric stumble into danger because, at the end of the day, that’s what readers expect from a detective story—even if it’s the same old story dressed up in new clothes.

The thematic weight here is all about reinvention, but the execution feels like it’s been rushed through a paper shredder. The idea of a town built for people to disappear is compelling—it’s the kind of premise that could work if it weren’t so heavily reliant on the same old beats. The original *Rockton* novels had a certain grit, a sense that the world was as messy as the characters. But this feels more like a franchise extension, a way to milk the brand for another season of the same old formula. And the forest? It’s not even a character yet, just a placeholder for whatever horror the author hasn’t bothered to define.

At the end of the day, this isn’t a story about reinvention—it’s a story about repetition, about the way genres and franchises keep churning out the same old product with the same old problems. The synopsis doesn’t even try to sell you on the idea that this is anything new. It’s just another mystery, another couple, another forest. And if that’s the case, then why bother? Why not just read *The Girl with All the Gifts* again and save yourself the trouble?
