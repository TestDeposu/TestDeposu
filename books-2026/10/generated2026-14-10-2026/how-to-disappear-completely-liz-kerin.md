**Author:** Liz Kerin  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis of *How to Disappear Completely* is a glittering, half-buried minefield—just like the kind of mystery that makes you suspect the author’s been reading too many *True Detective* season 1 scripts while sipping something dangerously strong in a dimly lit bar. The premise is a masterclass in controlled ambiguity, the kind of thing that makes you wonder if Liz Kerin’s been playing with the same psychological fire as *The Last House on Needless Street* but with the emotional temperature cranked up to "slow-burn paranoia." The opening lines—*"There’s an explanation for everything. There’s nothing to be afraid of..."*—are the literary equivalent of a door creaking open in the middle of the night. You know something’s wrong, but you can’t look away.

The structure here is a *deliciously* unreliable one. Hazel’s journey is framed as both a personal reckoning and a collective descent into something darker, something that feels less like a whodunit and more like a slow-motion collapse into a cult’s basement. The podcast *The Satellites* is a brilliant narrative device—it’s the modern-day Ouija board, the digital echo chamber where the truth (or whatever passes for it) starts to unravel. But the real genius is in how the synopsis teases the mystery without ever fully committing. Twenty years of grief, cryptic déjà vu, a sister’s disappearance—it’s all there, but the way it’s presented feels like a puzzle missing half its pieces. You can almost hear the author smirking, daring you to fill in the blanks.

And then there’s the romance. Oh, the romance. Silas is the kind of character who would make a *House of Leaves* scholar weep with joy—or at least pause to adjust their glasses in disbelief. The synopsis doesn’t shy away from the tension, but it doesn’t gush either. It’s the quiet, dangerous kind of attraction that feels like it’s happening in the background of a horror movie, the kind that makes you wonder if the real villain isn’t the conspiracy but the way Hazel keeps letting Silas in. It’s the kind of dynamic that could either elevate the book into something like *The Only Good Indians* (if Kerin leans into the mythic dread) or devolve into the kind of toxic romance that makes you want to throw a book at a wall (but let’s be honest, we all have our guilty pleasures).

The thematic weight here is *heavy*—not in the way of a thesaurus, but in the way of a lead balloon. The book seems to be asking: What happens when memory becomes a weapon? When the past isn’t just a ghost but a living, breathing entity that refuses to stay buried? The synopsis hints at something cosmic, something that feels like it’s been lurking in the Midwest for decades, waiting for Hazel to dig too deep. It’s the kind of setup that makes you think of *The Fisherman* by John Langan, where the horror isn’t just in the monsters but in the way they’ve been woven into the fabric of human existence. But Kerin’s voice—if this synopsis is any indication—has a sharper edge, a more urban, *lesser-known* kind of dread.

Yet—and here’s where I pivot like a cat who just saw a laser pointer—there’s something *too* neat about the way the synopsis frames the mystery. The podcast, the dead bodies, the romance—it all feels like it’s been carefully arranged to fit into a genre box. There’s a risk here of the book becoming a *very* good thriller but not quite the *unforgettable* one. The synopsis doesn’t quite sell the *weirdness* of it all, the kind of off-kilter magic that makes *The Only Good Indians* or *Mexican Gothic* feel like they’re from another planet. If Kerin doesn’t lean harder into the surreal, the book might just be another entry in the "chilling mystery" canon—competent, but not *iconic*.

But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the beauty of *How to Disappear Completely* is in its *controlled* ambiguity, its refusal to give you all the answers upfront. Maybe it’s the kind of book that makes you want to sit down with a glass of something strong and a notebook, ready to take notes like you’re solving a cold case. And if that’s the case, then Kerin’s already won half the battle.

Just don’t expect me to wait in line for this one. I’ve got a Monet replica to haggle over.
