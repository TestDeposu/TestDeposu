**Author:** Carrie Ann Ryan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-14 APL

Cousin Greg is currently explaining cryptocurrency to an aunt who thinks the Wi-Fi is sentient, and I am three sips away from throwing this entire ceramic mug across the patio. Let us talk about *Patience*. 

Carrie Ann Ryan is giving the masses another dip into the Ashford Creek universe, leaning heavily on the kind of small-town emotional collateral that makes people buy paperbacks at grocery store checkouts. Teagan and Rush are returning for the requisite second chance at emotional survival. Right person, wrong time. The eternal literary crutch of characters who simply refuse to text each other for a decade. Rush moves from London—because apparently rural towns in these franchises require at least one foreign stamp on a passport to justify a wardrobe upgrade—and crashes right back into Teagan’s orbit. Or maybe I am imagining the wardrobe upgrade. The synopsis is blissfully thin on actual plot mechanics, relying instead on the heavy lifting of capitalized FEELINGS. 

The thematic architecture here rests on the fragile weight of inherited guilt. The synopsis casually mentions the protagonist's father committing "the worst of sins"—which is delightfully vague, leaving us to imagine everything from embezzlement to forgetting to recycle. Small-town ostracization is a tired trope, yet writers keep returning to it because humiliation plays well in romance. You leave town with your tail between your legs, you return years later with a chin-up posture and a secret child, and suddenly the local diner gossip loses its sting. Except, wait. The single dad element actually complicates things nicely. Rush isn’t just carrying his own baggage; he has a dependent. That forces the stakes past mere hormonal nostalgia into actual logistical territory. 

Or maybe it does not. On second thought, let us be entirely honest about what this is—a brand extension built on comfort food tropes. If you have read one small-town single-dad forced-proximity reunion novel by Colleen Hoover or Lucy Score, you have practically ghostwritten this one. But Ryan usually delivers a punchier emotional cadence than her contemporaries, refusing to completely sugarcoat the familial resentment. The hidden hallway encounter is laughably melodramatic, of course. Grown adults do not bump into each other in gothic corridor tropes unless the author needs a physical excuse for pupils to dilate. 

Family secrets will threaten everything, naturally. They always do. If a romance novel character communicated transparently on page thirty, the book would be a short story, and nobody wants a thirty-page novella when they are trying to drown out the sound of a relative debating tax policy over lukewarm potato salad. We are here for the inevitable third-act reconciliation where someone cries in a kitchen and someone else makes a grand gesture involving rain or truck keys. 

Leo just dropped my favorite Parker pen into the decorative koi pond and nobody is currently looking.
