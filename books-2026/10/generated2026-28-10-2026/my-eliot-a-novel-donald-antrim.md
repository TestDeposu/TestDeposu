**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

A quarter-century of silence from Donald Antrim ends with what the industry insists on calling a novel, though *My Eliot* reads more like the literary equivalent of the Costa Concordia sinking in slow motion. 

### The Architecture of Domestic Collapse

Consider the setup: a narrator trapped in his childhood home, haunted by dead siblings and cruel parents, while his ex-lover speeds away with a rival in a sports car. It is the kind of mid-century domestic neurosis we thought we buried with the third wave of postwar American angst. Except Antrim injects hallucinogenic fungi and a talking possum named Tom into the mix. Because nothing says profound psychological excavation quite like a woodland rodent dispensing metaphysical advice. 

### The Weaponization of T.S. Eliot

The alleged catalyst here is a projected book about H.T. Antrim—amateur cobbler, autodidact scholar, and devotee of the high-modernist wasteland architect. Except the narrator never actually writes it. Instead, he wanders through technicolor trees, dodging phantoms and local witches. One might argue this deflection represents a brilliant critique of the impossibility of mourning. Let us reverse course entirely. It is actually just an author stalling for two hundred pages because confronting a dead father without the safety net of magical realism requires a spine few contemporary stylists possess. This narrative dithering feels less like *The Waste Land* and more like Fyre Festival day two—expensive, chaotic, and utterly devoid of functioning plumbing. 

### The Promise of Transcendence

The synopsis leans heavily on the promise of transcendence, pushing past despair into something stranger. Yet we have heard this siren song before. Every aging American male writer eventually retreats to the woods to wrestle with his daddy issues under the influence of local flora. One waits for a literary landscape brave enough to ignore the ghosts entirely. 

My editor is breathing down my neck, but the science fair project takes precedence.
