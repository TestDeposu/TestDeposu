**Author:** Diana Urban  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-09-17 APL

The synopsis for *You're Overthinking This* reads like a self-help manual that’s been backlit by the kind of existential dread you’d expect from a 2010s Tumblr post about "emotional labor" and "gaslighting"—but with the structural rigor of a *Haruki Murakami* novel if Murakami had a side hustle as a dating app therapist. The problem isn’t that the book’s premise is vague—it’s that it’s *too* precise in its clinical precision, which makes it feel less like a transformative experience and more like a spreadsheet for emotional regulation. You can practically hear the author’s voice in your head, whispering, *"But what if you’re overanalyzing this?"*—a question so tired it’s already been answered by the collective unconscious of every woman who’s ever Googled *"why does he text me at 2 AM?"* at 3 AM.

The book’s greatest strength is its refusal to romanticize anxious attachment. It doesn’t frame it as a quirky personality trait or a "charm" to be cultivated—just a pattern, like a bad habit or a misfiring circuit. The synopsis leans heavily on the language of *self-trust*, which is a nice way of saying *"stop waiting for your partner to validate your existence."* But where’s the poetry in this? Where’s the *friction*? The closest it gets to emotional resonance is in its mention of *"shadow-work exercises,"* which sounds like the kind of thing you’d find in a *Susan Cain* manifesto if Susan Cain had a dark room and a Ouija board. The exercises themselves—*"visualization prompts,"* *"secure-response practices"*—feel like they’ve been cobbled together from a mix of *The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck* and a *Wiccan self-help guide*. There’s no room for the messy, the irrational, the *human*—just a series of prompts designed to recalibrate your brain like a faulty thermostat.

And yet—here’s the thing—this is exactly the kind of book that will sell. The market for *"how to not be a disaster in love"* is saturated, but the demand is insatiable. It’s the same reason *Attached* by Amir Levine and Rachel Heller became a phenomenon: people want a blueprint, not a conversation. They want to *fix* themselves, not question why they feel the need to fix themselves in the first place. The synopsis doesn’t so much *analyze* anxious attachment as it *diagnoses* it, which is fine if you’re looking for a quick fix. But if you’re hoping for something with the depth of *Reasons to Stay* by Bill Watterson (the comic strip, not the movie), you’re out of luck. This is *practical*, not profound.

The most interesting part of the synopsis is its insistence that *"healing anxious attachment is not about becoming detached."* That’s a line that could’ve come from a *Brené Brown* podcast, but it’s also the kind of thing that makes you wonder: *If not detachment, then what?* The book seems to want to straddle the line between *"you’re not broken"* and *"here’s how to not be broken,"* which is a tension that feels deliberate but also slightly performative. It’s the self-help equivalent of a yoga studio that promises *"no judgment"* while charging $150 for a class where the instructor *definitely* judges you.

The real question is whether this will feel like a *journal*—or just another book you’ll open once, skim, and then let gather dust beside *The Gifts of Imperfection*. The synopsis suggests it’s interactive, which is either a genius marketing move or a way to monetize the reader’s own emotional labor. Either way, it’s a gamble. If you’re someone who thrives on structure, who likes the idea of *practicing* emotional regulation like you’d practice scales on a guitar, then this might be your thing. But if you’re someone who’s ever felt like their anxiety was less a *condition* and more a *personality trait*—something that gives them a certain edge, a certain *charisma*—then you might find the book’s clinical tone as stifling as a corset made of spreadsheets.

The worst part? The synopsis doesn’t even *mention* the word *"love."* It’s all about *"relationships"* and *"connection,"* which is code for *"how to not drive your partner away."* But love isn’t a skill you *practice*—it’s a verb that’s already happening, whether you’re ready or not. And that’s the thing no self-help book can ever truly address: the fact that you can *know* how to be secure, but you can’t *feel* it until you’re already there.

The indie café down the street replaced their scarred wooden tables with marble last week. I sat at one of the new ones yesterday and watched a woman in a hoodie scroll through her phone, her fingers tapping out messages she didn’t need to send. She looked like she was waiting for something. Or maybe she was just waiting to stop waiting.
