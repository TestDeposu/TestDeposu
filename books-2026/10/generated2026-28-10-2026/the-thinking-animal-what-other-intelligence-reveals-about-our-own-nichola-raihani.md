Hold onto your coffee because the L Train is currently doing that terrifying metal-screeching thing around the 14th Street curve while some guy tries to air-drum directly onto my backpack. People are elbowing past my ribs like they are late for a hostage negotiation, but honestly? Everyone looks so soft and kind today, like melting vanilla ice cream, and I just cannot summon the malice to care about the personal space invasion. I am standing here staring at the blurb for *The Thinking Animal*, trying to figure out if Nichola Raihani’s upcoming 2026 drop is going to slap or if it is just more high-gloss pop-science fluff designed to be read on a commuter train and immediately forgotten before the next platform.

### The Great Ape Showdown

Why do dogs say hello and skip the goodbyes? Frankly, my ex did the exact same thing right before blocking my number, so maybe zoology has some answers my therapists couldn't afford. Raihani is leaning hard into comparative cognition—think Frans de Waal meets a caffeinated TED talk. The synopsis promises a deep dive into why bonobos throw tantrums while chimpanzees just vibe with tactical restraint. And honestly? I am so here for it. We love a petty primate drama. Give me the evolutionary tea. We are constantly obsessed with proving our cognitive exceptionalism—our big, heavy, terrifying brains holding the monopoly on grief and math—yet elephants are out here hosting full-scale vigils while ants are literally doing calculus in the dirt. 

### Epistemological Flexing in the Underground

The prose in this blurb hits with a strange, breezy intellectualism—it is asking big ontological questions without sounding like an insufferable academic paper by someone who needs to touch grass immediately. But let us be real for a hot second. The text relies heavily on classic teaser architecture. It throws out cute animal factoids like tiny breadcrumbs to keep the commuter masses from throwing the hardcover onto the subway tracks. Is it going to radically redefine our place in the cosmos? Unlikely. It reads like the kind of brain-candy that will get optioned for a Netflix docuseries narrated by someone with a very soothing British accent who smells faintly of tweed and expired Earl Grey. If you liked *An Immense World* by Ed Yong, this is clearly aiming for that exact same sweet spot of making you look smart while reading it near strangers who are watching TikTok edits at full volume.

### The Evolutionary Flex

We want so badly to be special. We build rockets and write bad poetry and invent entirely new tax brackets just to prove we are not just fancy hairless apes staring into the abyss. Yet Raihani seems poised to dismantle that exact arrogance by showing that our so-called genius is just a different flavor of evolutionary soup. It is low-key humbling. Or maybe it is just nice to think about chickadees hoarding seeds while I hoard half-read paperbacks in a studio apartment the size of a walk-in closet. The cognitive dissonance of reading about elephant grief while a screeching metal wheel grinds against the rail is peak urban existence anyway. 

I need to wrap this up because my stop is looming and I have to go write an absolutely scathing editorial about the grotesque commercialization of modern art before my soul completely dissolves into the transit system.
