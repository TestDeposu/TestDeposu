**Author:** Ginny Myers Sain  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

We are perpetually doomed to recycle the romantic agony of the meteorologically distressed adolescent. Ginny Myers Sain is apparently attempting to resurrect the suffocating atmosphere of *Dark and Shallow Lies* in a new prairie-gothic endeavor called *These Twisted Hearts*, and one can already smell the ozone and cheap paperback glue from here. Or perhaps it is just the residual scent of the private jet leather. Either way, the publishing industrial complex refuses to let teenagers simply study calculus in peace. No, they must chase tornadoes, lose their first loves named Ezra, and encounter enigmatic strangers named Star who sound less like human beings and more like discarded character concepts from a failed Björk music video. 

### Meteorology as Existential Malice

The premise rests entirely upon a town called Promise—an unbearably ironic moniker if ever there was one—plagued by violent weather because the earth itself possesses a malignant grievance. And why shouldn't it? If I had to endure generations of melodramatic adolescents screaming into the wind about missing boys and cosmic destinies, I too would manufacture red rain and force the tall grass to choke the livestock. The synopsis leans heavily into the pathetic fallacy, dragging innocent fauna and dying farms into a personal domestic dispute disguised as a supernatural reckoning. 

### The Aesthetic of Manufactured Longing

We are told the book is steeped in "storm magic" and "ferocious longing," two phrases that immediately trigger my somatic intolerance for YA marketing jargon. Longing is rarely ferocious; it is usually tedious, sweaty, and smells faintly of burnt toast in the dormitory common room. Yet the publisher insists on invoking *The Raven Boys* and *House of Hollow* as comparative texts, as if stacking names like cordwood will somehow elevate a plot about a girl and a boy wiping a town clean down to its bones. It has all the sociological nuance of the Fyre Festival, minus the cheese sandwiches. 

And then there is Star Holloway. What an exhausting name. One pictures a brooding youth standing dramatically in the wreckage of a mobile home, speaking exclusively in riddles while a wind machine blows his hair with cinematic precision. Jubilee Callahan, our storm-chasing protagonist, is apparently destined to help him unleash an apocalypse upon Oklahoma. Because nothing says healing after a breakup quite like committing localized terraforming and mass structural erasure. It is essentially the plot of *Twister*, filtered through the pretentious lens of an MFA graduate who read one translation of García Márquez and mistook magical realism for an excuse to avoid plotting. 

The synopsis leaves the mechanics of this apocalypse delightfully, maddeningly vague. We get blood-soaked dirt and fleeing armadillos, but precious little logic. One wonders if the red rain is meant to be terrifying or simply annoying for the local dry cleaners. 

My pilot just messaged from the tarmac to inform me that air traffic control has grounded our departure for another hour, which gives me precisely sixty minutes to contemplate why modern fiction insists on turning rural meteorology into a metaphor for adolescent angst.
