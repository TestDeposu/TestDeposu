**Author:** Penelope Bloom  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Unveiled* reads like a love letter to the kind of YA fantasy that mistakes emotional whiplash for depth—think if *The Hunger Games* had a nervous breakdown and tried to pass it off as existential dread. The Empire’s inquisitor? A plot device so tired it’s already been exhumed by *The Lies of Locke Lamora* and buried again. And Raith, that "dark presence" infiltrating their bond—because nothing says romantic tension like a magical ghost in the machine, a trope so overused it’s practically a *House of Leaves* footnote.

The real crime here isn’t the vague menace of "darkness" seeping into their relationship—it’s the way the synopsis leans on the same old power fantasy: the chosen one, the dragon bond, the academy as a pressure cooker. Where’s the subversion? Where’s the moment where Nessa’s survival isn’t just about outsmarting the system but *exposing* it? The Empire’s inquisitor arrives with questions pointing at her—so let’s see her turn the tables, because right now, this feels like *Shadowhunters* if it had a panic attack about its own relevance.

And the friends caught in the crossfire? Classic. The training intensifying? Check. The love interest being unreliable? *Please.* It’s the kind of narrative that would make *The Last of Us*’ Joel side-eye the entire premise. The synopsis sells Nessa as a survivor, but the way it frames her allegiance—"where does her true loyalty lie?"—suggests she’s still just a pawn in a game where the board is rigged. Where’s the moment where she *refuses* to play?

The dragon bond is the real villain here, a magical leash that’s been worn thin by a thousand fanfics. Raith’s betrayal—if it even is—feels like the kind of emotional gut-punch that would make *The Raven Cycle*’s Blue Sargent roll her eyes. The synopsis doesn’t even bother to ask *why* the bond is failing, just that it is. That’s the problem with fantasy that mistakes atmosphere for substance. It’s like watching a *Dark Souls* boss fight where the enemy just stands there screaming, "I’M THE DARKNESS!"—no strategy, no depth, just pure existential dread as a crutch.

The Empire’s inquisitor is the closest thing to a fresh angle, but even that feels like a cop-out. If this is going to be the twist—Nessa’s not the threat, the system is—then the synopsis should’ve given us a hint that the real danger isn’t the dragon or the Empire, but the idea that power is ever truly hers to wield. Instead, it’s all about the *feeling* of danger, the *vibe* of paranoia, the kind of narrative that would make *The First Law*’s Glokta laugh in its face.

And let’s talk about the title, *Unveiled*. Because nothing says "mystery" like a word that’s been overused in YA since *The Selection* tried to pass itself off as *The Handmaid’s Tale*’s cooler cousin. It’s the kind of title that makes you wonder if the book will deliver more than a glossy surface, or if it’ll just be another layer of performative angst.

The synopsis doesn’t tell us enough to judge, but what it *does* tell us is that *Unveiled* is a book that’s more interested in *looking* dangerous than actually being it. It’s the kind of story that would make *The Poppy War*’s Rin’s training montage look like a masterclass in restraint. And if this is the direction Penelope Bloom’s taking the Confluence Academy series, then I can only hope she’s saving her best work for when she stops treating her characters like chess pieces and starts treating them like people.

I’m logging off now to enjoy a cigar on my private terrace, because this synopsis has me more exhausted than a *Stranger Things* season finale that forgot how to end.
