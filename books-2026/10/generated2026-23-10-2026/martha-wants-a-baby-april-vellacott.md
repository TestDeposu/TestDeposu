**Author:** April Vellacott  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

October air tastes like wet concrete and crushed aspirations, which feels entirely appropriate for dissecting April Vellacott's debut, *Martha Wants a Baby*. The publishing machine churns out another cautionary tale wrapped in ombré stained edges and premium case stamps, because apparently capitalism demands that our existential dread come color-coordinated. We are staring down the barrel of a literary landscape oversaturated with women sweating out their societal conditioning on stationary bicycles. Yet, Vellacott's premise cuts right through the corporate wellness grift with the brutal efficiency of a liver shot. 

### The Cult of Pedaling and Perceived Perfection

Look at the architecture of this setup. Velora isn't just a gym; it's a secular temple demanding complete ontological surrender from its acolytes. Martha has checked every single box on the bourgeois spreadsheet—dependable fiancé, pristine real estate, a corner office—only to discover that the punchline is her biological clock ticking louder than the bass-heavy playlist. It is peak aestheticized misery. And honestly? The juxtaposition against Georgie's Instagram-filtered nightmare—where a glowing pregnancy hides a woman barely holding her life together by thread and sheer cortisol—is as painfully predictable as a washed-up heavyweight telegraphing a right hook. 

### Moving the Goalposts of Modern Womanhood

Dolly Alderton and Rufi Thorpe walk so Vellacott can aggressively sprint on a mechanized belt going absolutely nowhere. The central thesis here is exhausting—the relentless, Sisyphean labor of feeling like you are *enough* in a culture that monetizes your self-doubt. Martha's secret shame colliding with Georgie fighting corporate maternity bias feels less like an organic narrative and more like a checklist of contemporary anxiety tropes engineered in a lab. But maybe—just maybe—the sharp wit promised in the blurbs will actually land a punch instead of just shadowboxing with the patriarchy. We have seen this bout before. The fighters change their shorts, the branding gets a shiny new pastel font, but the opponent is always the exact same structural rot. 

### The Verdict on the Aestheticized Grind

Will this limited-print deluxe edition hold up under actual critical scrutiny, or is it destined to be propped up exclusively as bookshelf decor for chronically online bookfluencers? I am betting heavily on the latter. The literary equivalent of a fighter who looks terrifying at the weigh-ins only to gas out in the second round. The prose better bring some actual teeth, because a pretty case stamp cannot save a narrative that refuses to interrogate its own premise with genuine menace. 

And now I need to go rescue my vintage bicycle from a sudden downpour.
