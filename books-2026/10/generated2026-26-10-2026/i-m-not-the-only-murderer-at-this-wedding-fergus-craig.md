October air has this specific, bone-dry chill to it right now, biting right through my flannel while the local teenagers scream obscenely loud outside my window for the third consecutive hour because nobody apparently teaches their kids how to use an indoor voice or respect basic perimeter boundaries anymore, which brings us directly to the absolute farce masquerading as cozy plotting in Fergus Craig’s upcoming *I'm Not the Only Murderer at This Wedding*, a book that expects us to chuckle along with geriatric shenanigans involving a retired serial killer who apparently treats homicide investigations like a Tuesday bridge club in a retirement community called Sheldon Oaks where nobody ever seems to focus on the terrifying fragility of human life or the agonizing, irreversible ways people break each other down over decades of domestic quiet desperation. 

And let us be entirely honest about the exhausting market fatigue of turning senior citizens into adorable amateur sleuths—because Richard Osman already milked that dry-witted nursing home aesthetic to the absolute hilt, and now we are apparently pivoting to literal retired serial killers dodging local law enforcement just in time for a picturesque nuptial ruined by a disgruntled ex-husband named Nigel impaled on a cake-support spike, which is a structural metaphor so aggressively literal it makes my teeth grind down to the pulp while I stare at the ceiling wondering if my six-year-old will ever sleep through the night without waking up in a cold sweat reliving his preschool nightmares. 

Actually, wait. 

Maybe the cake spike is a brilliant metaphor for the structural collapse of modern marriage. 

Or maybe it is just utterly lazy writing. 

Hard pass. 

The premise leans heavily into the tired cozy-mystery trope where murder is treated as a mild inconvenience interrupting the canapés, ignoring the genuine, gut-wrenching devastation of sudden bereavement and the way violent trauma actually fractures a community instead of uniting quirky pensioners in a whimsical battle of wits. 

Yet people will preorder it by the hundreds of thousands while real families struggle to keep their heads above water in a world that feels increasingly hostile and absurd. 

The gentrification of my neighborhood is driving out all the good dive bars.
