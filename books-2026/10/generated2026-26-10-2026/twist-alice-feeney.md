**Author:** Alice Feeney  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

October brings a distinct bite to the air, rusting the maple trees outside the classroom window into brittle shades of orange while my eighth-graders stubbornly refuse to wear their jackets at recess. They think they know everything about the world just because they can scroll through TikTok faster than I can grade an essay on the industrial revolution. 

Enter Alice Feeney and her upcoming exercise in marital paranoia, *Twist*. 

### The Architecture of Duplicity

The premise relies on a flight attendant named Ariella splitting her existence between a wholesome village family and a penthouse bridegroom in London because she believes in multiple soulmates. Because why settle for one domestic disappointment when you can franchise the misery? Meanwhile, a woman named Darcy lives on a quirky street believing in the singular romantic ideal. Naturally, a husband gets murdered. Naturally, nobody knows anyone. 

Give me a break. 

The synopsis practically screams domestic-thriller bingo, ticking off every lazy trope currently rotting the bestseller lists. We have the dual timelines or dual perspectives—though the blurb leaves that delightfully vague—and the requisite dead body to kick off a plot that Freida McFadden claims has more twists than a ride at an amusement park. (Spare me the McFadden pull-quote; I get enough artificial sweetness from the candy wrappers students sneak into my homeroom.) 

I am already exhausted by the sheer stubbornness of this marketing campaign. Everyone is obsessed with writing the same exact psychological mystery about suburban women hiding dark secrets behind white picket fences or London penthouses. Why must every female protagonist either be a liar, a drunk, or a murderer? Can a woman simply enjoy a cup of tea in October without her husband turning out to be a serial killer or a bigamist? I blame Gillian Flynn for spawning a decade of imitators who think cynicism equals depth. Give me Shirley Jackson any day of the week—at least she understood that psychological horror requires actual atmosphere instead of cheap plot-twist gymnastics designed to trick readers who lack basic attention spans. 

### The Geography of Lies

The thematic core hinges on the impossibility of truly knowing another human being. A valid observation, sure. Yet it is delivered with the subtlety of a fire alarm going off during a standardized test. Feeney wants us to gasp at the revelation that people lie. Groundbreaking. Next, she will tell us that water is wet and teenagers hate reading poetry. 

The juxtaposition between Ariella’s dual lives and Darcy’s solitary devotion sets up a binary that feels entirely contrived. Will they collide? Obviously. Will someone prove unreliable? Without a doubt. I am stubbornly refusing to care about the husband who bites the dust, because let us be honest, he is just a cardboard cutout designed to motivate the surviving women into monologues about trust. 

I need to go outside and check the tire pressure on my wife's car before her road trip.
