**Author:** Ronald Lee Robertson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis of *Goliath* reads like a family tree grown into a redwood—impressive in its ambition, but already showing the first signs of rot at the roots. There’s a certain *Tender Is the Flesh* desperation in the way it frames David Jeremiah Lightfoot’s return to Mississippi as both a heroic act and a doomed one, as if the author is trying to reconcile the myth of the self-made man with the actual economics of sharecropping. But where *Tender* at least had the audacity to be grotesque, *Goliath* seems to be hedging its bets, oscillating between the tragic and the sentimental like a character in a bad soap opera who can’t decide whether to weep or scheme.

The narrator, Little Bit, is positioned as this wide-eyed, almost *The Secret Life of Walter Mitty* innocent, but the problem is that innocence is a luxury in a story about land ownership—it’s either a weapon or a liability. The synopsis doesn’t tell us whether Little Bit’s narration will be a shield or a blindfold, but given how quickly the plot spirals into "high-stakes struggle," it’s hard not to imagine him as the kind of narrator who’ll later reveal he was complicit in the family’s downfall, like a *The Godfather* godson who didn’t see the bodies piling up until it was too late. The synopsis hints at this—"quiet moments of sweetness, joy, and transcendent beauty"—but it’s all wrapped in the kind of saccharine language that makes you wonder if the author has ever actually read *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* or *Beloved*. There’s a difference between a family saga and a family *annual*, and this seems to be the latter, with all the emotional depth of a Hallmark card written by a committee.

The thematic stakes are there—land as legacy, inheritance as curse—but the execution feels like it’s borrowing from every trendy Black historical novel without committing to any of them. Where is the *Citizen* edge? The *The Water Dancer* defiance? Instead, we get the kind of "profound meditation" that sounds like it was ghostwritten by a focus group of people who’ve never stepped foot on a Mississippi sharecropper’s plot. The mention of "ancestral curses" is particularly grating—it’s the literary equivalent of a celebrity using "vibes" in a tweet. Curses aren’t just backstory; they’re *drama*, and if this book treats them like a plot device rather than a psychological pressure point, it’s going to feel as weightless as a *Love Is Blind* reunion special.

And then there’s the title itself, *Goliath*—a name that immediately conjures the kind of overblown, biblical symbolism that belongs in a *The Chosen* episode, not a novel. Goliath wasn’t just big; he was *monstrous*, and the Lightfoot family’s struggle with their land feels less like David and the Philistine and more like a family trying to outrun their own bad decisions in a *Succession* heirloom drama. The synopsis doesn’t even hint at the kind of brutal specificity that would make this more than just another "Black family vs. the land" origin story. Where’s the *The Known World* grit? Where’s the *Pachinko* layered history? Instead, we get the vague promise of "destiny and redemption," which is the literary equivalent of a restaurant menu promising "fresh, locally sourced ingredients"—unless you’re already sold on the idea that tragedy is just a fancy word for "emotional payoff."

The real crime here isn’t the story so much as the *lack* of story. The synopsis reads like a press release for a museum exhibit, not a novel. It’s all about the *potential*—the "high-stakes struggle," the "multigenerational epic"—but where’s the *conflict*? Where’s the *choice*? The Lightfoot family’s fate is already written in the stars, like a *Star Wars* prequel where the heroes are doomed from the first scene. And yet, the synopsis insists on framing this as a story of *redemption*, which is either naive or cynical, depending on whether you believe in the power of narrative to change lives or if you think it’s all just a setup for a sequel.

The only thing more predictable than the family’s downfall is the way critics will praise this book for its "richness" and "depth," as if the fact that it’s about Black Americans grappling with land ownership automatically makes it *literature*. But literature isn’t just about themes—it’s about *tension*, and this synopsis doesn’t even hint at the kind of tension that would make readers care. It’s the literary equivalent of a *Big Little Lie* episode where everyone’s feelings are validated but nothing actually happens.

And yet—here’s the thing. If Robertson can deliver on the promise of Little Bit’s narration with the kind of sharp, unflinching voice that *The Hate U Give* had in its protagonist, or if he can turn this into something as brutal as *The Water Is Wide*, then maybe it’ll be worth the hype. But right now, it reads like a book that was written by committee, with all the emotional resonance of a *MasterChef* judge’s critique: "It’s got *potential*, but it needs more *spice*."

Meanwhile, at the gallery, someone finally bought the Monet replica I’d been eyeing. The irony is not lost on me.
