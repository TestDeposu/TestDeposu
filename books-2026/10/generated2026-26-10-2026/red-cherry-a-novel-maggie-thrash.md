**Author:** Maggie Thrash  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis of *Red Cherry* reads like a fever dream stitched together by a writer who’s been too long in the company of *The Parallax View* and *The House of Mirth*—but with the kind of reckless, unhinged energy that makes you wonder if Maggie Thrash has ever actually met a human being who doesn’t live in a spy novel. The premise is so thick with paranoia and adolescent obsession that it borders on the grotesque, yet there’s something undeniably intoxicating about it. It’s the kind of book that would make a 1970s film critic like Pauline Kael nod in approval while simultaneously facepalming at the sheer audacity of its premise.

The opening lines—“Vicky is a teenager searching for meaning in a city that has lost its soul”—are the kind of cliché that should make you roll your eyes, but Thrash has a knack for making even the most overused tropes feel fresh, if only because the execution is so deliberately messy. The idea of two girls, Vicky and Tita, fixating on a mysterious German waitress as a Soviet spy is the kind of plot device that would make *The Stepford Wives* look like a cozy mystery. Yet, the synopsis doesn’t just lean into the absurdity—it *celebrates* it, as if to say, “Why not? The Cold War was absurd, and so are teenagers.” And that’s the thing: the book seems to be playing with the idea that paranoia isn’t just a political tool but a psychological one, a game that can spiral out of control in the most dangerous ways.

But here’s where the synopsis starts to feel like it’s been written by someone who’s never actually read a spy novel. The father’s obsession with home security is treated as a red flag, but it’s also framed as something almost romantic—like a man who’s seen too much and now trusts no one. It’s the kind of character beat that would work in a *le Carré* novel, but the way it’s presented here feels more like a plot device than a fully realized psychological study. And then there’s the love affair—“an earth-shattering love affair that crosses all boundaries”—which is so vague it could mean anything. Is it a forbidden romance? A political betrayal? A teenage crush gone wrong? The synopsis doesn’t say, and that’s the problem. It’s the kind of ambiguity that would make a reader like me—who has spent years dissecting the emotional beats of books like *The Remains of the Day*—wonder if Thrash is trying to be mysterious or just lazy.

And speaking of lazy, I can’t help but think about how *Red Cherry* would fare against something like *The Goldfinch*—a book that also deals with obsession, trauma, and the blurred lines between love and danger, but with the kind of literary precision that makes it feel like a masterclass in character development. *The Goldfinch* doesn’t just *tell* you about its characters’ fears; it *shows* you, through prose so rich and layered that you can’t help but feel the weight of every decision. *Red Cherry*, on the other hand, seems to be more interested in the *idea* of paranoia than the reality of it. It’s the difference between a firework and a sparkler—one lights up the sky, the other just flickers in the dark.

But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe Thrash is going for something more like *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo*, where the paranoia is so thick you can’t tell what’s real and what’s not. And if that’s the case, then the synopsis is doing a decent job of setting up the kind of atmosphere that would make a reader lose sleep over every shadowy figure in the background. The problem is, the synopsis doesn’t give us enough to go on. It’s like watching a movie where the plot is just a series of hints and half-truths—you’re left wondering if the book will deliver on the promise of its own mystery.

And now, because I can’t help myself, I have to talk about how much I hate it when books try to be too clever. I was watching *Blue’s Clues* the other day—yes, *Blue’s Clues*—and Steve was explaining something about a “mystery” in a way that made me want to scream. “What’s the mystery, Steve?” I asked. “It’s a mystery, Sarah,” he said, like that was supposed to make sense. It’s the same way *Red Cherry* seems to be treating its own plot: as if the fact that it’s a spy novel is enough to make it interesting. But a spy novel without real stakes, without real characters, is just a gimmick. And gimmicks don’t cut it anymore.

Still, I can’t deny that there’s something compelling about the idea of a book that’s willing to embrace its own absurdity. If Thrash can make the paranoia feel as real as the love affair, then maybe *Red Cherry* will be the kind of book that lingers in your mind long after you’ve turned the last page. But if it’s just another exercise in style over substance, then it’ll be the kind of book you forget as soon as you put it down.

And now, because I have to, I have to explain why we cannot eat ice cream for dinner. It’s not just that it’s unhealthy—though, sure, that’s part of it—but it’s that ice cream is a crutch. It’s the literary equivalent of a deus ex machina, a quick fix for a problem that deserves something more substantial. And if *Red Cherry* is going to be anything like its synopsis suggests, it’s going to need more than just a scoop of paranoia to hold it together.
