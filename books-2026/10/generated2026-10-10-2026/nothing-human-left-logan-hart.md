The soil out by the south pasture is heavy with wet clay today, clinging to my boots like a stubborn memory as I watch the autumn cabbage leaves curl against the frost. It makes a person soft, almost—willing to forgive the world its sharp edges and bitter weather. Yet Logan Hart’s upcoming *Nothing Human Left* promises no such gentle mercy in its pages. We are dragged straight back into the dirt of a Pennsylvania coal mining town, where the earth itself feels claustrophobic and the human mind decays right alongside the industrial ruin.

### The Weight of Rotten Roots

Consider the psychological landscape sketched in this synopsis. A mother claiming her victims wore "People Faces"—an eerie echo of ecological estrangement, of humanity divorcing itself from its biological skin and pretending to be something monstrously artificial. It reminds me of the utter dreck churned out by corporate publishing houses last year, those bloated climate-dystopian thrillers where every tree has a serial number and characters lecture you about carbon footprints for four hundred pages straight while the plot flatlines into a predictable slog. Give me a Gothic farmhouse and a fractured memory over didactic eco-parables any day of the week. 

### Fractured Mirrors in the Coal Dust

Memory is notoriously unreliable fertilizer. It rots. It shifts. Hart leans hard into the epistemology of doubt—how can we trust the ghost of our own childhood when the landscape that forged it has been hollowed out by industry and trauma? The synopsis positions Grace as a social worker drowning in professional disaster, returning to Fallen Rock to unearth ghosts that should have stayed buried deep in the shale. And honestly, if this book does not lean heavily into the uncanny rot of isolated American backwaters, I will eat my favorite gardening shears. Fans of Paul Tremblay or Catriona Ward will recognize the scent of gaslit recollections immediately. 

The prose will likely hum with that specific brand of rural dread. A predictable slog. No, wait—that fear belongs to the lesser writers of our generation, the ones who rely on cheap jump scares instead of lingering, damp atmosphere. Hart has a razor-sharp premise here, provided the execution avoids the trap of neat, tidy resolutions. Life in the dirt does not tie itself up with a polite little bow. 

I really must go rescue my favorite vintage Waterman fountain pen before my grandson uses it to sketch dinosaurs across the kitchen wallpaper again.
