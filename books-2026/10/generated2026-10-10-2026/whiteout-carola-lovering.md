**Author:** Carola Lovering  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Another cup of lukewarm sludge from the counter. God, this place smells like ozone and fried regret. 

*Whiteout* drops in 2026, and I am already exhausted. Carola Lovering is back to dissect the rotting carcass of the American nuclear family—specifically, the kind where the husband gets a Wikipedia page and the wife gets a graveyard shift of existential self-erasure in Aspen. Let us be entirely clear about the premise. June Lyons has her ambitions shoved down the garbage disposal so her husband Shep can write bestselling trash, until a ski accident ends her pregnancy and wipes her short-term memory clean. 

Groundbreaking. Truly a staggering leap for contemporary psychological thrillers.

### The Gaslighting Industrial Complex

We are expected to salivate over the ensuing amnesiac scavenger hunt. Why was June on skis at nine months pregnant? Where was the golden-boy spouse? Did she walk into a snowbank, or did someone give her a gentle, premeditated shove? Because God forbid a woman in modern fiction have autonomy without a husband lurking in the shadows with a gaslight and a match. 

Or maybe I am projecting. Wait. Let me back up. Actually, scratch that—maybe the amnesia is actually a brilliant metaphor for how women are structurally lobotomized by domesticity, and I am just being a cynical bitch because the diner's neon sign keeps buzzing against my retina. 

Lovering carved out a niche with *Tell Me Lies* by weaponizing toxic codependency, and this looks like the snowy, upscale spiritual successor to that exact brand of emotional terrorism. We are looking at a masterclass in unreliable narration. The past timeline featuring June, her sister Penny, and the genesis of June and Shep's relationship is obviously going to drop breadcrumbs that completely undermine the present-day grief narrative. It is Liane Moriarty meets Gillian Flynn on a black diamond trail. 

### The Aesthetics of Erasure

The thematic machinery here relies entirely on the slippery slope between fact and fiction. The synopsis explicitly tells us it "excavates the thin line between fact and fiction, memory and reality." Oh, really? You don't say. Thanks for spelling out the thesis statement in neon ink. But beneath the eye-rolling genre tropes of the dead-eyed rich couple in a mountain town, there is a genuinely sharp knife slicing through the mythology of sisterhood and motherhood. 

June’s sister Penny is stationed right across town as the eternal confidante. But how much you wanna bet Penny is hiding a massive, plot-twisting secret about that day on the mountain? It is giving major girlboss betrayal energy, and honestly? I am seated. Even if the prose occasionally dips into Lifetime movie territory, Lovering knows how to construct a puzzle box that makes you ignore the glaring structural plot holes until the very last page. 

Except I shouldn't care. I should care about literary fiction with actual teeth, not suburban suspense novels engineered for beach reads and Hulu adaptations. Yet here I am, parsing the synopsis like an FBI profiler. The draft leaking through this poorly insulated window is freezing my fingers and making it nearly impossible to type.
