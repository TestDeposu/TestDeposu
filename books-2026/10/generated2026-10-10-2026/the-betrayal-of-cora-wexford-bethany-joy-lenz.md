Candlelight is entirely overrated. Give me a Tuesday afternoon with the windows thrown open wide, letting in the chaotic hum of traffic and the persistent scent of fresh eucalyptus. I am feeling unusually tender today. Soft, even. Which makes picking up *The Betrayal of Cora Wexford* by Bethany Joy Lenz a dangerous exercise in romantic optimism. 

### The Aesthetics of Deception
Picture 1880s London. Cobblestones slick with fog, gas lamps hissing in the dark, and a solitary woman painting the dead because life left her with precious few alternatives. It is gothic. It is grim. Publishers Weekly is already calling it a dark tale. But look closer at the premise. Cora meets a charming stranger named Miles Vale on her birthday—the exact cinematic cliché that makes every sensible person scream at the page—and suddenly she is falling into trust and surrender. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that. Surrender is a terrible word. Nobody should surrender anything in a partnership, let alone their autonomy to a man who waltzes into their life smelling of cologne and hidden agendas. 

Actually, scratch that entire thought. Maybe surrender is precisely what makes love delicious. Total, unadulterated vulnerability. Or perhaps I am just projecting my afternoon sentimentality onto a text that promises to be a blood-soaked psychological nightmare. 

### Shadows in Whitechapel
The book intertwines Cora’s private unraveling with the terrifying backdrop of the Jack the Ripper murders. She is commissioned to paint ordinary women ignored by a broken system, immortalizing victims of a cruel society while a monster stalks Whitechapel. It is a brilliant thematic collision. Art as a monument to the overlooked. Yet, the synopsis plays it remarkably close to the chest regarding Miles. We are told he dismantles her over time, right there in her own bed, while witnesses watch. 

It smells of gaslighting in a corset. 

Fans of Laura Purcell or Sarah Waters will find plenty to adore here, provided Lenz avoids the trap of melodrama. We do not need histrionics when quiet psychological erosion is so much more effective. Give me the slow-drip poison of a toxic domestic dynamic over a shadowy villain in a cape any day of the week. Real terror wears a familiar face and drinks your morning tea. 

The synopsis leaves us guessing about the mechanics of the betrayal, relying heavily on mood and Victorian atmosphere to carry the weight. Will it hold up across an entire novel? Absolutely. Lenz has a knack for theatricality, and placing a woman at the intersection of public tragedy and private gaslighting is a masterstroke of suspense pacing. 

I just desperately hope Miles turns out to be a ghost, though the universe rarely grants us such merciful supernatural outs. Romance is hard enough without factoring in Victorian serial killers and emotional architects. 

My home office has been invaded by action figures.
