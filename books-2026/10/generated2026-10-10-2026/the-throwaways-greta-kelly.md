**Author:** Greta Kelly  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-20 APL

There is a very specific kind of exhaustion that settles into your bones by three o’clock on a Tuesday afternoon when you have spent the last seven hours listening to thirteen-year-olds explain why a piece of plastic junk they bought on TikTok is worth forty dollars, which is precisely the mood Greta Kelly’s upcoming *The Throwaways* manages to evoke through its sheer reliance on quirky, manufactured chaos. 

And look, I get it. We are supposed to swoon over the modern comedic thriller where the women possess "Olympic-level banter" and an "inexplicable fear of dolls," because apparently, standard psychological depth is too much to ask for when you can just slap a quirky personality trait on a protagonist like a neon bumper sticker. 

Aster Calloway sells a Fabergé music box on eBay just to fund her mother’s run for mayor against a book-banning nemesis—truly, local politics has never sounded so painfully millennial—only to accidentally shatter forty years of espionage secrets because grandmother Iris was apparently a defected KGB operative hiding out with a list of names tucked inside a jewel-encrusted trinket. 

Naturally, this means a creepy doll appears on the porch, because no modern thriller manuscript can survive the editorial process without a mandatory nod to cinematic creepiness, setting off a ridiculous cross-continental hunt involving three dangerous spies from Iris’s colorful past. 

Give me a break. 

Hard pass.

The tropes here are so violently over-extracted that I can practically smell the Diet Coke and burnt coffee in the writers' room where this pitch was cobbled together to appeal directly to the *Finlay Donovan* crowd who demand their cozy crimes served with a heavy side of snarky family dysfunction. 

Yet we are expected to buy into this espionage melodrama while simultaneously choking on the forced whimsy of generational trauma masked as witty comebacks, as if a sharp tongue ever stopped a trained assassin or a heavily funded municipal campaign. 

The synopsis relies entirely on treating international espionage like a quirky family sitcom sketch where the bullets might be real, but the punchlines are definitely tested in focus groups. 

Maybe the actual prose will rescue the premise from falling entirely into the abyss of manufactured quirk, but I am entirely unwilling to hold my breath while waiting for a book that treats Cold War remnants like a cute prop for an eBay auction. 

Right now, the bell is going to ring in precisely four minutes, and the moment it does, I am walking straight to the fridge, grabbing a very cold beer, and staring blankly at the lawn until my brain stops humming with irritation.
