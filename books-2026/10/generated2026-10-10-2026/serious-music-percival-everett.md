**Author:** Percival Everett  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Flicking ash onto the cracked pavement while the muffled bass thumps through the brick wall behind me, I am staring at a plot blurb that reads like a mad-lib designed by a syllabus committee hopped up on speed. Percival Everett is following up *James* with *Serious Music* — because apparently resting is for authors who didn't just spend the last two years rewriting Mark Twain through an ontological meat grinder. And honestly? I am here for the carnage. 

### The Conservatory and the Trenches

Arthur Champion gets booted from Oberlin straight into the Somme mud because some petty academic bigot couldn't handle getting checked. Pure, unadulterated bureaucratic spite. Everett does not do slow burns. He writes like he is trying to outrun a sniper. We are talking about a kinetic velocity that makes most historical fiction look like a corpse decomposing in slow motion. The man pivots a classically trained Black musician from French artillery craters to Harlem bandstands and finally to Woodrow Wilson’s clandestine Oval Office backrooms. Talk about tonal whiplash. It is historical maximalism on a sugar crash. 

### Ownership, Jazz, and the White House

Who actually owns a sound? Who owns the cultural capital extracted from the bleeding edge of Black artistry while the ruling class sips bourbon in the executive mansion? Everett is about to put the entire American mythology in a headlock. Think Colson Whitehead meeting Ishmael Reed with a dash of Thomas Pynchon’s paranoid architecture, but stripped of the masturbatory navel-gazing. This isn't just a book about the 1918 zeitgeist. It is a ruthless deconstruction of how power commodifies genius. The premise demands a level of biting, razor-sharp cynicism that very few writers possess without sounding like a tired Twitter thread. Everett handles institutional racism with the cold, surgical precision of a coroner who has seen it all before and missed his lunch break. 

Get ready for the literati to completely miss the joke while they clutch their pearls over the tonal shifts from the trenches of Europe to the smoke-filled corridors of Washington. They will call it audacious. They will call it a masterclass. I just call it another Tuesday night in the literary industrial complex. If this thing doesn't sweep every major award, I'll eat my own shoes. But right now, the fixer smells like stale menthols and bad decisions, and I have to develop photographs in my makeshift darkroom.
