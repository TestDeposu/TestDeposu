The hearth fire has burned down to a quiet heap of embers, casting long, wavering shadows across the floorboards where my grandchildren usually scatter their toys. They are asleep now. The house holds that heavy, breathing silence I have come to cherish in my old age—the kind of quiet that makes a person think of the old stories, the ones passed down through scarred hands and murmured over kitchen tables long after the lamps went out. 

Katie Ortiz brings us *The Southern Sorority of Superstitious Witches*, a book scheduled for late 2026 that leans heavily into the bruised soil of post-Civil War Alabama. 

### The Weight of Old Dirt and Bitter Roots

Look at the setup. Lucille, Catherine, and Victoria step onto a university campus supposedly built for enlightenment, only to find the gates barred by small-minded men clutching their privileges like precious heirlooms. Yet, the true magic here is not merely the conjuring of Southern folklore into a makeshift sorority. No. The real marvel lies in the desperation. Freedom from a controlling father, an unwanted marriage, a crushing mill-town guilt—these are the heavy anchors dragging these young women toward the dark. 

I said earlier that historical fiction about universities tends to grow stale. Forget I said that. When an author treats academic halls not as ivory towers, but as ancient burial grounds for ambition, the whole landscape shifts. 

Ortiz invokes the spirit of *Witchcraft for Wayward Girls* and Hester Fox. But let us be entirely honest. Comparisons to popular historical gothic books are often lazy marketing tricks designed to lull readers into buying a facsimile of a trend. I refuse to believe this novel is merely a copy of what came before. The inclusion of a darker, institutional force trying to purge every female soul from the campus elevates the stakes far beyond petty campus pranks. 

### The Shadows We Keep

And why shouldn't magic smell of wet pine and iron? History is written by the victors, but folklore is preserved by the survivors. When the academic environment turns hostile, the response must match the venom. 

Perhaps the premise relies a bit too heavily on familiar tropes of institutional exclusion. But does that matter when the prose promises to sink its teeth into the dark underbelly of human nature? 

The younger generation forgets that endurance requires a certain stubborn malice. We spend our lives teaching children to be kind, forgetting that the old tales—the ones whispered in the dark corners of the porch—were about survival. This book understands that blood and folklore are old cousins. 

The cost of orthodontic care for the grandchildren these days is giving me a massive headache.
