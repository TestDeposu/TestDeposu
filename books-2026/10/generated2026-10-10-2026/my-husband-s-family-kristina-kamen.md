Fluorescent humming overhead, a tiny plastic chair digging uncomfortably into my spine, and thirty more minutes until a fourth-grade math teacher explains why my kid forgot to reduce fractions. Perfect conditions to evaluate the psychological weight of *My Husband's Family*. Kristina Kamen clearly understands the lucrative intersection of extreme wealth and violent maritime inconvenience. 

### Sun, Sea, and Sociopathy

Billionaires on boats. A trope older than Aristotle, yet somehow perpetually bankable. We get the standard-issue billionaire checklist: the iron-willed matriarch, the golden child sweating bullets for his inheritance, and an influencer girlfriend documenting the ruin of her own digital brand. But let us be entirely fair here—this is pure, unadulterated airplane literature meant to be consumed while wearing oversized sunglasses indoors. Or, in my current reality, while aggressively nodding at a teacher holding a poorly glued diorama. 

Wait. Let me backtrack on that dismissal. Actually, the inclusion of a jilted ex-fiancée who happens to be a top corporate executive changes the math. That adds a sharp corporate malice to what otherwise might drift into a standard murder mystery. 

This altered my brain chemistry. Or at least, it provides a mildly better distraction than listening to a frantic educator explain homework policies at eight-fifteen at night. 

### The Billionaire Body Count

The synopsis leans heavily on the comparison to *The Guest List* and *The Perfect Couple*. Predictable blurb work. Publishers love a safe lineage. Yet, what makes *My Husband's Family* worth watching upon its 2026 arrival is the sheer claustrophobia of a mega yacht. Trapped in international waters with a floating corpse and a heap of familial resentment? Wonderful. 

And why am I even analyzing a book that hasn't officially hit shelves yet? Because word counts demand sacrifices, and someone decided that late-night exhaustion is the prime demographic for dissecting capitalist satire on the Amalfi Coast. 

Everyone is hiding something behind a veneer of Italian linen. The outsider bride with an enigmatic past. The desperate older brother. A random deckhand who apparently drew the shortest straw in Mediterranean maritime history. Kamen is playing a familiar chord, but the high-stakes corporate succession angle elevates it above the standard beach-read fodder. 

I am going to put on a Coltrane vinyl and forget about this book.
