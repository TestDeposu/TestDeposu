Hold onto your metrocard because somebody shoved their backpack directly into my ribcage while I am trying to parse the promotional copy for *What Makes a Monster*. Emily Cooper is dropping this trainwreck—or masterpiece, who knows, the publishing industrial complex keeps feeding us the exact same cosmic-romance slurry—in 2026. Look. I am literally just writing this godforsaken text to hit a corporate word count so my editor stops emailing me about deadlines while this express train violently rattles my teeth out of my skull. 

### Constellations and Criminals

The pitch decks compare it to *Heavenly Bodies* and *Divine Rivals*. Classic marketing alchemy. Take two moderately successful YA properties, throw them into a blender with some astral magic, and boom. Instant pre-order spike for the TikTok girlies. Yet, what we actually have here is a protagonist named Celestine Savareau rotting in a cosmic slammer because she—oopsie—tried to commit literal omnicide. Relatable! We have all had bad Tuesdays. But now she has to save the very celestial zip code she attempted to vaporize. 

And naturally, she is forced to work with the Nightguard. Because nothing screams workplace synergy quite like being handed a clipboard and a sword by the guys whose mothers you indirectly barbecued. Kivessin hates her guts. Obviously. He wants her dead. But wait—there is a mandatory proximity clause! They are training together, which means the academic institution of enemies-to-lovers is operating at maximum velocity. The synopsis leans heavily on the fact that she is plotting to reunite with her ex-lover and co-conspirator, Aire, while simultaneously developing "undeniable tension" with Kivessin. Poly-adjacent treason? A love triangle fueled by impending geopolitical annihilation? The epistemological implications of celestial policing are truly wild here. 

### The Aesthetics of Apocalypse

Cooper is playing squarely in the sandbox of high-stakes, star-lit romantasy. But let us be entirely real for a second—the synopsis relies on the laziest trope in the modern speculative fiction canon: the reformed baddie whose villainy was merely a misunderstood vibe. The blurb literally spoils its own narrative arc by telling us, "Celestine might not be the monster they thought." Groundbreaking. Truly avant-garde character development in this economy. 

Yet, I am morbidly fascinated. The architectural framework of Velandor—magic ruled by constellations—offers plenty of room for purple prose about starlight and lingering gazes. If Cooper leans into the messy, morally bankrupt psychology of someone who genuinely tried to hit the reset button on reality instead of just giving us a sanitized, girlboss anti-heroine who is actually just misunderstood by the patriarchy, this might actually slap. Or it will be five hundred pages of recycled YA tropes wrapped in foil stamping. Honestly, my money is on a mid-tier BookTok obsession that gets optioned for a streaming service before the ink dries. 

Stop leaning your elbow on my shoulder, sir, we are underground. Also, my upstairs neighbor has decided *right now* is the optimal hour to practice the cello, and the screeching minor scales are utterly annihilating whatever microscopic shred of cognitive focus I had left.
