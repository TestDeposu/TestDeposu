**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

Scotch should not taste like burnt carpet fibers, yet here we are, courtesy of Uncle Richard’s baffling belief that twenty-year-old peat can bridge a generation gap. Let us discuss *Camera Stays On* instead. Tylor Paige clearly wants to dine out on the corpse of meta-horror, dragging us back to the cinematic execution-style dramatics of the Donner Party. 

Five years after an altar-bound humiliation that reads like a lesser episode of *Succession*, our heroine Andi gets lured into a low-budget snuff film disguised as art. Naturally, the ex-fiancé turned auteur is holding the lens. Naturally, the brooding brother is cast as the husband. It is a cinematic love triangle with the structural integrity of a collapsed soufflé. 

### The Celluloid Abattoir

The premise leans heavily into found-footage tropes that died somewhere around the third installment of *V/H/S*. Paige attempts a high-wire act of slasher romance—a genre pairing about as graceful as a rhino in a Valentino boutique. We are promised dangerous desires and deadly stakes on a film set. But what does the synopsis actually give us? A recycled revenge fantasy wrapped in greasepaint and cheap acrylic lighting. 

Everyone is directing the show, according to the jacket copy. How terribly original. Pirandello did it with six characters in search of an author, and Paige apparently thinks adding a chainsaw and some familial sexual tension makes it modern. Whatever floats your boat. 

The psychological interplay between the brothers Delambre and our cynical protagonist reeks of manufactured urgency. Andi was left at the altar, rebranded, and then lured back by a cash-strapped production. This is not high art. This is a Wattpad fever dream that somehow secured an ISBN and a hardback release date. 

### Behind the Lens of Mediocrity

One must wonder about the target demographic for this particular flavor of blood-soaked melodrama. Readers who prefer their prose with the subtlety of a sledgehammer will doubtless rejoice. The rest of us will sip our subpar scotch and pray for a swift execution of the plot. Or better yet, an actual blackout. 

The framing device relies entirely on the tired conceit that the camera never lies, a notion disproven daily by Instagram influencers and true-crime documentarians alike. Paige wants us to believe that the protagonist's journey from victim to final girl is revolutionary. Yet every single plot beat feels telegraphed from the opening line. 

Expect breathless pacing, predictable betrayals, and dialogue that probably sounds profound if your literary diet consists exclusively of airport thrillers. The aesthetic is pure neon-lit exploitation cinema. Think Nicolas Winding Refn, but entirely devoid of visual intelligence.
