**Author:** Jessica Joyce  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Heavy boots on the hardwood upstairs and the relentless ticking of the kitchen clock measuring out another afternoon where the silence feels a bit too loud, a bit too permanent. Staring at these promotional blurbs feels like an exercise in peering through frosted glass, trying to find a pulse underneath the manufactured gloss of contemporary romance publishing—where every single conflict can apparently be solved by forced proximity in a scenic house and a timeline ticking down like a time bomb. Jessica Joyce is returning after *The Ex Vows*, and the formula here is so aggressively familiar it hurts: Summer Beckett, carrying the weight of emotional impermanence like a cheap winter coat, gets thrown into a month-long domestic arrangement with Dylan Parker, a baseball star who also happens to be her brother's best friend and the ghost of a near-kiss past. 

And yet, there is something aching about the premise of feeling like the second choice, the person who stays transient while everyone else builds stone foundations. Or maybe I am just projecting my own desperate need for permanence onto characters who only exist in the damp margins of galley proofs and advance reader copies. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that entirely. 

Actually, the premise is entirely derivative of every single Emily Henry and Ali Hazelwood manuscript currently clogging the bestseller lists, and pretending otherwise is just literary cowardice.

The forced-roommate trope has been beaten into the ground until it is fine powder. 

Summer claims she is temporary, but let us be entirely honest with ourselves—she is going to get her happily ever after by page three-hundred-and-fifty whether her heart is ready for it or not. The thematic core here supposedly hinges on vulnerability and the terrifying audacity of asking for permanence from someone whose life is played out in stadiums and luxury hotels. But can a book really explore the profound terror of emotional transience when it is wrapped up in "steamy benefits" at an aunt's house? 

Perhaps. 

The synopsis leans heavily on the architecture of avoidance, forcing two people who have spent two years dodging the aperture of their own unsaid feelings into a single, claustrophobic fishbowl. Baseball players make notoriously stubborn romance heroes, usually carved from the same block of stoic, protective timber that requires a major third-act misunderstanding to chisel open. Joyce clearly knows her audience—readers who want the sharp sting of angst coated in the sugary shell of forced proximity and domesticity. But I find myself exhausted by the sheer predictability of it all, the way emotional survival is always tied to securing the gaze of the charming man who was standing right there the whole time. 

Bracing for the inevitable emotional payoff.

Or maybe just bracing for the laundry. 

Hiding in the pantry for five minutes of peace.
