**Author:** Sarah Beth Durst  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Staring at the speckled acoustic ceiling tiles while the fluorescent lights hum a dull, accusatory C-sharp, I wonder how anyone expects us to focus on future literature when the linoleum under these miniature plastic chairs smells faintly of damp coats and spilled apple juice, which brings me to Sarah Beth Durst and her upcoming 2026 release, *The Imagitorium*, a novel that arrives on a wave of pure, unadulterated whimsy that feels either like a desperately needed antidote to our modern malaise or a saccharine trap designed to lure us back into the suffocating nostalgia of playground bruised knees and missing mittens, because let us be entirely honest about what a ten-year-old protagonist named Amelia represents in the current marketplace—she is the avatar for every burnt-out adult sitting in a windowless room waiting to hear about long division, a shy daydreamer stepping into a chaos of candy cyclones and butterfly-winged bears named Ruru who give excellent hugs, though I have serious reservations about whether a fluff-based creature can anchor a narrative without drowning the prose in sticky sentimentality reminiscent of a bad Neil Gaiman pastiche crossed with late-period Roald Dahl, and yet, the premise hinges on that classic, spine-chilling moment when childhood ends not with a bang, but with a missing Caretaker and a mechanical collapse of our internal architecture, forcing the quiet kids to pick up the slack while the rest of us just want five minutes of quiet in a parking lot where the rain is finally washing away the yellow leaves.

Shudders.

Consider the sheer exhaustion of inventing a friend just to survive a Tuesday afternoon, a burden Sarah Beth Durst usually handles with sharper teeth in her adult fantasy, though here we find ourselves wading into middle-grade territory where dragons and sea monsters double as metaphors for anxiety, and honestly, if I have to read one more synopsis about a timid protagonist discovering they are unexpectedly brave while dodging candy-related structural hazards, I might just fold this permission slip into a tight little paper airplane and launch it directly at the dry-erase board, because the thematic skeleton here—the privatization or industrialization of imagination itself—deserves a much darker, teeth-gritting treatment than a fluffy bearlike being suggests, leaning closer to the corporate surrealism of Jasper Fforde or the whimsical dread of Norton Juster rather than standard modern children’s lit fare, and yet I am sitting here on a stiff wooden chair, clutching a cold cup of stale coffee from the faculty lounge, desperately hoping the author lets Ruru have a sinister ulterior motive because otherwise, we are just looking at another predictable romp through a magical warehouse of lost whimsy.

Exhaustion.

The sheer existential dread of staring at a blinking cursor is too much.
