**Author:** Allen Eskens  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

My knuckles are currently dyed an aggressive shade of yellow-orange from hacking down twenty pounds of carrots while my aching lower back whispers bitter betrayals about the passage of time—seriously, who decided meal prep was a relaxing Sunday hobby for adults clinging to the fading embers of their youth—and yet I find myself scrolling through marketing copy for Allen Eskens's upcoming *The Loyal Daughter* like a gossiping oracle peering into a sacrificial goat’s liver, wondering if this man can actually pull off a multi-generational trauma narrative without drowning it in the usual procedural cliches. 

Look. The synopsis leans hard on the kind of dramatic irony that usually makes me want to throw my chef's knife across the linoleum—our protagonist Niki is a police detective who hides her familial connection to investigate her own father, because apparently conflict-of-interest laws do not exist in the thrilling world of Minneapolis law enforcement—and yet there is a genuine, gnawing mythological weight lurking beneath the boilerplate crime-fiction gloss. 

We are looking at an ancient ghost story disguised as a modern thriller, the kind of narrative architecture that demands a heavy dose of Homeric hubris. 

Or perhaps Ovidian transformation, though let us be honest, half the time these contemporary suspense novels read less like epic poetry and more like a rushed episode of a network procedural written by someone who skimmed Wikipedia on their lunch break. 

My grandchildren think I am a wizard simply because I know the difference between Cronus and Zeus, but watching a modern crime novelist attempt to translate the horrors of the Secret War in Laos—a devastating, forgotten proxy conflict that shattered hundreds of thousands of Hmong lives—into a cat-and-mouse game about a mysterious figure called "the demon" makes me feel every single one of my seventy-two years, especially when the synopsis plays it up like a standard-issue villain reveal rather than a profound reckoning with historical erasure. 

Give me Cormac McCarthy's bleak atmospheric dread or Louise Erdrich's piercing cultural intimacy any day of the week, because Eskens is walking a razor-thin wire here between exploitative thriller tropes and actual cultural excavation, and I desperately hope he resists the urge to wrap fifty years of geopolitical catastrophe up in a neat, bow-tied courtroom confession by chapter thirty-two. 

Doubtful. 

Because nobody asked, my neighbor Dave has been blowing leaves into my pristine gutters for three straight hours while wearing a sleeveless denim vest that defies all known laws of meteorology and local fashion, and his sheer audacity somehow offends me more than a poorly constructed synopsis. 

Anyway. 

If *The Loyal Daughter* treats the ghosts of the Laotian civil war with the gravity of a clever plot twist, it will sink faster than my half-baked sourdough starter, but if Eskens actually anchors the father-daughter dynamic in the suffocating silence of immigrant survival—well, then maybe I will forgive him the ridiculous trope of a cop investigating her own flesh and blood. 

Actually, I have to go scavenge for a replacement copper spring to repair my broken French press before the caffeine headache achieves sentience.
