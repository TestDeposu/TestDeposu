**Author:** Ross Montgomery  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Shadows of Kensington Gore* is a masterclass in how to sell a mystery without actually telling you anything—because why would you? The real art is in the *suggestion*, the way it drips with the kind of lazy, privileged prose that assumes you’ll fill in the blanks with your own fantasies of Edwardian decadence and detective genius. And yet—here’s the thing—it’s *so* good at being vague that it makes you wonder if the book itself will be a masterpiece of controlled ambiguity or just another case of "oh look, a murder in London, how quaint."

The setting is *London, 1911*—because nothing says "tension" like a city on the brink of rioting while the upper crust pretends everything’s fine. Coronation fever, suffragette unrest, a policeman’s body left like a warning—it’s all there, but the real hook is the *detective duo*. Miss Decima Stockingham, an octogenarian with a "sharp tongue" and a "brilliant, fearless" attitude that sounds suspiciously like a woman who’s spent her life being ignored, paired with Stephen Pike, her "loyal servant" who was *first on the scene*. The dynamic here is *deliciously* subversive—because nothing says "unlikely partnership" like a retired detective and her butler-turned-investigator. It’s the kind of setup that makes you think of *Miss Marple* if she’d been written by *P.G. Wodehouse* after a few too many gin and tonics.

But the *real* gold is in the framing: "this case is not what it seems." That’s the kind of line that makes you sit up straight, because it’s the kind of thing that *should* lead to a twist, but in the hands of a writer who’s actually good, it could just be the setup for something far more interesting—like a mystery that’s less about solving a crime and more about exposing the lies of the people who think they’re above it. The mention of an "invisible network of maids" is *chef’s kiss*—because nothing says "social commentary" like a group of women who are literally invisible to the powers that be, yet hold the key to the truth. It’s the kind of thing that makes you think of *The Secret History* if it had been written by *J.K. Rowling* after she’d had a few too many years of thinking about class.

The synopsis also does something clever with the *tone*—it’s witty, it’s funny, it’s *Downton Abbey* meets *Sherlock*, but it’s also got that underlying edge of something darker, something that’s not quite right. The way it talks about the city "crackling with tension" is *deliciously* vague—is it political? Is it personal? Is it just the kind of tension that comes from living in a world where the rules are changing and no one knows what to do about it? The fact that it doesn’t say is part of the appeal, because it lets you imagine the worst.

But here’s the thing—this is all *so* easy to do wrong. The risk is that the book will be *The Murder at World’s End* but with more maids and a slightly different color scheme. The real test will be whether Montgomery can turn this setup into something that’s *actually* fresh, something that doesn’t just rely on the charm of its characters but also the depth of its ideas. Because let’s be honest—if this is just another cozy mystery with a fancy setting, it’s not going to stand out. But if it’s something more, something that uses the tension of the times to explore the way power works, the way secrets are kept, the way people lie to themselves and to each other—well, then it might just be something special.

And that’s the thing about mysteries—you don’t need to know the answer to enjoy the ride. You just need to know that the writer knows where they’re going. The synopsis here is *so* good at making you want to believe that Montgomery does, that it’s almost enough. Almost.

But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the best mysteries are the ones that make you *think* you know what’s coming, only to pull the rug out from under you at the last second. And if that’s the case, then *The Shadows of Kensington Gore* is already a winner.

Now, if you’ll excuse me, I have to write a fiercely opinionated essay on the death of modern critique—because apparently, that’s what happens when you spend too much time reading synopsises and not enough time actually reading books.
