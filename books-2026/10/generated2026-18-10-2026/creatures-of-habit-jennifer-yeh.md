**Author:** Jennifer Yeh  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Creatures of Habit* is so deliberately vague it borders on the cruel—like handing you a half-eaten sushi roll and calling it a meal. Gina Lee’s life unravels in the most *predictable* way possible: her husband leaves her for a younger woman, her family scatters like confetti at a funeral, and then—oh, the *twist*—a sentient amphibian climbs through her window to save the day. It’s the literary equivalent of a Hallmark movie’s opening montage, where every emotional beat is pre-chewed and served with a side of existential dread you didn’t ask for.

But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t *tell* you anything about the prose. Is this a novel where the amphibian is a metaphor for grief, or is it just a plot device to make Gina feel less alone in her despair? The way it’s written, it could be either, which means Jennifer Yeh either has a *brilliant* restraint or a *terrible* editor. The comparison to *The Correspondent* and *Remarkably Bright Creatures* is telling—both books balance quiet introspection with surreal, almost fairy-tale elements, but Yeh’s debut risks becoming a gimmick if the amphibian isn’t more than a gimmick. What’s the point of a creature that crawls through a window if it doesn’t *bite back*? If it doesn’t force Gina to confront something she’s been avoiding?

The synopsis also does nothing to clarify the tone. Is this a wry, observational comedy about middle age, or a melancholic meditation on loss? The phrasing leans toward the latter—*"a future that looks nothing like the one she imagined"*—but the amphibian’s arrival feels like a last-minute attempt to inject levity. And levity, in the wrong hands, can feel like a slap in the face to the reader who’s just spent 200 pages wallowing in Gina’s emotional wreckage. The synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at whether the amphibian is a guide, a trickster, or just a really bad roommate. It’s all left to the reader’s imagination, which is either a strength or a cop-out depending on how much you trust the author not to underdeliver.

The most frustrating part? The synopsis doesn’t *show* anything. It doesn’t describe Gina’s window, the way the amphibian’s scales catch the light, or the exact moment Gina realizes she’s not just grieving her marriage but the life she thought she’d have. It doesn’t even tell us if the amphibian speaks in riddles or just nods sagely. The comparisons to *Remarkably Bright Creatures* are especially misleading because that book’s magic is in its *specificity*—the way the narrator’s relationship with her cephalopod friend is built on tiny, tender details. Yeh’s synopsis gives us nothing to latch onto, nothing to *feel*. It’s like being handed a menu with only the prices circled in red.

And yet—there’s a kernel of something here. The idea of a single day reshaping a life is *chef’s kiss* if executed right. Think *The Night Circus* meets *Eileen*, but with more existential dread and fewer tents. The amphibian could be a perfect foil to Gina’s stagnation—something wild, unpredictable, forcing her to move. But the synopsis doesn’t *promise* that. It just *hints*, which is either a sign of confidence or a sign of laziness. I’m betting on laziness.

Literally.
