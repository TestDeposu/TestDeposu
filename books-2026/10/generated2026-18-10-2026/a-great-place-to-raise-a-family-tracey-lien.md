**Author:** Tracey Lien  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Give teenagers a ceramic box and they will inevitably turn it into a vault for existential dread, or at least a repository for the kind of catastrophic fibs that keep educators staring blankly into the middle distance during seventh-period study hall while wondering where they went wrong with their life choices. Tracey Lien is stepping back into the ring with *A Great Place to Raise a Family*, and if her previous work is any indication, she has zero interest in writing gentle suburban dramas where everyone sits around politely sipping tea while discussing real estate values; instead, she is dragging us straight into the sticky, sweat-soaked chaos of Sydney in 2005, right as North Cronulla Beach decides to violently implode and remind everyone that multicultural harmony is often just a thin layer of fresh paint over a structural nightmare. But what truly captures the dark comedic irony of domestic life here isn't the macro-level racial friction tearing the neighborhood apart, but rather the localized, bunker-mentality panic of a household whose ransacked home reveals a terrifying truth: the ten-year-old’s stolen trinket box wasn't holding spare change or friendship bracelets, but four distinct sets of psychological baggage contributed by a mother who weaponizes emotional armor, a father who thinks bowing his head will stop the rain, an attention-starved older daughter who would probably set a building on fire just to check her reflection in the flames, and a youngest child stuck acting as the mortar holding the collapsing masonry together. 

Brilliant. 

Except I am already exhausted by the sheer predictability of generational trauma tropes being trotted out like performing seals in every single contemporary family drama that hits the market, because apparently we are utterly incapable of writing about immigration without treating the past like a radioactive ghost ship haunting the basement. Give me a break. We get it, survival makes people paranoid, assimilation makes people desperate, and teenagers are emotional hand grenades waiting for someone to pull the pin, themes that Celeste Ng and Min Jin Lee mapped out with surgical precision years ago while leaving very little room for anyone else to stumble in and reinvent the wheel. Yet Lien insists on shaking the snow globe yet again, forcing us to watch another quartet of thinly veiled character archetypes run around like headless chickens because someone stole a ceramic container, which feels awfully thin for a premise unless the narrative is hiding some heavy-hitting prose behind that suspiciously vague publisher's blurb that tells us practically nothing about how the actual crime resolves. 

Perhaps I am simply too accustomed to dealing with adolescents who confess to much worse crimes—like smuggling contraband energy drinks into homeroom—to find the Luu family's secret-hoarding particularly earth-shattering, or maybe I am just bitter that I have to spend my Friday afternoon wrestling with a stubborn piece of rubber before I can even think about picking up a novel that doesn't treat domestic life like a pressure cooker set to self-destruct. 

Ugh.
