Conventions dictate we must cheer for the bruised underdog who vanquishes the dragon and redeems their broken spirit. Vomiting up tired narratives where ordinary mortals find extraordinary resilience makes for great box office receipts, yet it utterly bankrupts our collective imagination. Sam Rebelein’s upcoming *It's Only a Game* threatens to inject some actual dread into the tired romantic comedy industrial complex. 

Consider the premise. Two bitter exes trapped inside a remote lodge, forced to solve lethal puzzles or perish. 

### The Architecture of Petty Grievances

We are told Julian and Millie need a huge cash prize. They need closure. They need to survive. But why must art always pander to this redemption arc? I prefer my protagonists deeply flawed, petty, and entirely undeserving of a happy ending. Actually, strike that. Maybe what makes these modern genre mashups work is the sheer audacity of weaponizing romantic nostalgia against the participants. Brian McAuley compares it to Jigsaw writing a rom-com. Yet I suspect Rebelein is doing something far meaner—interrogating how capitalistic survival games mimic the transactional nature of modern dating.

### Clues, Corpses, and Conceptual Fatigue

The synopsis leans heavily on the porous boundary between performance and paranormal. The puzzles are impossible. The ghosts are real. Fine. We have seen this gothic blueprint before, from Shirley Jackson's haunted hills to Paul Tremblay's unsettling domestic dread. Yet the genre insists on treating trauma as a puzzle box waiting to be clicked into place. Unlock the trauma, save the relationship, win the cash. 

Or perhaps the lodge refuses to offer closure. Perhaps the ghosts simply mock their pathetic attempts at communication. 

Clate Hill Lodge sounds like every overpriced artist residency I ever foolishly applied to—isolated, freezing, and entirely populated by malevolent entities demanding impossible deliverables. Except here, the stakes are literal blood rather than metaphorical failure. 

Let us hope Rebelein resists the urge to hand us a neat moral conclusion. Give us the messy, unhinged devastation of two people who realize the game is rigged from the start. Give us characters who fail the final clue.
