**Author:** Lucy Tan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-17 APL

Another story about wealthy tech founders and impostors from Queens. Groundbreaking. We are apparently expected to swoon over two young men sharing a summer internship at a fictional corporate monolith called K.O. Systems—because nothing says authentic human drama quite like a Silicon Valley sandbox. Lucy Tan wants us to believe that economic anxiety and billionaire ennui hold equal weight. They do not. 

### The Architecture of Envy

Jack Chen is an expert at studying the rich from afar. What a tired archetype. The kid from Queens faking his credentials while the depressed billionaire's son pines for a life without scrutiny. Cue the mandatory romantic interest to complete the tired triangle. And yet. The synopsis actually hints at something sharper beneath the prestige-drama gloss. The board game Go framing their rivalry—circling each other for years, trading identities and ambitions like captured stones. That metaphor might just save the manuscript from drowning in its own melodrama. Or it might turn into an exhausting exercise in pretentious corporate commentary. 

Let me backtrack. The board game angle is actually a bit heavy-handed. Do we really need a physical game to spell out that ambition is a zero-sum game? Tan is a talented writer—*What We Were Promised* proved she can dissect class mobility without hitting readers over the head with a Go board. But marketing departments love a neat symbol. They love tidy parallels where characters mirror each other across the great divide of wealth. 

### The Privilege Panic Cycle

We are living through a massive saturation of the ultra-rich-youth-misbehaving genre. *Succession* reruns walked so these novels could sprint off a cliff. Every publishing house in New York seems convinced that readers want another 400 pages about people who fly private crying over their daddy issues. Give me working-class grit or give me actual satire. Instead, we get this mid-tier yearning where the poor kid wants the rich kid's trust fund and the rich kid wants the poor kid's supposed freedom from expectation. Spoiler alert: nobody ever actually trades places permanently. 

The prose will undoubtedly be gorgeous. Tan writes sentences that practically hum with quiet devastation. But gorgeous sentences cannot rescue a premise that feels lifted straight from a prestige television pitch meeting. Friendship as competition is an old trick. Patricia Highsmith did it better decades ago without needing a tech internship as a backdrop. 

Perhaps the execution will surprise us. Perhaps the psychological claustrophobia will transcend the glossy tech-bro setting. Or perhaps we will spend three hundred pages watching two guys named Jack engage in very serious conversations while eating overpriced salads in Midtown. Time will tell. Though honestly, my current rent increase is a much more compelling study in economic anxiety than anything happening at K.O. Systems. Speaking of bills, the rising cost of film for my analog camera is a serious concern.
