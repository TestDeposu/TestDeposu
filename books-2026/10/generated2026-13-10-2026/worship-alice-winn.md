**Author:** Alice Winn  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Alice Winn is attempting something mildly terrifying with *Worship*. Crack open the Arthurian legend? Splendid. But let us be entirely honest here: rewriting medieval lore is the literary equivalent of casting Sydney Sweeney as a Byzantine empress. You hold your breath, reach for the Pomerol, and pray it avoids becoming a wretched middle-school theatrical production. 

The synopsis itself reads like a chaotic fever dream sketched on a cocktail napkin at Chiltern Firehouse. Princess Isode is sitting in Ireland with hair like pure gold, casually healing knights who immediately fall into a state of acute, pathetic delirium. Groundbreaking. But then a brooding outsider named Tristram arrives, completely unimpressed by royal matchmaking, choosing instead to thirst exclusively for his idol, Sir Palomides—who is apparently too busy negotiating his personal relationship with the Almighty to notice. The sexual tension here resembles the disastrous Fyre Festival logistics. Everyone is staring at each other with unrequited intensity while Camelot collapses in the background.

### The Geography of Desperation

And yet. The thematic anatomy of Winn’s previous work, *In Memoriam*, suggests she understands the excruciating architecture of obsession. She writes about longing the way Joan Didion writes about grief—coldly, precisely, and with a terrifying eye for self-delusion. 

Wait. Let me correct my previous assessment. Obsession is perhaps too clean a word for what is happening here. This is pure, unadulterated fanaticism masquerading as chivalry. 

Tristram does not want love; he wants beatification from a man who is too busy ghosting him for God. Isode wants geopolitical stability wrapped in the aesthetic of a fairy tale. Palomides is undergoing an existential crisis that would bankrupt a Swiss psychoanalyst. Winn is clearly interrogating the transactional nature of devotion—how every prayer tossed into the medieval void is actually just a quiet demand for validation. Or perhaps she is just indulging in very expensive fan fiction with better prose. 

### The Inevitable Reckoning

Expect clever dialogue that feels entirely too modern for someone wearing chainmail, punctuated by sudden bursts of visceral violence. The voyage Isode and Tristram take will undoubtedly force them to confront their own emotional bankruptcy. But will it transcend the usual tropes of historical whimsy? One genuinely worries it might lean too heavily into quirky-medieval-romance territory—the literary equivalent of a targeted Instagram ad for artisanal sourdough starter. 

The heated seats in my Cayenne are currently stuck on maximum intensity, which is an absolute tragedy.
