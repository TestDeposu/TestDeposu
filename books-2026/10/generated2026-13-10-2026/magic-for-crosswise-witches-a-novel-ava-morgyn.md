**Author:** Ava Morgyn  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The lake doesn’t just hold water—it holds *promises*, the kind that rot from the inside out like a half-burned offering left too long in the mud. And *Magic for Crosswise Witches* knows this better than most, because it’s not just a story about a witch returning home; it’s a story about what happens when the home itself is a liar. The synopsis teases a Southern Gothic with the kind of slow-burn dread that makes you wonder if the real villain isn’t the lake, but the *idea* of the lake—how it’s been mythologized, how it’s been *used*. Truly Thorngood isn’t just fleeing violence; she’s fleeing the weight of a legacy that’s been passed down like a curse wrapped in velvet.

But here’s the thing—this is where the synopsis stumbles. It talks about "dark secrets" and "the Witch in the night," but it doesn’t *show* what those secrets look like. Is this the kind of dark where the water itself is sentient, where the bargains aren’t just deals but *contracts* with teeth? Or is it the kind where the real horror is human—where the Thorngood women have been complicit in their own oppression, feeding the Witch not out of love, but because they’ve been taught that’s the only way to survive? The synopsis leans into the gothic aesthetic—*Practical Magic* and *The Mayfair Witches*—but it doesn’t clarify whether this is going to be the kind of witchy revenge fantasy where the women rise up, or the kind where the lake *wins* in the end, and the Thorngoods are just the last gasp of a dying tradition.

And then there’s the Justice Deacon Clay. Oh, he’s a *piece of work*—corrupt, powerful, the kind of man who makes you wonder if he’s more monster than man. But the synopsis doesn’t tell us *how* he fits into the larger mythos. Is he a descendant of the Witch’s original bargainers? A man who *knows* the truth and is using it as leverage? Or is he just a red herring, a stand-in for the kind of patriarchal violence that’s been plaguing these stories since *The Witches of Eastwick*? The way the synopsis frames him—"the most powerful and corrupt man in her small town"—suggests he’s more than just a personal antagonist. He’s a *symbol*, and symbols are dangerous because they’re easy to misread.

But let’s pivot—because here’s where the synopsis *almost* works. The idea of Truly returning home after thirteen years, only to find that the love she left behind is still there, waiting—it’s *rich*. It’s the kind of emotional core that could make this book feel like a love letter to the slow, simmering tension of *The Love Hypothesis* meets *Midnight Mass*. But then the synopsis drops the ball by making it sound like the Clay family’s vengeance is the *only* thing driving the plot. Where’s the witchcraft? Where’s the *magic*? The synopsis mentions offerings and spirits, but it doesn’t tell us if this is going to be the kind of supernatural story where the magic is *real* and visceral—like the kind of folk horror that makes you flinch at the sound of a door creaking—or if it’s going to be more metaphorical, where the magic is the way the women have been conditioned to see themselves.

And yet—despite all this, there’s something *deliciously* vague about it. The way it hints at the lake’s secrets without ever spelling them out. The way it leaves the reader wondering: *Is the Witch a force of nature, or is she just a projection of the Thorngood women’s guilt?* That’s the kind of ambiguity that could make this book *brilliant*. It’s the kind of thing that makes you think of *The Only Good Indians* by Stephen Graham Jones—where the supernatural isn’t just a plot device, but a *character* in its own right, one that’s as complex and layered as the people who worship it.

But then again, maybe that’s too much to ask. Maybe this is just going to be another witchy revenge tale, where the real magic is in the *drama*—the love triangles, the family feuds, the slow unraveling of a woman who’s been told she’s not good enough. And if that’s the case, then fine. Because even then, it’s better than most of what’s out there. The market’s saturated with witchy romances and gothic fantasies, but *Magic for Crosswise Witches* has the potential to stand out if it leans into the *uncertainty*. If it lets the reader *feel* the weight of the lake, the way the water presses against the bones of the Thorngood women, the way it’s always been there, always watching.
