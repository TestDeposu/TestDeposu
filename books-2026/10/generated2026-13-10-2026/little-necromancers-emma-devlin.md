**Author:** Emma Devlin  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Little Necromancers* arrives like a poorly sealed jar of pickled regret—sweet on the surface, but the longer you stare, the more the brine seeps into your ribs. It’s the kind of premise that smells like a *Knives Out* script written by someone who’s never actually had to clean up after a murder mystery, where the bones of the story are laid out with the precision of a butcher’s block but the soul of a fast-food drive-thru. Mercy Hawkins is the kind of protagonist who exists in the liminal space between hustle and ruin, a psychic debt-slave with a gift that’s as unreliable as a haunted house’s Wi-Fi—her death predictions never pay off, so she’s stuck in this cycle of *almost* being useful, *almost* being free, *almost* anything that doesn’t involve drowning in the Granvilles’ opulent, rotting debt.

The real kicker here is the setup of the Granville family—affluent necromancers who’ve resurrected their own dead, only to then treat their daughter’s death like a bad Tinder date they can’t unmatch. The idea of a murder mystery where the suspects are also the ones who *literally* brought the victim back from the dead is either genius or a desperate attempt to cash in on the current trend of "ghosts as service workers" (see: *The House in the Cerulean Sea*, but with more existential dread and fewer glittery angels). The synopsis leans hard into the *whodunnit* framework, but it’s the *why* that’s missing—the *why* of the ghosts, the *why* of the feud, the *why* of Mercy’s own complicity in this mess. Right now, it reads like a *True Detective* pilot script written by someone who’s only ever watched the first season and assumed the rest would follow logically.

The thematic potential here is *chef’s kiss*—a gothic family drama where the ghosts are the real power players, the ones who’ve been silently toiling for decades, only to be treated like overworked interns by the living. It’s the kind of dynamic that could easily morph into something as rich as *The Turn of the Screw* meets *Succession*, if the author isn’t too busy getting lost in the mechanics of "ghosts who cook and clean." The problem? The synopsis doesn’t do much to distinguish between the ghosts’ motives and the Granvilles’—both sides are framed as equally suspicious, which is either a brilliant setup for a twist or a lazy way to avoid committing to a clear villain. Either way, it feels like the author is playing it safe, which in YA is the literary equivalent of serving lukewarm soup.

Mercy herself is the most interesting character here, but she’s also the most underdeveloped. She’s a psychic debt-slave with a death prediction that never comes true—sound familiar? It’s the same trope as *The Raven Boys*, but with more necromancy and less poetic tragedy. The real question is: what does Mercy *want*? Is she just another poor girl trying to escape her fate, or is she complicit in the Granvilles’ games? The synopsis doesn’t say, and that’s the problem. Without a clear emotional core, she risks becoming just another "chosen one" who’s more interested in the paycheck than the truth.

The biggest red flag? The synopsis doesn’t even *try* to sell the dark magic. It’s all "twisted murder mystery" and "ghostly whodunnit," but where’s the *weirdness*? Where’s the body horror, the psychological unraveling, the kind of magic that makes you question whether the ghosts are even real or just manifestations of the Granvilles’ guilt? Right now, it feels like a *Stranger Things* episode written by someone who’s only ever seen the first season and assumed the rest would be just as easy.

The most damning thing? The synopsis doesn’t *need* to be this vague. It could have been tighter, sharper, more *alive*. Instead, it reads like a grocery list of tropes—*Knives Out* meets *The Inheritance Games*, dark magic, a murder mystery, ghosts who cook and clean—without any sense of how these elements will actually *collide*. It’s the literary equivalent of a TikTok trend that’s been reposted too many times, losing all its original spark.

But here’s the thing—if the author can pull off the balance between the *Knives Out* whodunnit and the gothic family drama, this could be something special. The potential is there, buried under layers of synopsis fluff. The question is whether it’ll ever dig itself out.

My tarot reading this morning predicted an evening of profound annoyance, so I’m not holding my breath.
