**Author:** Peter Swanson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-06 APL

October air presses hard against the single-pane glass of this cramped, radiator-clanking room where my roommate's perpetual indie-rock soundtrack bleeds through the drywall. Peter Swanson is back at it, offering up a slice of seasonal malice with *Do You See What I See?* because apparently, autumnal domestic bliss is just too mainstream to bother writing about. 

### The Architecture of Resentment

Writers shoved together in coastal isolation? Groundbreaking. Yet Swanson usually skips the cozy tropes in favor of a psychological meat grinder. Nicholas Child finds himself exiled to Cape Cod to ghostwrite for Marco Tavares, a man whose success clearly breeds contempt among a rotating cast of bitter hangers-on. We are looking at a locked-room dynamic draped in tinsel. But wait—the synopsis casually drops two corpses before the turkey is even cold, which makes the whole premise feel a bit aggressively contrived. Let me backpedal on that; maybe the contrivance is the entire point. A meta-textual nod to Agatha Christie dipped in misanthropic irony. 

The character lineup reads like an inventory of human toxicity. An alienated wife sleeping down the hall, an unhinged ex-girlfriend dragging along a weaponized husband, and a rival scribe crying intellectual property theft over a bestseller. It is peak literary grievance. Swanson understands how fragile an authorial ego actually is. He weaponizes petty jealousy better than almost anyone currently working the psychological thriller beat, channeling Patricia Highsmith vibes mixed with a heavy dose of Donna Tartt's academic claustrophobia. 

### Yuletide Malice and Other Lies

Holiday cheer is in short supply because joy doesn't sell paperbacks. Bodies dropping before Christmas Day turns the seasonal aesthetic into a sick aesthetic choice, trading stockings hung by the chimney with care for chalk outlines on hardwood floors. The text promises a slow burn, but I am side-eyeing the setup. A setup that screams elaborate gaslighting from the first page. Nicholas is walking into a trap, and we are just here to watch him get ratio'd by the plot twists. It's giving high-concept gaslighting, honestly. A total psyop orchestrated by people with too much square footage and zero emotional regulation. 

Whether Swanson can stick the landing without relying on a ridiculous final-act confession remains to be seen. The plot skeleton provided in the publisher's pitch is skeletal at best. It lacks the deep, breathing dread of *The Kind Worth Killing*, leaning instead into a more theatrical, parlor-game framework. Still, the pedigree holds up. One reads Swanson not for reinvention of the wheel, but for the satisfying crunch when the wheel runs someone over. 

I am going to close the laptop now, grab my oversized thrift-store coat, and go sit on a freezing park bench to aggressively people-watch strangers until the streetlights flicker on.
