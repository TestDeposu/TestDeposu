**Author:** Stephen Wright  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-20 APL

Chewing lukewarm turkey on rye while the fluorescent lights hum a malignant C-flat right above my skull is perhaps not the most dignified backdrop for evaluating a high-seas picaresque epic about 18th-century piracy, yet here we are, staring at *Black Moon* and wondering if Stephen Wright can pull off an Anne Bonny pastiche without drowning in the tired conventions of historical fiction. 

And why do publishers insist on comparing every wild, violent book to George Saunders and Percival Everett like those names are interchangeable magic spells meant to hypnotize us past the checkout counter? 

Look at the trajectory outlined in this synopsis—from Irish orphanages to European battlefields, then stumbling into an aristocratic marriage only for the war-besotted husband to immediately perish in some muddy ditch so our heroine can fall into the arms of a pirate captain. 

It is utterly exhausting. 

Also, why does the guy in the cubicle next to mine insist on chewing his artisanal almonds with his mouth completely open like a cement mixer? 

Every single crunch vibrates straight through the drywall and into my molars, making it practically impossible to concentrate on Amethyst Connell’s supposed native sensitivity to the main chance. 

Anyway. 

The thematic core here clearly hinges on reinvention—turning grief and systemic abandonment into sheer, unadulterated cunning upon the open waves. 

We are promised a brutal, bounteous world that mirrors our own lawless anxieties, which is code for modern cynicism dressed up in tri-corner hats and petticoats stained with gunpowder. 

Will it feel like a genuine work of ventriloquism or just another bloated manuscript begging for a stern editor with a heavy red pen? 

Hard pass. 

Or maybe yes. 

The starred review from *Shelf Awareness* claims it captures our dreams and nightmares, though trade reviews have possessed the critical spine of a wet noodle since the dawn of the internet. 

Still, the sheer velocity of the pitch—ambition, violence, romantic chaos—suggests a kinetic energy that might actually survive the transition from page to brain, assuming the prose avoids the usual historical-novel trap of sounding like a Wikipedia article written by someone who huffed too much formaldehyde. 

I need to debate the merits of absurdism with a stranger in a dive bar.
