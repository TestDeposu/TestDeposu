**Author:** Blair Fell  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-20 APL

The synopsis for *The Two Wills* is so tantalizingly vague it makes me want to scream—like when you’re halfway through a *Bluey* episode and suddenly the plotline about the dog who won’t share his toys gets derailed by a random commercial for organic baby wipes. One minute you’re invested in the emotional core of a child’s tantrum, the next you’re being sold a product you didn’t know you needed. Blair Fell’s book, meanwhile, promises a love story so layered it could double as a Renaissance-era *Shark Tank*—except instead of investors, we’re dealing with a poet who’s either a genius or a fraud, and the stakes are whether or not he’ll finally admit he’s in love with a cross-dressing toilet cleaner.

The synopsis leans *hard* into the "queer love story for fans of *Hamnet* and *The Song of Achilles*" angle, which is either a masterstroke or a desperate bid for algorithmic love. Because let’s be real—if you’re going to compare your book to Maggie O’Farrell and Madeline Miller, you’d better have the emotional gut-punch of a sonnet written by a man who just lost his wife and child, or the mythic weight of a Greek tragedy rewritten for the modern queer canon. The synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at the depth of Willy’s grief over Barnaby Stubb—just that he’s "heartbroken" and "desperate to escape." That’s the literary equivalent of saying a character is "angry" and then expecting us to care. Where’s the *why*? Why does Willy’s heartbreak manifest as a career in theft and impersonation? Is it trauma? Is it defiance? Is it just really good at selling the illusion of femininity to drunk men? The synopsis doesn’t say, and that’s a crime against narrative tension.

Then there’s the poet Will—because of course he’s named Will, like every other Shakespearean stand-in since *The King Must Die*. The synopsis drops hints like breadcrumbs for a cat who’s already eaten the whole loaf: "Why does he look at Willy *that way*?" "Private love sonnets arrive." "Mistress who’s blocking Willy’s quest." It’s all there, but it’s all *implied*, which is either genius or lazy. If Fell pulls this off, it’ll be the kind of book where every line of dialogue feels like a sonnet in disguise, where the subtext is so thick you could spread it on toast. If she doesn’t, we’re left with a love triangle so predictable it could be plotted by a *Teen Choice Awards* algorithm—Will’s wife, Will’s mistress, and Willy, the poor sap who’s just trying to get laid by the greatest writer of his time.

The Marlowe subplot is the cherry on top of this literary sundae, because nothing says "I’m a queer love story" like throwing in a real-life historical figure who was probably bisexual, possibly murdered, and definitely wrote some of the most scandalous poetry of the era. The synopsis doesn’t explain how Marlowe fits in, but I’ll wager it’s either a rival poet who’s also in love with Willy (because why not?) or a mentor figure who’s just here to remind us that art is always a little bit stolen. Either way, it’s a gambit that could either elevate the book into the realm of *The Night Circus* or reduce it to the level of a *Doctor Who* spin-off where every character is named after a famous writer.

The real question is whether Fell can make this work without resorting to the kind of melodrama that makes *Outlander* fans weep into their porridge. The synopsis suggests a story about love that’s "not really lost," which sounds like the kind of romantic nonsense that would make Virginia Woolf roll her eyes in her grave. But if Fell can balance the historical weight of Shakespeare’s sonnets with the raw, messy reality of queer desire in the 16th century, she might just pull off something special. And if she doesn’t? Well, at least we’ll have another book to blame on the fact that people still think "historical romance" is a genre and not just a way to sell the same old stories with different names.

Now, if you’ll excuse me, I have to go patch a hole in the drywall from last night’s indoor football incident. The drywall is holding, but my patience is not.
