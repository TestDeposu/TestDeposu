**Author:** Leslie Jamison  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The Daughters* reads like a literary Ouija board—three women, three eras, one myth’s shadow stretching across millennia. Jamison, who’s spent her career dissecting the way stories twist into monsters (see: *The Empathy Exams*), is clearly playing with the same bones here. But the problem? The synopsis is so broad it could apply to a dozen books. Where’s the *specificity*? Where’s the *bite*? Because right now, it feels like a menu at a buffet where the only thing guaranteed is that someone’s going to eat something sad.

Pasiphaë, the witch queen of Knossos, is the most intriguing hook—because let’s be real, the Minotaur is *gold* for a writer who loves exploring the grotesque and the sacred. But the synopsis doesn’t tell us if Jamison’s going to lean into the myth’s psychological horror (like *The Song of Achilles*’ take on Achilles’ mother) or if she’s just going to serve up a historical romance with a side of labyrinthine metaphor. And Beth in 1944? Suburban discontent is a trope so old it’s practically a fossil, but if Jamison’s going to make it fresh, she’ll need to do more than just dangle an "ancient image" like a cheap horror movie plot device. The real question is: Is this a story about myth as curse, or myth as escape?

Then there’s Jac in 1997, because *of course* there’s a cult. But why Crete? Why *now*? The synopsis doesn’t say. Is this a love letter to the island’s real-life cults (like the infamous *Dionysus* sects) or just another iteration of the "women in the woods" narrative that’s been mined since *The Handmaid’s Tale*? The danger here is that Jamison, who’s so sharp about female rage (*Grimsdyke*), might get lost in the same old well of "dangerous women" tropes unless she digs deeper than "charismatic leader" and "dark secrets."

The real test will be whether Jamison can make these three women feel like more than vessels for myth. Because right now, the synopsis reads like a *Game of Thrones* synopsis—three women, three timelines, one big payoff. But Jamison’s not George R.R. Martin. She’s not writing about dragons or political intrigue. She’s writing about the way myths *stick* to us, the way they fester under our skin like an old scar. If she can make Pasiphaë’s grief feel as visceral as *The Night Circus*’s atmosphere, or Beth’s suburban ennui as suffocating as *The Bell Jar*, then this could be something. But if she just serves up three women reacting to the same old mythic punchline, we’re all just waiting for the next *American Gods* knockoff.

And let’s not forget—Jamison’s nonfiction is *brutal* in its honesty. *The Empathy Exams* isn’t just about medical school; it’s about the way trauma rewrites you. *Make This Your World* isn’t just about travel; it’s about the way desire and despair collide. So if *The Daughters* is going to work, it can’t just be about monsters. It has to be about the monsters *inside* the women who love them.

But here’s the thing—why am I even writing this? Because I have to. Because some part of me thinks this book might actually be good, and I need to pretend I care enough to dissect it. And also because I’m running out of things to do before my shift at the coffee shop, and I’d rather argue about literary tropes than argue with the barista about whether their beans are *actually* fair trade.
