Staring at yet another press release about a Nordic noir involving snow, a dead body, and a brooding detective with a tragic backstory makes me want to throw my espresso cup through the triple-glazed windows of this overpriced flat. Honestly, I agreed to write this preview purely because the wire transfer cleared, and frankly, my landlord lacks a sense of humor about rent. Or wait, let me backtrack on that—actually, the rent check bounced this morning, so I am writing this out of sheer, unadulterated panic while watching a delivery cyclist wipe out on the slush outside. 

### The Frigid Geography of Cliché

Ragnar Jónasson is back with *The Body in the Bath*, the third installment in the Helgi series, because apparently, Iceland’s crime rate in fiction is quadruple that of actual Chicago. According to the marketing copy provided to me by an exhausted publicist who clearly hates life as much as I do, our hero Helgi wants a quiet Christmas. But alas. Snow blankets Reykjavík. The plotting relies on the oldest trick in the lazy writer's handbook: the detective's personal life gets aggressively detonated so he has a motive to care about a case. His girlfriend dies in a car crash driven by his abusive, alcoholic ex—who then walks free, because legal systems in fiction exist solely to torment protagonists. 

### Ghostly Predecessors and Bathtubs

To cope with this staggering level of daytime soap opera melodrama, Helgi pivots. He investigates the mysterious disappearance of his predecessor, Hulda—a retired officer presumed dead, because in these books, nobody ever just retires to a nice villa in Spain to grow tomatoes. Naturally, the lines between past and present blur. Because narrative cohesion is optional when you have a moody atmosphere. Then, the abusive ex turns up lifeless in a bath, and our boy Helgi is immediately the prime suspect. Shocker. I didn't see that coming at all. Give the man a Pulitzer for predictability. Wait, let me take that back—maybe the predictability is a comfort. Like a warm blanket, if the blanket were woven from discarded manuscripts by Jo Nesbø and Gillian Flynn copycats. 

The synopsis leans entirely on tropes we have all digested a thousand times over cheap airport gin. We get the haunted investigator. We get the institutional betrayal where colleagues suddenly turn hostile. We get the corpse in a bathroom. It is a paint-by-numbers exercise in grimdark Scandinavian misery porn. Yet, millions will buy it. They will consume it while sitting under heated blankets, pretending they understand the existential dread of living in a country with universal healthcare and a functioning social safety net. 

God, I need a drink. And to make matters worse, I just checked my notifications and my private flight to London has been delayed by an exact, excruciating hour.
