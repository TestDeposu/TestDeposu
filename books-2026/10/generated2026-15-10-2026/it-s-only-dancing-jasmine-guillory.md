Fluorescent bulbs hum a cruel, low-frequency torture tune while the espresso machine hisses its two-AM death rattle, leaving my brain practically vibrating against the inside of my skull. It is freezing cold outside this wretched little booth—crisp October winds are currently beating the asphalt into submission—yet here I am, staring down Jasmine Guillory’s YA debut, *It's Only Dancing*. 

### The Desert Heat and Aesthetic Plagiarism

Let us be completely honest with ourselves—the premise drips with the kind of derivative nostalgia that makes a caffeinated nocturnal cynic want to throw their keyboard across the laminate floor. A girl named Kitty gets dragged to a desert camp for the summer, hates it, and instantly runs into a brooding dance instructor named Jordan. Groundbreaking. We are basically recycling *Dirty Dancing* for a generation whose attention span has been systematically fried by short-form video algorithms. But wait. Guillory usually writes adult rom-coms about adult professionals eating artisanal cheese and having mature, emotionally regulated conversations. Shifting gears into teenage angst—high school senior year, college apps, sneaking around—feels like a midlife pivot into the YA industrial complex just to secure a shiny badge from the morning-show book club. Do not get me wrong; the YA market is a massive cash-cow right now, brimming with authors like Jenny Han or Nicola Yoon printing money off of teenage yearning. Still, reducing an entire demographic’s emotional landscape to a retread of Patrick Swayze shaking his hips in the Catskills is peak capitalist laziness. 

### The Psychology of Abandoned Pointe Shoes

Yet beneath the eye-rolling setup, the core trauma here actually slaps. Kitty was forced to quit ballet two years prior, abandoning her entire identity because of some unspecified catastrophe. And that is where the book might actually save itself from pure mediocrity. The grief of lost physical mastery—when your body used to speak a language you loved, and suddenly you are struck mute—is deeply real. Guillory throws in hip-hop dance as the forbidden fruit, dragging Kitty out of her academic self-exile and straight into the realm of messy, uninhibited motion. The thematic engine isn't just about a cute boy with magnetic abs. It is about bodily autonomy, teenage rebellion, and the terrifying process of forgiving yourself when a dream violently dies. 

### The Inevitable Verdict

Will it be predictable? Absolutely, fam. You can smell the third-act misunderstanding and the inevitable tearful reconciliation from three chapters away. The synopsis is painfully vague about the actual mechanics of the plot, relying entirely on the weight of the tropes to carry the momentum. But if Guillory leans into the somatic devastation of losing one's artistic passion instead of just leaning on the cheap dopamine hit of a YA romance, it might actually transcend its own marketing. Or maybe I am just projecting my own desperate desire to escape this fluorescent-lit purgatory onto a fictional teenager sweating in a southern California desert. 

I have to jet. I am ridiculously late for a fiercely intellectual philosophical debate at a smoky underground jazz club downtown.
