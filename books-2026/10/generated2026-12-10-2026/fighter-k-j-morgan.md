Counting down the exact hours since a human being last experienced the simple, grounding comfort of a physical touch feels less like a narrative hook and more like a heavy-handed mathematical equation designed to wring tears out of the commuter train crowd. Yet here we are, looking ahead to K.J. Morgan’s upcoming *Fighter*, a sports romance that leans aggressively into the kind of high-stakes isolation that feels entirely at home next to your favorite Mariana Zapata or Chloe Walsh paperbacks. The premise rests entirely on the friction between a white hat hacker turned agoraphobic recluse and an ex-con MMA fighter trying to outrun his own wreckage. It is a loud collision of physical contact and emotional paralysis. 

### Bruised Knuckles and Broken Timers

The thematic weight here trades heavily on the currency of guilt and touch starvation, positioning the local gym not as a place of sweat and leisure, but as a sterile crucible where two broken people can awkwardly rebuild their respective foundations. Savannah Reed’s hyper-specific countdown of fifteen months, four days, and ten hours operates as a ticking clock for her soul, though counting hours since human contact stretches the realm of melodrama just a fraction too far for my taste—wait, actually, no, let me take that back, because grief makes time dilate in absurd, agonizing ways, and maybe that hyper-fixation is precisely the point of her psychological armor. 

Kai Makenzie brings the requisite brooding energy of an undefeated fighter nursing a ruined career, carrying the universal literary burden of an unforgivable mistake that keeps him tethered to his past. The forced proximity trope does heavy lifting here. Opposites attract because they must, driven by a mutual need to cure each other's specific brand of isolation. 

Tough.

The inclusion of shadowy old enemies threatening to drag them both back into the mud feels tacked on, a standard genre obligation to inject artificial adrenaline into a relationship that already has enough internal combustion. Sports romance readers do not strictly require drive-by threats from a criminal underworld to make a romance work; the sheer terror of letting another human being past your defenses is usually enough narrative fuel for a four-hundred-page manuscript. 

Except when it isn't.

One must wonder if the suspense subplot will clutter the emotional beats that actually matter. Watching two guarded souls learn how to occupy the same room without flinching is where the real work happens. If Morgan lets the pacing breathe, the quiet moments of the caretaking dynamic will easily eclipse the external danger.
