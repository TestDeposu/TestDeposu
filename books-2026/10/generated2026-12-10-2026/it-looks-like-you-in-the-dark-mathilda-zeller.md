The damp morning air clings to my wool sleeves while I scrape yesterday’s mud from my spade. Out here, October brings a quiet sort of reckoning—the garden beds are spent, the compost is steaming in the chill, and the horizon turns the bruised color of a plum before noon. 

Mathilda Zeller understands this exact brand of isolation. Her upcoming debut, *It Looks Like You in the Dark*, promises a certain frostbitten dread that modern speculative fiction desperately needs. After her brilliant contribution to *Never Whistle at Night*, the bar is set deliberately high for this release. 

### The Soil and the Sin

Mining corporations always leave a mess. They tear up the permafrost, poison the watershed, and leave the locals to sift through the tailings. Zeller sets her sights directly on this ecological violence in the Kobuk River Valley. Tapeesa’s home village of Chukchi is threatened by a rare earths mine—a modern scar carved into ancient land. But the real genius here lies in the intersection of corporate greed and ancestral terror. 

Or perhaps the supernatural elements are merely a distraction from the human cost. Actually, strike that. The folklore is entirely the point. If Zeller relegates the kushtuka to a mere metaphor for capitalist exploitation, the book will fail. Monsters need to be teeth and bone, not just heavy-handed political commentary about strip-mining. 

Zeller avoids the typical urban-centric gaze by dropping us squarely into the Arctic night. Tapeesa’s struggle—navigating familial doubt while searching for her missing sister Esther—anchors the terror in something fiercely human. Zeller writes with a sharp, grab-you-by-the-throat urgency that feels less like a traditional novel and more like a distress signal. Fans of *Mongrels* or Stephen Graham Jones will find plenty to chew on here. 

### Shadows on the Tundra

The synopsis leans heavily on the weight of community gossip and generational trauma. Tapeesa is labeled as having "bad blood." It is a tired trope in lesser hands, yet Zeller’s previous short fiction suggests she subverts these rural gothic clichés with surgical precision. 

And the horror builds outward from the landscape itself. When the land is commodified, the spirits take notice. Zeller weaponizes the silence of the far north, turning the absence of light into an antagonist. 

Yet, I worry the pacing might stumble once the corporate villains enter the frame. Real estate developers and mining execs often make terribly dull monsters compared to shape-shifting river spirits that mimic the voices of your dead relatives. Let us hope the rare earths mine remains a looming atmospheric threat rather than a tedious boardroom melodrama. 

The frost is thickening on the cabbage stalks outside the window, and I really need to get out there and chop another cord of firewood before the winter chill truly sets in for good.
