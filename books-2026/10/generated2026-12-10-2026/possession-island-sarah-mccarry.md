**Author:** Sarah McCarry  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

The synopsis of *Possession Island* reads like a fever dream written by someone who’s never actually been to a Pacific Northwest island—just watched too many *True Detective* episodes while nursing a cheap whiskey. The premise is so broad it could be the tagline for a *Scream* knockoff directed by a guy who thinks gothic means "lots of fog and a guy in a trench coat." Angela Bell and Sally Raleigh? Really? Because if this were a *Plain Bad Heroines* novel, the characters would have names like "Regina" and "Dorothy," not "Angela" and "Sally"—names that sound like they were plucked from a 1970s soap opera bingo card.

The idea of a vampire novel’s real-life setting is a trope so overworked it should be illegal—*Interview with the Vampire* already did this in 1976, and the only thing that’s changed since then is the quality of the prose. But here’s the kicker: the synopsis doesn’t even *try* to sell the vampire angle. It’s just two women, a murder, and a bungled police investigation—unless, of course, the island itself is the vampire, which would be a *Twilight* plot point if it weren’t so lazy. The synopsis doesn’t say. It doesn’t *have* to, because the whole thing is so vague it could be the backstory for a *Stranger Things* episode where the monsters are just bad Wi-Fi and unpaid rent.

Take notes, everyone. The synopsis is so thin it could be the lining of a *Gucci* handbag—elegant on paper, but you’d be surprised how little it actually holds. The dynamic between Angela and Sally is framed as an "unlikely alliance," which is code for "two women who hate each other but will team up for the plot." This is the same old "enemies-to-lovers" trope, but with the emotional stakes of a *Bachelor* reunion. The only thing more predictable than their eventual reconciliation is the fact that someone will inevitably compare this to *The Handmaid’s Tale*—because why not? It’s not even close, but if you’re desperate for a feminist thriller, you’d be better off reading *The Power* by Naomi Alderman, where the actual stakes involve women *literally* overthrowing the patriarchy instead of just solving a murder.

The mention of Adrian, Sally’s "charismatic twin brother," is a red flag as big as the one on a *Titanic*-themed cruise ship. Charismatic twins in queer gothic thrillers are a dime a dozen—see: *The Song of Achilles*, but with less poetry and more body count. The synopsis doesn’t specify if Adrian is the killer, the victim, or just the guy who’s going to die off-screen to make the sisters team up. It doesn’t matter. The real question is: does this book have the guts to subvert the "twin evil" trope, or is it just going to lean into it like a bad *Dexter* episode?

The island itself is treated like a character, which is fine—until you realize the only thing it’s really doing is providing a backdrop for the women to argue over who’s more traumatized. The Pacific Northwest setting is a gimmick, not a theme. It’s the literary equivalent of a *Netflix* original series that’s set in "a big city" because the writers couldn’t be bothered to think of a real location. The synopsis doesn’t even hint at what makes Possession Island *special*—is it cursed? Is it haunted? Is it just really expensive real estate? The vagueness is so deliberate it’s almost insulting, like a restaurant menu that lists "steak" as an option without specifying if it’s dry-aged, grass-fed, or just a sad piece of meat someone microwaved.

The comparison to *Interview with the Vampire* is the most damning thing here. Anne Rice’s novel isn’t just a vampire story—it’s a *literary* vampire story, with depth, history, and a protagonist who’s actually interesting. This synopsis doesn’t suggest *Possession Island* will have any of that. It’s more like *Let the Right One In* meets *Gossip Girl*, which is a recipe for disappointment unless you’re in the mood for a shallow, plot-driven mess.

And then there’s the whole "queer gothic thriller" tagline, which is either a marketing gimmick or a sign that the author thinks "queer" and "gothic" are interchangeable adjectives. Queer gothic literature deserves better than this—see: *The Only Child* by Megan Hunter, where the gothic isn’t just atmosphere, it’s *psychology*. Here, it feels like an afterthought, like the author threw in "queer" because it’s trendy and "gothic" because it’s easy.

The synopsis doesn’t even *try* to sell the emotional core of the story—just the surface-level conflict. Angela’s grief, Sally’s loyalty, the island’s secrets—none of it is given enough weight to make you care. It’s all just setup for whatever body count the author decides to hit by page 200. The only thing more predictable than the murderer’s identity is the fact that someone will inevitably call this "a feminist revenge thriller," which it isn’t, because revenge thrillers are about *revenge*, not just two women solving a crime while occasionally glaring at each other.

The real crime here isn’t the murder—it’s the synopsis. It’s so barebones it could be the back of a *Harper’s Bazaar* cover that’s been photocopied five times. If this book delivers on its promises, it’ll be a forgettable mess. If it doesn’t, it’ll be a missed opportunity. Either way, it’s not worth the paper it’s printed on.
