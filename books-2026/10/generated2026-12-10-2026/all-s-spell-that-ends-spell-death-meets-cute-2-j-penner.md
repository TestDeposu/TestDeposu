**Author:** J. Penner  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

There is an exhausting fatigue that settles over the soul whenever modern publishing insists that women can only be interesting if they are aggressively mean, hoarding grievances like a dragon on a pile of gold, especially when the synopsis for *All's Spell That Ends Spell* leans so heavily into the tired archetype of the "wickedest witch" who bakes cookies as a subversive threat instead of just admitting that domesticity and genuine affection are not inherently weaknesses to be eradicated by a council of judgmental siblings; give me a protagonist who communicates her boundaries with calm, measured eye contact and I will buy ten copies, but here we are staring down the barrel of another forced proximity farce where a woman has to actively sabotage her cute neighbor's theater career just to prove to her family that she lacks a pulse, which feels less like a subversive feminist fantasy and more like a deeply inefficient use of a Tuesday evening. 

Obviously.

Yet, J. Penner—riding the cozy romantic-comedy wave that currently has us all by the throat, somewhere between the sugar-dusted chaos of *Legends & Lattes* and the petty bickering of Ali Hazelwood’s labs—proposes something infinitely softer beneath the prickly exterior of Poppy Weyward. 

Actually, scratch that. 

The synopsis promises us a villainess who *wants* a happily ever after, which completely undermines the fun of being a villainess in the first place because true wickedness requires commitment to the bit, not folding the second a fae neighbor turns out to be decent at stage blocking and possesses a functional sense of humor. 

The local theater auditions are a brilliant narrative prop—nothing forces emotional vulnerability quite like community improv and questionable costume fittings—and I find myself genuinely rooting for a romance where the armor actually cracks because the other person listens, not because a magic spell misfired like a faulty toaster. 

We deserve stories where falling in love is an act of courage rather than a catastrophic failure of brand management. 

Except the synopsis relies entirely on the cliché that kindness is an accident caused by botched magic instead of a deliberate, radical choice a person makes every single day. 

But who am I to judge? 

The deluxe edition comes with full-color character art and stained edges, which tells me everything I need to know about the target demographic: people who want their bookshelves to double as altar pieces for fictional men who know how to fold a fitted sheet. 

I just hope the prose has enough bite to balance the sugar. 

Oof.
