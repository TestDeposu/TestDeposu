**Author:** Rose Wilding  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-03 APL

God forbid anyone just sit down and watch a movie with the kids for five minutes without someone demanding a snack, a lost shoe, or an explanation for why the sky is blue, which is precisely why I am retreating into the pitch-black fortress of my own mind to dissect Rose Wilding’s forthcoming *Cherry*—a book that promises murder in 1999, though the synopsis reads like a collection of dropped puzzle pieces swept under a rug by a publisher who desperately wants us to guess the picture, featuring a grieving widow dodging her therapist's very sensible advice to wander the streets, a fallen pop star reinventing herself as a domestic help because apparently every nineties celebrity's post-fame trajectory must involve scrubbing baseboards for a middle-class family, and an overwhelmed mother named Ally whose husband unilaterally hires said pop star before bolting off to Indonesia like a coward escaping a sinking ship, leaving three distinct women to spiral into a suffocating vortex of obsession and infatuation that allegedly ends in bloodshed, a setup that feels entirely plucked from the Gillian Flynn school of domestic paranoia mixed with the neon-drenched nostalgia of the turn of the millennium, except I am already exhausted by the sheer predictability of women being pitted against each other in the name of psychological suspense, because must every narrative about female connection immediately devolve into a toxic web of mutual destruction just to keep the commuter train crowd awake? 

Actually, the nineties nostalgia might save it. 

Or perhaps not, considering we live in an era where every single thriller synopsis acts like a cheap magic trick, flashing a dead body in the final sentence just to distract us from the fact that nobody in the publishing industry knows how to write a middle section that doesn't involve domestic surveillance or repressed trauma. I have zero patience for characters wandering around avoiding grief by stalking strangers. Get a hobby. Or at least drink some chamomile tea. Yet there is something undeniably delicious about the 1999 time capsule element—Y2K panic meets repressed queer longing meets the absolute indignity of upper-middle-class childcare arrangements—that reminds me of Sarah Pinborough trying to out-twist herself on a rainy Tuesday. 

Exhausting. 

Give me a break.
