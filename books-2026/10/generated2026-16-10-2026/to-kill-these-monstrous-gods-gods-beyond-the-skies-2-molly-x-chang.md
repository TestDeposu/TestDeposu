Betrayal stings, but watching fictional characters miscommunicate while holding magical death powers is a special kind of agonizing torture that I simply cannot look away from. *To Kill These Monstrous Gods* arrives with the heavy burden of sophomore novel expectations, dangling a deliciously dangerous premise right in front of us. Ruying faces an empire draining her home dry, yet instead of curling into a ball of despair, she plots a high-stakes assassination alongside her childhood crush, Baihu. 

Let us talk about emotional honesty for a single second. Why do fantasy protagonists always hide behind layers of political subterfuge instead of just sitting down and having an honest conversation about their feelings? It reminds me of watching third-tier soap operas where a simple text message could resolve an entire season of romantic tension, or that utterly baffling subplot in the third season of *Bridgerton* where everybody just whispered in corners instead of expressing basic human vulnerability. Real love requires open communication, mutual respect, and zero assassination plots against royal family members at welcoming parties—though I suppose hosting a deadly coup with your former crush beats a traditional dinner date any day of the week. 

### The Aesthetics of Regicide

Power dynamics in speculative fiction are fascinating when they mirror real emotional imbalances. Ruying wields the actual magic of Death—a wonderfully subtle metaphor for how holding onto past resentments can completely kill a budding romance—while navigating the treacherous waters of Roman hierarchy. Cassius arrives as the third prince, holding the ultimate trump card of subjugation or destruction. 

And yet.

My heart always roots for the healthy baseline. I want emotional transparency. I want someone to look their partner in the eye and say, "Hey, let's not murder three princes today, let's instead communicate our boundaries." But where is the fun in emotional maturity? Molly X. Chang clearly leans into the same delicious, knife-to-the-throat tension that made R.F. Kuang and Shelley Parker-Chan household names. We are here for the dramatic yearning. We are here for the shared glances across crowded imperial ballrooms right before someone summons the skeletal remnants of the afterlife. 

Fingers crossed that the hidden forces mentioned in the final lines do not devolve into a messy, rushed love triangle, because life is simply too short for manufactured jealousy tropes when there is an entire colonial regime to overthrow. 

Anyway, I have to go hunt down a decent cup of matcha in this aggressively gentrified neighborhood before my blood sugar plummets entirely.
