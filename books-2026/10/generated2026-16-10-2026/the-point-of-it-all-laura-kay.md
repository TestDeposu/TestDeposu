My bones are vibrating at the frequency of caffeine and fluorescent light, dragging myself through the final grueling hours of a third consecutive night shift while my brain desperately tries to eject the ghost of the five-hundred-page epic I finished at dawn, which completely destroyed my circadian rhythm and left no crumbs. Now I am staring at the promotional copy for *The Point of It All* by Laura Kay, and my sleep-deprived eyes are crossing at yet another promise of a decades-long queer romance featuring the eternal, agonizing tango of two women who simply cannot get their timing right. 

### The Chronology of Compulsion

We get the standard setup. Hotel room. Wedding dress. Cold feet. The specter of the first great love lurking in the margins of a sanctioned, respectable future. It is a premise as old as narrative fiction itself—Sally Rooney and Marian Keyes have practically built entire literary empires on the exquisite agony of missed connections and institutionalized domestic dread. And yet, Laura Kay leans hard into the geography of longing, stretching this emotional rubber band across cities, continents, and the messy detritus of other relationships. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that. I shouldn't be so dismissive of the geographical scope just because I am currently trapped within four sterile cinderblock walls watching the tractor diagnostics blink red on my tablet screen. 

Distance matters in these books. It functions as a third character. The synopsis promises an orbit, which implies gravitational pull, physics, and inevitable collisions. Kay clearly wants to dissect whether growing up means growing apart, a tired thematic inquiry that usually devolves into sentimental pummelling. But the blurb from Tegan Quin offers a flicker of hope—suggesting a narrative that avoids the frictionless straight line of traditional rom-com resolutions in favor of something much messier. 

Brutal.

The structural danger here lies in the sheer vagueness of the synopsis, which relies entirely on atmospheric mood-board phrasing like "private griefs" and "public reinventions" without giving us a single concrete anchor to hold onto while the tractor outside leaks hydraulic fluid into the drainage basin. 

### The Weight of Chronological Baggage

Writing a multi-decade span requires structural discipline that many contemporary romance authors entirely abandon in favor of emotional shorthand. If Holland and Ros are going to circle each other through years of professional highs and private losses, the pacing needs to bleed on the page. It cannot just be a greatest-hits compilation of breakup scenes in various European capitals. 

Still. 

The promise of sharp humor usually saves these projects from drowning in their own earnestness. If Kay balances the crushing weight of queer yearning with actual wit—the kind that cuts through the suffocating nostalgia—this might actually survive the comparison pile-up it is currently careening toward. 

I need to go fix the leaky faucet in the guest bathroom.
