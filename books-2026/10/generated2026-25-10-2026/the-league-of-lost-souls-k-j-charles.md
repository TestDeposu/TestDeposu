**Page Count:** Unknown

My head is pounding against a thrift-store cushion that smells faintly of cheap pinot noir and someone else's regrettable life choices. God, the upholstery is sticky. Right. A book. *The League of Lost Souls* by K.J. Charles. Let us dissect this mess before my hangover fully evolves into a philosophical crisis. 

### A Pact with the Patriarchal Devil

We are promised *Ocean's 11* meets *The Chosen and the Beautiful*, which is precisely the kind of aggressively manicured marketing pitch that makes me want to throw my lukewarm mug of tap water at the wall. But let us look at the meat here. Seven years of consequence catching up to a group of people who thought an occult party game was just harmless patriarchal parlor amusement—sound familiar? It is the eternal curse of women (and their unfortunate associates) paying the blood-tax for men who treat reality like a board game. 

And who holds the leash? Giles Wingate. Wealth, influence, dangerous lackeys, and a supernatural entity doing his bidding because apparently, capitalism just isn't horrific enough on its own. Eleanor Knox stands against him armed with a bad-tempered witch, a failed journalist, and three dysfunctional wishmakers who despise each other. Perfection. Give me messy, unlikable characters or give me death. I am so profoundly exhausted by sanitized heroines who apologize for taking up oxygen. Eleanor has two months to turn unwanted, useless, dangerous wishes into weapons against an empire of toxic masculinity and cosmic debt. Damned if they do, damned if they don't. Mood. Absolute fucking mood.

### The Mechanics of Malice

What interests me—between waves of nausea and staring blankly at a stray glitter fleck on my boot—is how Charles plans to balance the heist mechanics with the gothic dread. Heists require precision. Gothic fiction wallows in rot, decay, and the inescapable weight of the past. Mixing them is risky as hell. If Charles leans too hard into the clever banter of a heist caper, the stakes of literal eternal damnation dissolve into quirky whimsy. And if I have to read one more book where systemic oppression is defeated by a clever quip and a found-family brunch, I am going to launch myself off a fire escape. 

Yet, there is genuine promise here. The synopsis breathes pure, unadulterated spite. These people hate each other, they are cornered, and the clock is ticking down to Halloween. It gives off serious *The Secret History* meets *Gideon the Ninth* energy, wrapped in a glittery, bleeding package. Whether Charles can sustain the tension without devolving into predictable magical fisticuffs remains to be seen. The synopsis is delightfully vague on the actual mechanics of the plot—relying heavily on vibes and impending doom rather than world-building logistics—which usually means the middle act is going to be an absolute clusterfuck. But honestly? Sometimes a clusterfuck is precisely what the literary canon ordered. 

I need to go brew a cup of lapsang souchong and ponder my existential dread while questioning every single life decision that led me to waking up fully clothed under a pile of discarded coats.
