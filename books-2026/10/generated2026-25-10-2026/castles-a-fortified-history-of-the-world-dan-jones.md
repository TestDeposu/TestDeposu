**Author:** Dan Jones  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-03 APL

The synopsis for *Castles* reads like a cross between a museum exhibit description and a *Game of Thrones* prequel—broad enough to appeal to casual readers, dense enough to make historians nod approvingly. Dan Jones, that ever-reliable chronicler of medieval mayhem, has traded swords for stone and mortar, and the result feels less like a departure and more like a thematic pivot. Because really, what’s a castle if not a fortress of ego, a monument to the kind of power that outlasts the people who wield it? The promise here is that Jones will do what he does best—tell a story so vivid you can almost smell the damp stone and the smoke from the torches—while also delivering something that feels like a visual history textbook with the charm of a *National Geographic* special.

But here’s the thing: the synopsis is so deliberately vague it’s almost a feature. "Three thousand years of iconic fortifications"—okay, but which ones? The ones that held out against the odds, or the ones that crumbled under their own weight? The ones that inspired love songs or the ones that inspired *The Lord of the Rings*? Jones has a habit of making the past feel immediate, but when the scope is this broad, the risk is that *Castles* becomes less a narrative and more a catalog. It’s the difference between reading *The Devil’s Highway* and reading a Wikipedia page on border walls—both are informative, but one makes you feel something. And Jones, for all his brilliance, hasn’t quite mastered the art of making dry history *feel* like a story. His earlier work, like *The Plantagenets*, had the pacing of a thriller; this feels more like a guided tour of a theme park where the rides are all "History: The Exhibition."

Yet—and this is the pivot—maybe that’s the point. Maybe Jones isn’t trying to write a thriller; maybe he’s trying to write a *cultural* history, one that treats castles not just as military structures but as symbols. The synopsis mentions "the resilience of people under siege," which is a nice touch—because castles aren’t just about kings and battles, they’re about the people who lived inside them, the ones who barricaded doors, who whispered in the dark, who knew the weight of stone was the only thing standing between them and annihilation. That’s where the real drama lies, not in the grand sieges but in the quiet defiance of the unnamed. If Jones can weave that into the narrative, he might pull off something akin to *The Book of Lost Things*—not in tone, but in the way it makes you see the ordinary as extraordinary.

But then again, Jones has never been one to shy away from the grand. The mention of "the ultimate expression of wealth, status, and supremacy" suggests he’ll lean into the spectacle—Windsor, Krak des Chevaliers, Himeji—all the places where stone and power collide. The problem with that approach is that it risks making *Castles* feel like a travelogue for history buffs, which is to say, a niche product. The real challenge will be making it accessible, turning the dry mechanics of fortification into something that feels alive. Simon Schama did it with *Citizens: A Chronicle of the French*; Jones would need to do the same, which means finding the human stories buried beneath the rubble.

Which is a polite way of saying it’s a mess. Or at least, it has the potential to be. The synopsis is so broad it could easily become a sprawling, uneven affair—think *The Guns of August* meets *A History of the World in 100 Objects*, but with all the depth of a Wikipedia page. The key will be whether Jones can resist the temptation to turn this into a survey course and instead focus on the moments that matter: the betrayals, the sieges, the quiet acts of resistance that make history feel like something more than just a series of dates and battles.

And then there’s the matter of the illustrations. Jones has never been one to rely on visuals, but in a book about castles, they could be the difference between a dry read and an immersive experience. If the synopsis is to be believed, these aren’t just decorative—they’re part of the story. That’s a gamble. Art can elevate or distract; it can make the past feel tangible or just look pretty. The risk is that the illustrations become a gimmick, a way to make the book feel more like a coffee-table tome than a serious work of history.

But let’s be honest: the real test will be whether Jones can make castles *matter* beyond their architectural significance. Can he turn them into something more than just stone and mortar, into symbols of human ambition, fear, and endurance? If he does, this could be his most ambitious work yet. If he doesn’t, it might just be another book about things that used to be important but aren’t anymore.

Now, if I don’t get this done soon, I’ll have to salvage that mid-century chair from the sidewalk before it rains—again. The thing’s been there for weeks, and the city’s not doing anything about it. Which is a polite way of saying the world’s a mess.
