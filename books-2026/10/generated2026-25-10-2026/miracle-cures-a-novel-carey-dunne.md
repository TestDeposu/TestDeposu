We are currently careening headfirst into a cultural moment where the boundary between epistemic rigor and an expensive bottle of snake-oil face dirt has dissolved entirely into a pink, sparkling sludge, which brings us squarely to Carey Dunne’s impending *Miracle Cures*, a novel resting its entire weight on the fragile shoulders of an underpaid fact-checker wading through wellness gibberish, a premise that practically begs to be read with an espresso in one hand and an eyebrow permanently hitched toward the ceiling because we all know exactly how this corporate circus operates—you give a twenty-something aspiring writer a cramped desk, a looming twenty-sixth birthday, employer-sponsored healthcare aspirations that die a quiet death under fluorescent bulbs, and a mandate to verify the un-verifiable claims of a disgraced actress peddling exotic essences for chronic yawning, and what do you get? A satirical slaughterhouse of late-stage capitalism where everyone is desperately looking for an inner glow because the outside world is comprehensively, irredeemably bleak. 

Respectfully, what?

### The Epistemology of Jade Rollers

The central friction point here involves the collapse of truth, a theme beaten to death in contemporary fiction yet somehow revitalized here through sheer, blistering exhaustion. We are promised an exploration of belief systems crumbling under their own weight, reminiscent of Melissa Broder’s damp, anxious mysticism or Jen Beagin’s biting social observations, though one wonders if the book leans too heavily into the obvious punchlines about Gwyneth-adjacent archetypes without offering a genuinely sharp ideological knife. 

Brutal.

Because let us be entirely serious for a single microsecond: verifying that a self-proclaimed animal psychic is committing tax fraud or that a crystal-infused eye cream cures existential dread is not a narrative arc; it is Tuesday in media. 

### The Illusion of the Cure

Yet, the synopsis hints at a personal reckoning when our protagonist suffers a health scare and falls down the very rabbit hole she is paid to excavate, a delicious little hypocritical pivot that saves the manuscript from becoming a dry tract on journalistic ethics. We crave the lie. We demand the magic pill because reality smells faintly of ozone and damp basement carpet in New Jersey. Dunne appears poised to dissect the neurological architecture of gullibility—that specific, terrifying vulnerability of wanting to believe in youth and vitality so fiercely that you are willing to swallow crushed pearls and expensive dust. 

Truth is overrated. 

Give us the fiction.

The persistent draft coming from your poorly insulated window is unbearable.
