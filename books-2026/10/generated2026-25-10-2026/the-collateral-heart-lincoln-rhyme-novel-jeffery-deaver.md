**Author:** Jeffery Deaver  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-17 APL

The air smells like cinnamon and damp leaves, the kind of scent that makes you want to sit down with a book and a cup of tea that’s been sitting too long. But instead, we’re here—kids screaming, parents yelling, the kind of chaos that makes you wonder why anyone ever thought pumpkin patches were a good idea. Meanwhile, *The Collateral Heart* arrives like a well-timed threat, a Lincoln Rhyme novel that promises to burn through the autumnal haze of suburban nostalgia with the same precision as its arsonist protagonist.

Deaver’s synopsis is the kind of tease that makes you want to claw your way through the synopsis itself, like a detective sifting through ash for a clue. The premise—arson, clues, a history podcaster, a Long Island detective—feels like a love letter to the kind of procedural that thrives on controlled chaos. But here’s the thing: the synopsis is so broad, it could be describing a dozen different books. Where’s the edge? Where’s the *something* that makes this stand out beyond the usual Rhyme-and-Sachs formula? The way it’s written, it could be a generic thriller about a serial arsonist, or it could be a deep dive into the psychology of destruction—something closer to *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo* meets *True Detective*’s first season, if the first season had more fire and less existential dread.

And yet—there’s a certain *vibe* here, a sense that Deaver is playing with fire, literally and metaphorically. The mention of a history podcaster is either a clever nod to the idea that knowledge is power—or a lazy way to introduce a red herring. It’s the kind of detail that makes you wonder if the book will lean into the intellectual side of the mystery, or if it’ll just be another case where the tech outshines the human element. Because let’s be honest, Rhyme’s genius is his ability to see what others miss, but if this book relies too heavily on his forensic brilliance, it risks feeling like a rehash of *The Bone Collector*’s early glory days.

But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe Deaver is doubling down on the formula because, let’s face it, the world doesn’t need another original thriller—it needs another *Lincoln Rhyme* book, the kind that makes you forget about the pumpkin patch and the screaming kids and just want to dive into the chaos. And if that’s the case, then fine. But don’t expect anything too clever. Don’t expect the kind of thematic depth that would make this more than just another entry in a well-worn series.

The synopsis also hints at a potential collision between cases, which is either a smart way to build tension or a lazy way to force a plot twist. It’s the kind of setup that could work if Deaver leans into the idea that the arsonist’s motives are more complex than just destruction for its own sake. But given the vagueness, it’s hard to tell. Is this a book about vengeance? Is it about obsession? Or is it just another case of a killer who enjoys watching things burn?

And speaking of burning—why does every thriller these days have to be about fire? It’s like the publishing industry has a collective anxiety about not being able to think of anything else. First it was *The Girl on the Train*, then *Sharp Objects*, now *The Collateral Heart*. It’s exhausting. But hey, at least it’s consistent.

But let’s cut through the noise. The real question is: will this book feel fresh, or will it just be another step in the Rhyme-Sachs saga? If Deaver can make the arsonist’s motives feel personal, if he can make the history podcaster more than just a plot device, then maybe this will be worth the wait. But if it’s just another case of the duo outsmarting a killer who’s more about spectacle than substance, then it’ll just be another book that gets lost in the autumnal shuffle.

And honestly, that’s the problem with these kinds of books. They’re too predictable. They’re too safe. They’re the kind of thing you read because you know what you’re getting, not because you’re actually excited about it. But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the best thrillers aren’t the ones that surprise you—they’re the ones that make you feel like you’re right there in the chaos, even if you’ve seen it all before.
