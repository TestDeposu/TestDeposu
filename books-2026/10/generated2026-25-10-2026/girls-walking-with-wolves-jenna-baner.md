The fluorescent tubes overhead are humming like an ancient cicada, casting a sterile, sickly pallor over my half-eaten egg salad sandwich, and honestly, the grease on my fingers makes holding a stylus feel like a sick joke while I stare at the early promotional materials for *Girls Walking with Wolves*. We spend our entire lives trying to recapture the raw, unfiltered terror of the old stories—the ones our grandmothers whispered over cooling stew while the wind rattled the windowpanes—only to watch modern publishing churn out yet another high-concept fairy tale mashup that relies entirely on a recycled hook about a blind protagonist and a misunderstood forest dweller. Jenna Baner is scheduled to drop this in late 2026, and I find myself simultaneously dreading the predictable beats and desperately hoping it captures that old, dark magic before the marketing department flattens it into cardboard.

### The Geography of Blind Faith

Arden Hood walks into the woods blind, which immediately signals a sensory-heavy narrative framework, though one worries whether the author will actually do the hard work of rendering a sensory landscape or simply lean on cheap literary crutches like heavy breathing and rustling leaves. And the Queen’s hunter! A nameless cipher stalking through the underbrush because he was sent there for a reason, which is perhaps the laziest inciting incident since the Chosen One tripped over a glowing sword in a ditch. We have seen this exact archetype stalk across the pages of Naomi Novik and Katherine Arden until our boots are caked in the metaphorical mud of a thousand derivative retellings. Yet the dynamic between Arden and Myra—the solitary, benevolent witch hiding from pitchfork-wielding villagers—promises a tender subversion of the monster-in-the-woods trope, provided the dialogue doesn't sound like it was workshopped by a committee of panicked interns in a glass-walled conference room. Wait. Scratch that. Maybe the hunter isn't a cipher at all; maybe the real tension stems from the quiet domesticity of Arden's daily life, making the impending violence feel entirely secondary to the grief of losing an ailing grandmother.

Boring.
