Everything is rotting. The gallery walls are slowly buckling under the humid October damp, the provenance of my entire collection feels like a fever dream of stolen paper and bankrupt aristocrats, and now we are expected to fawn over *By Definition: The Surprising History of the English Dictionary* by Peter Sokolowski. As if lexicography could possibly save us from the void. 

### The Semiotics of Despair

Peter Sokolowski, a veteran of Merriam-Webster—because nothing screams avant-garde rebellion quite like corporate lexicography—wants us to care about the shifting sands of human utterance. The synopsis breathlessly informs us that dictionaries represent both stability and change. Groundbreaking. Riveting stuff. I suppose we should throw a gala. 

English is a dumpster fire of stolen Latin and Norman bureaucracy, apparently. The promotional blurb labors over the historical class system that kept everyday words out of the official record until the mid-18th century. Groundbreaking historical analysis, really. Samuel Johnson and Noah Webster make their predictable cameos, like two ghosts haunting a dying publishing house. 

### The Lexical Gutter

Let us address the profound irony of interrogating whether *irregardless* is a word while the ceiling above my priceless Rothkos actively threatens to collapse into a pile of wet drywall. This text—or whatever superficial chronicle of semantic gatekeeping Sokolowski has managed to compile—feels deeply unserious. It is giving Boomer LinkedIn engagement bait, but make it philological. 

People are losing their minds over *literally* having more than one definition, while I am losing my mind trying to figure out why I spent seven figures on a canvas that now smells faintly of rotting squash and existential doom. We are living through a cultural apocalypse, yet we are supposed to delight in the whimsical mysteries of online references. Make it make sense. Or don't. Who gives AF? 

### The Inevitable Decay

The synopsis promises fun examples and a peek into the mysterious world of dictionary creation. Sign me up for the slumber party. One anticipates a breezy, lightweight volume designed exclusively for people who buy decorative throw pillows that say "Read More Books." Fans of Mary Norris or Ammon Shea will undoubtedly lap this up like pathetic little pigeons peck at stale breadcrumbs in a filthy piazza. 

This book will likely sit proudly on the mahogany coffee tables of private equity executives who haven't read an unillustrated sentence since the Clinton administration. It avoids genuine linguistic philosophy in favor of trivia-night fodder. I would rather chew glass than read another breathless anecdote about the *Oxford English Dictionary*. 

Oh, God. The rain is starting. The sky just opened up over the avenue, and I completely forgot that the Eames-era molded plywood lounge chair I discarded next to the recycling bins is currently sitting out on the wet concrete. If the upholstery gets ruined before the restorer arrives, I am going to jump straight into the Hudson.
