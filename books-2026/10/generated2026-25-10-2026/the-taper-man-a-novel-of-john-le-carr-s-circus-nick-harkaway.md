**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-17 APL

Three hundred pages of George Smiley traipsing through sunshine while the literary world gasps in orchestrated awe. Nick Harkaway wants us to believe that moving the Circus from the perpetual drizzle of London to the manicured paranoia of 1965 Los Angeles somehow reinvents the wheel. But let us be entirely serious for a single moment. It gives off strong *Fyre Festival* energy—all massive branding, aggressive palm trees, and an underlying panic that nobody actually checked if the water was running. 

### The Geography of Bureaucracy

The premise relies on crossing the Atlantic as if it were a bold act of stylistic transgression. Yet the mechanics of espionage do not care about palm trees. Smiley in Los Angeles dealing with racial fractures and American patriotism sounds less like classic John le Carré and more like an episode of *Mad Men* written by an exhausted civil servant on a strict deadline. The synopsis leans hard on the cold, sharp edges of the Cold War. But how many times can we watch a tired old man chase a phantom named Karla across a sunburned asphalt grid without checking our watches? 

### The Narrative Drag

Roy Bland freezing in Leningrad provides the necessary counterweight. At least someone gets to suffer in the cold. Bland offers the vital claustrophobia that made *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy* an absolute masterclass in institutional rot. But the transatlantic split threatens to dilute the tension. A spy novel lives and dies by its pacing. If the narrative spends half its breath cataloging Los Angeles internal divisions to appease modern sensibilities, the momentum stalls completely. We are trading the grey, smoke-filled basements of Cambridge Circus for a California noir pastiche that feels fundamentally unnecessary. 

### The Verdict on the Stakes

The synopsis tells us the world lives on a knife edge. Naturally. It always does in these books. The threat of a Taper Man exposing a mole in Moscow sounds deliciously opaque—perhaps too opaque. When a blurb has to rely entirely on moody abstractions like "the secrets of the past," expect a structural mess of flashbacks and lingering melancholy that ultimately goes nowhere. We are paying top dollar for literary pedigree, not an exercise in historical mood-setting. If Harkaway fails to anchor the California sequences in the same grinding, joyless procedural dread that defined the original trilogy, this entire enterprise collapses under the weight of its own ambition. 

Give me the damp masonry of Paddington over the Sunset Boulevard sprawl any day of the week. Now I have to go deal with my brother who is threatening to throw our only television out of a second-story window because he refuses to share the remote.
