**Author:** Jedidiah Jenkins  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

My ramen water is currently threatening to boil over onto my burning hotplate because the landlord refuses to fix the stove dial, yet I am supposed to care about a guy who voluntarily hangs out in a luxury rustic shed for sixty days. Jedidiah Jenkins is back at it with *Go Quiet on the Mountain*, which is apparently hitting shelves in 2026. Because apparently, the greatest crisis facing humanity right now is that rich travel memoirists spend too much time staring at glowing rectangles in Los Angeles before retreating to Colorado to cry about their relationships. 

Let us look at the facts. Or rather, the complete lack thereof. The synopsis tells us absolutely nothing about the actual mechanics of this so-called digital detox—how does he pay his rent? Does he have a ghostwriter? No, instead we get sixty days of pure emotional bourgeoisie navel-gazing. And yet, I am strangely fascinated by the structural mechanics of his psychological collapse. 

Actually, wait. I take that back. The psychology presented here is not fascinating at all. It is painfully derivative. 

### The Romance of the Outhouse

We have reached peak late-stage capitalism when a grown man escaping to a shed without plumbing is marketed as a revolutionary act of courage. Thoreau did it at Walden, but at least he brought a notebook and didn't leave a boyfriend crying in Silver Lake. Jenkins asks why he can't focus for ten seconds and why his relationships end in disaster. Here is a wild hypothesis—maybe it is because you spend your entire existence curating your trauma for book advances. Give me a break. Or better yet, give me an empirical study on dopamine receptor fatigue that actually cites peer-reviewed neuroscience instead of poetic waxing about ice-cold rivers. 

The supporting cast sounds like it was generated by a bad fiction algorithm. There is a mountain innkeeper holding town secrets—classic trope—and a guy named Chicken Bill who wears a poultry costume every single day. I am not making this up. It is right there in the official text. Is Chicken Bill a metaphor for our collective societal absurdity? Or is he just a local eccentric who regrets signing a media release form? We will probably never know because the book focuses entirely on Jenkins writing dramatic letters to a boyfriend he expects to dump him. 

### The Sociology of the Sabbatical

And yet, this fits squarely into the current publishing trend of performative isolation. It is the literary equivalent of Henry David Thoreau meets *Eat, Pray, Love*, minus the self-awareness. We are supposed to swoon over the "dangerous act of living on the other side of stimulation." Give me a microscope and a lab coat. True isolation is failing organic chemistry because you cannot afford the textbook, not voluntarily abandoning your smartphone while a publisher cuts you a massive check. 

Yet I will probably read it when it eventually ends up in the bargain bin. Or maybe I will just stare at my own broken stove until I achieve enlightenment. No, that is a lie. I need to go dig through a stack of water-damaged paperbacks at the municipal book depository for out-of-print philosophical treatises on existential dread just to cleanse my palate.
