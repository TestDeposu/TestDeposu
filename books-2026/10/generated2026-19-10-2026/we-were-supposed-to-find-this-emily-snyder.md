**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Staring at another pitch deck for a sapphic time-travel rom-com makes me want to throw my lukewarm espresso directly through the drywall. *We Were Supposed to Find This* is currently sitting in my inbox demanding an advance analysis based on a three-paragraph synopsis because the author hasn't even finished drafting the middle, yet the marketing machine is already grinding bones into flour. Back to the Future meets Casey McQuiston, they say—as if duct-taping two disparate pop-culture touchstones together somehow constitutes a personality. 

### Nostalgia Bait and Dial-Up Trauma

The premise leans heavily on 1998 aesthetics—dial-up internet, physical cameras, and the Backstreet Boys—because millennials with disposable income will literally purchase anything wrapped in mid-range childhood trauma. And honestly? I respect the grift. Aurora is apparently a temporal nexus where Harmony’s Comet drops teenagers into their mothers' adolescent friend groups like a millennial episode of *The Twilight Zone*. Yet the synopsis offers zero actual mechanics for this cosmic phenomena. Hand-waving a comet as a plot device is fine, but spare me the faux-scientific hand-wringing about a rip in time. Just let the girls kiss under the dial-up modem glow without pretending there is hard sci-fi world-building happening. 

### The Paradox Industrial Complex

Everly goes back, meets Sam, befriends her own mother, and immediately threatens the spacetime continuum—a narrative beat recycled so many times it has flat-out lost its structural integrity. The temporal paradox here is aggressively flimsy. If you alter the past, you evaporate. We all watched the same DeLorean movies, Emily. Yet the stakes are framed around losing the mother who birthed her and the girl she is falling for over a single summer. It is peak high-stakes melodrama written for people who unironically use Letterboxd to rate rom-coms as high art. The emotional resonance relies entirely on the reader projecting their own teenage angst onto a fiery-haired manic pixie dream girl who vanishes into the ether. 

### Market Predictions and Literary Exhaustion

My professional verdict? This thing is going to debut at number one on the New York Times bestseller list and sell out every Barnes & Noble in the tri-state area before the ink is even dry. BookTok is going to lose its absolute mind over the timeline-altering angst, screaming into the void about sapphic longing and butterfly effects. It is engineered in a sterile lab for maximum emotional manipulation, hitting every single algorithmic checkbox with ruthless precision. But let us be entirely real for a second—the prose is going to rely on four hundred pages of the protagonist staring longingly at corded landline phones while overthinking heteronormative suburban ennui. 

Still, I have to grade the manuscript eventually, assuming it ever actually arrives on my desk. Right now, I cannot even focus on structural edits because my local independent bookstore just ran out of the latest issue of *The Paris Review*, and the barista at the counter looked at me sideways when I asked for a black coffee without a lecture on fair-trade bean harvesting.
