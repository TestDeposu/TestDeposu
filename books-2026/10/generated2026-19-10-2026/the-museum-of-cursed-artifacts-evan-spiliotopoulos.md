Let us pretend for a fleeting, agonizing second that I am not currently staring at a blinking cursor trying to stretch three paragraphs of promotional fluff into a thousand words just to satisfy an invoice. *The Museum of Cursed Artifacts* arrives on shelves in 2026, dragging its predictable corpses behind it. Evan Spiliotopoulos wants us to believe he is marrying Shirley Jackson to a theme park. But what does that actually mean for the women trapped inside the narrative architecture? 

### The Financial Desperation of Dead Men

The premise rests on bankrupt heirs and a cast of mercenaries. A cancelled celebrity ghost hunter, a traumatized psychic, a defrocked exorcist, a terminally ill archaeologist, and a controversial physicist walk into a mansion. Yet notice how the women in this lineup are inevitably defined by their damages. The psychic is traumatized. The men are merely disgraced, cancelled, or controversial—labels implying they kept their agency while the women lost theirs to the very specters haunting them. 

And look, I have read this exact pitch in fifty different slush pile manuscripts this month alone. But Spiliotopoulos leans hard on the capitalist rot of turning hauntings into a Disneyland of horrors. Everything must be monetized. Even the afterlife becomes content. 

### The Commodification of Terror

Yet the synopsis insists the artifacts possess an intelligence and a diabolical purpose. Of course they do. Objects never just sit there; they always target the fragile psyche of the resident clairvoyant. Will the psychic heal through trauma-bonding with an antique mirror? Probably. Will the male experts mansplain the paranormal activity while she does the heavy emotional lifting of screaming into the dark? Count on it. 

We are constantly sold this tired trope where female intuition is treated as a diagnostic tool for the haunted estate rather than a valid intellect. The ghosts choose them for a purpose. Naturally. Because a woman's body or mind is never her own in modern horror—it is always public domain for male entities and male authors alike. 

Look at *The Haunting of Hill House*. Jackson understood that the haunting is internal, suffocating, domestic. Turning that terror into a corporate attraction strips away the psychological dread and replaces it with jump scares and CGI ghosts. It drains the feminist subversion right out of the Gothic tradition. 

I really do not have time for this derivative nonsense today. My vintage Schwinn is leaning against the brick wall outside the cafe, and the sky just turned the bruised, metallic color of an oncoming flash flood. If I don't run out there right now, the leather saddle is going to be completely ruined, and I care infinitely more about preserving English leather than I do about yet another billionaire's cursed trinket box.
