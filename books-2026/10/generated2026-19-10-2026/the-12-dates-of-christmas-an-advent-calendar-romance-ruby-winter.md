**Author:** Ruby Winter  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *The 12 Dates of Christmas*—or rather, the *A Time for Giving* anthology—is a masterclass in how not to sell a book. Because let’s be honest: this isn’t a romance. It’s a *Christmas anthology*, and the synopsis reads like a grocery list of historical trauma with a side of "Dear Canada" nostalgia. The problem? It doesn’t *feel* like an anthology. It feels like a museum exhibit of Canadian historical fiction, where the curator forgot to mention the price of admission.

The synopsis leans *hard* into the "Christmas after" framing—because of course, these diarists are all back for a sequel, like they’re characters in a bad Hallmark movie where the villain never actually leaves. Johanna Leary reunites with her brother after Grosse-Île? That’s not a story, that’s a *plot hole*—unless you’re selling merch. Mary Kobayashi’s second internment camp Christmas? Bold. But where’s the payoff? The synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at the emotional arc, just drops names like they’re ingredients in a stew: Jean Little, Barbara Haworth-Attard, Sarah Ellis—all heavy hitters, but the execution here feels like a *Dear Canada* fanfic convention panel.

And then there’s the *A Special Gift* tease—Ruby Slipperjack’s preview for her *Dear Canada* entry, set before Residential School. That’s the kind of hook that makes you *want* to read it, because it’s not just another historical vignette. It’s a *setup*. But the synopsis doesn’t sell the tension, just the setup. It’s like being handed a movie trailer where the only line is, "And then… something bad happens."

The real crime? No thematic cohesion. This isn’t a *12 Dates of Christmas*—it’s a *12 Stories of Christmas*, and the synopsis doesn’t even acknowledge the romance angle. Is this a love story? A historical drama? A Christmas special? The synopsis doesn’t decide. It just *lists*. And that’s the problem with anthologies: they’re either a curated experience or a dumpster fire, and this one feels like someone threw a bunch of *Dear Canada* diaries into a blender and called it a gift.

But then—*lexical whiplash*—I’m being too hard. Maybe the magic is in the *unreliable narrator* of the synopsis itself. Because if this anthology *does* deliver the kind of emotional gut-punch that *Pieces of the Past* or *Flame and Ashes* did, then it’s a steal. If it’s just another *Dear Canada* rehash, then it’s a missed opportunity. The synopsis doesn’t tell you which.

And that’s the *pivot*: I initially thought this was a lazy cash-in, but now I’m wondering if the real genius is in the *contradiction*. Because what if the "Christmas after" isn’t about closure? What if it’s about the *weight* of what came before? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just *implies*.
