Down the espresso goes, leaving nothing behind but a bitter slick of dark roast against porcelain. My tailor made certain the charcoal wool sat razor-sharp across the shoulders—crucial when one must project absolute authority before staring down a canvas that cost more than a suburban estate. But my mind is currently stuck in the third round of a featherweight bout I watched last night, marveling at how a man can take a clean left hook to the jaw and still calculate his exit vector. Precision under duress. It is a rare aesthetic. Allison Saft clearly understands that kinetic panic, even if *Heartsgrove* trades the canvas mat for a dying forest and a slow botanical decay. 

### Roots, Rot, and Ruin

Look at the premise. A botanist is literally turning into timber. A ticking clock disguised as chlorophyll. It is delightfully grotesque body horror draped in the delicate lace of queer romance. Or perhaps I have misread the tone completely—perhaps it is actually a sweeping, breathless pastoral idyll. Actually, no. Let us discard that generous thought. The blurb explicitly promises a curse eating a man’s body alive while he blacks out in a blighted grove. That is not pastoral. That is psychological claustrophobia wrapped in bark. Saft proved with *A Dark and Drowning Tide* that she has a ruthless grasp of environmental dread—she treats ecosystems like pressure cookers. 

The formula here relies heavily on the forced-pact dynamic. A dying dryad prince offering a cure in exchange for a spot of heavy gardening. How quaint. We are swimming in these transactional romances lately, aren't we? Every modern fantasy author seems contractually obligated to force two stubborn entities into a damp cabin until they fall in love. Give me the visceral punch of an authentic power struggle over a predictable enemies-to-lovers arc any day. Yet, the inclusion of a fading prince whose life force is tied to his trees adds a tragic symmetry to the protagonist's own arboreal transformation. They are both losing their humanity in real-time. Or their plant-anity. Whatever the proper botanical term for leafy distress might be. 

### The Stakes of Chlorophyll

The narrative weight hangs entirely on whether Saft leans into the physical revulsion of bodily petrifaction. If Ludwig's transformation feels too much like a quirky cosmetic update—a cute little twig sprouting from behind the ear—the entire architecture collapses. I want bone-deep agony. I want the sensory terror of stiffening joints and creeping roots threatening to rupture human skin. Think Jeff VanderMeer meeting classic folklore, filtered through the bitter melancholy usually reserved for late-stage Gothic tragedies. 

And then there is the pacing. A handful of weeks to reverse a magical ecosystem failure while fighting off terminal botany. It leaves very little room for lingering glances by moonlight—though I suspect we will get an abundance of those anyway, nestled uncomfortably between exposition dumps about blighted soil. One hopes the prose maintains the sharp, atmospheric chill of her previous outings without leaning too heavily into the melancholic sugar that often plagues contemporary romantic fantasy. The best speculative fiction tastes like ash and iron, not spun glass. 

Time to settle the bill and head upstairs before the private crowd arrives and the pretentious murmuring begins. But first, I must return to my dressing room and reorganize those towering, gravity-defying stacks of sixteenth-century botanical folios that are currently threatening to avalanche across the floorboards.
