**Author:** Colleen Oakley  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

God save us all from the tyranny of nostalgia-bait marketed to people who buy artisanal salt. Colleen Oakley has apparently decided that what contemporary literature truly lacks is yet another fifty-year exercise in emotional water-treading, packaged neatly for airport bookstores next to the overpriced dried mango. Look at the setup of *In Concert*. Two teenagers, a Grateful Dead concert in 1977, and a Polaroid camera. Groundbreaking. One might as well exhume the ghost of Joan Didion just to slap her across the face with a wristband from Coachella.

### The Polaroid Parasite and Other Modern Plagues

The premise relies entirely on a flea market find ending up on social media, which immediately triggers my hereditary migraine. Do we honestly expect a reclusive classical violinist to be dragged down by an algorithm because some unemployed millennial with a ring light discovered garbage in a box of old postcards? It has all the sociological grace of a *Succession* outtake directed by Zack Snyder. The synopsis promises a heart-wrenching exploration of the connective power of music, as if we have not suffered through a decade of novels written by people whose only reference point for rock history is a Fleetwood Mac TikTok trend. 

### The Stolen Cadence of Star-Crossed Cliches

And what of the structure? Told only in snapshots of iconic concerts. How daring. How terribly avant-garde. One can practically smell the patchouli and cheap merlot radiating from the page. Ham and Bee—good grief, those names sound like characters from a straight-to-video adaptation of a mediocre YA dystopia—spend half a century missing each other by inches. They are together. They are apart. They are never far from each other’s hearts. The prose we are forced to anticipate will undoubtedly drip with the sort of faux-poetic sentimentality that makes one want to swallow a handful of watch batteries just to feel something sharp. 

### The Cruelty of Unearned Epics

Call me an elitist. I do not care. True poetry demands precision, a razor-sharp slicing away of the mundane, whereas this commercial machinery exists solely to harvest tears from readers who weep during insurance commercials. *Daisy Jones and the Six* was already a shallow pool; dragging Taylor Jenkins Reid’s ghost into the marketing copy feels like threatening the reader with a second helping of gruel. If I wanted to watch two people fail at timing for fifty years, I would simply read the memoirs of Lord Byron’s accountants. Or better yet, study the tragic trajectory of the Hindenburg, which at least had the decency to catch fire rather than linger in mediocrity. 

One simply cannot waste another evening entertaining the romanticization of missed trains and poorly timed polaroids when the cosmos itself demands immediate architectural intervention. I must excuse myself now, as I am contractually and existentially obligated to construct a fully scaled, glow-in-the-dark diorama of the solar system out of papier-mâché and vintage opera glasses before midnight.
