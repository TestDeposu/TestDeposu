**Author:** Donna Freitas  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-03 APL

Another nostalgia-soaked excursion into the 1970s involving a missing child. Because what the literary market desperately needed was yet another beach-town procedural channeling the exact energy of the Fyre Festival documentary—minus the self-awareness. Donna Freitas returns with *Wolves of Summer*, the second installment of her Ocean State series, trading sunshine for absolute derivative gloom. Bonnet Shores is apparently a place where children vanish into the scrub pines while teenagers brood on bikes. Groundbreaking.

### The Anatomy of a Monotony

The official hook relies on the oldest trick in the thriller ledger: the twelve-year-old girl who fails to return home. Naturally, the older sister blames herself. Because guilt is currency in these paperbacks. We are treated to a constellation of characters designed to tick every demographic box required by modern suspense. There is Maddie chasing shadows, Cricket unearthing teenage trauma—always with the trauma—and Diana, the twenty-one-year-old criminal justice student working part-time at the Narragansett Police Department. Hold your horses, Diana. Let us not solve the entire Rhode Island judicial crisis before sunset. 

And the wolves are always circling. The official synopsis tells us this with the subtlety of a sledgehammer to an antique porcelain vase. The themes are laid out like items on a dry cleaner's counter: lost childhood, female friendship, redemption. One can practically hear the collective sigh of editors in Manhattan ordering another round of coastal noir. 

### Aesthetic Bankruptcy

Let us critique the architecture of the pitch itself. The synopsis leans heavily on the convenience of the local community. The working-class neighbors bringing donuts and coffee. The beach club royalty. It is a class war played out in the dunes over stale pastries. Yet, we are expected to shiver at the realization that someone from the search party might be the culprit. A twist so ancient it probably appeared on the back of a clay tablet in Sumeria. 

Freitas is working in the shadow of superior stylists like Megan Abbott, whose explorations of female adolescence actually possess teeth instead of soft-focus nostalgia. Here, the prose promises a hard look at violence against women and girls, yet wraps it in the cozy blanket of middle-market suspense. It wants the prestige of a literary autopsy while serving up the narrative fast-food of a beach read. 

One looks at this setup and feels the desperate urge to laugh out loud, preferably while sipping something chilled and tremendously expensive. The caterer just cancelled for the Saturday evening acquisition dinner, leaving twenty-four very impatient collectors with nothing to eat except my growing contempt for contemporary publishing trends.
