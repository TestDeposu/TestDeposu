The air in the townhouse is entirely too dry this afternoon, smelling faintly of imported tobacco and the expensive mahogany polish the staff uses when they think I am not paying attention. I’ve been sitting on this review for weeks. Pam Jenoff is giving us yet another historical exercise in feminine solidarity, as if the entire 1920s existed solely for women to lock arms and stare defiantly into the middle distance while escaping the patriarchy. Groundbreaking.

### The Architecture of Despair

New York in 1919 should reek of coal smoke, cheap gin, and post-war panic. Yet the synopsis for *The Society of Forgotten Wives* smooths the jagged edges right off the Lower East Side, trading genuine historical squalor for a pristine, marketable mystery about missing men and plucky journalists. Hazel Brister leaves her scandal behind—how delightfully cliché—to punch typewriter keys for a living. Because apparently, every disgraced debutante with a trust fund deficiency immediately gravitates toward investigative reporting. 

I suppose we must applaud the sheer audacity of turning archival dust into a page-turning crusade. Or perhaps we shouldn't. Actually, wait—let me retract that mild concession entirely. The premise is painfully derivative. Edith Wharton would weep, and not for the right reasons. We are once again subjected to the tired trope of the upper-class woman discovering her inner gumshoe because some unfortunate named Freyda wandered into a newsroom looking bewildered. 

### The Commodity of Sisterhood

The thematic core here relies entirely on collective female resilience, a literary crutch that publishers lean on with the subtlety of a falling grand piano. Jenoff clearly aims for the emotional resonance of Kristin Hannah or Marie Benedict. Yet I worry the narrative will drown in its own earnestness. The synopsis promises "dark forces" trying to stop them, which sounds suspiciously like a melodramatic placeholder for actual historical tension. Do we really need another clandestine society of clever women outsmarting the shadowy patriarchy from a cramped basement? Give me actual human vice, genuine moral rot, or at least a protagonist who isn't entirely noble. 

Yet—and this is precisely where my mind fractures—perhaps the sheer commercial juggernaut of these historical fiction algorithms demands this exact brand of packaged empowerment. Maybe readers are exhausted by nuance. They want an easy narrative arc where trauma neatly converts into agency by chapter three. 

The prose itself in the synopsis reads like it was churned out by a committee of branding consultants terrified of offending anyone who buys books at airport terminals. 

Wait. Was that a shattering sound echoing from the foyer, or did the gardener just drop another imported terracotta planter? Excuse me, I must go inspect the wreckage immediately.
