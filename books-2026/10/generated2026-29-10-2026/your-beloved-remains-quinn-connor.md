People are constantly acting like professional jealousy and desperate academic fraud are charming little quirks instead of the exhausting, miserable personality traits they actually are, and quite frankly, my patience for another overwrought campus drama about women who apparently have never heard of a normal hobby is evaporating into thin air faster than my coffee getting cold on the counter while someone screams about a missing red block. Quinn Connor wants us to believe that *Your Beloved Remains* is going to reinvent the dark academia wheel by dragging some poor, isolated archivist named Celia and her precious professional rival Joan into a messy swamp of forged 19th-century manuscripts pickled in formaldehyde—yes, actual formaldehyde, because subtle metaphor is apparently dead and buried—just to save a crumbling Southern college that should have been put out of its misery decades ago, giving us all the predictable, eye-rolling thrill of watching two supposedly brilliant adults completely ruin their lives over dusty paper instead of just going to therapy or finding a decent book club. 

Unhinged.

And yet, looking closer at the publisher shoving this alongside *Plain Bad Heroines* and *If We Were Villains*, maybe I am entirely misjudging the sheer, petty pettulence of these characters. Actually, scratch that entirely, because petty academic backstabbing is precisely the kind of petty nonsense that keeps humanity entertaining on a rainy Tuesday when you just want to watch other people make catastrophically poor life choices from the comfort of a home that desperately needs a vacuuming. 

### The Molds We Deserve

The thematic weight here rests entirely on rot—both literal, moldering campus architecture and the metaphorical decay of institutional legacy supported by the rotting corpse of Beatrice Donahue’s estate. We are looking at a glaring critique of how universities prey on the blind loyalty of people like Celia, who project their illicit childhood fixations onto dead Victorian writers because they cannot manage actual human relationships with people living in the same century. Joan comes equipped with an astounding talent for mimicry and a dark secret, which is publisher-speak for "we needed a foil who looks good in a turtleneck and possesses zero boundaries." Connor is clearly leaning into the same theatrical, obsessive girlhood tropes that Donna Tartt birthed and modern TikTok book spaces weaponized into the ground. 

Brutal. 

Let us be entirely honest about the formula here: take one remote, atmospheric campus in the Southern Ozarks, add a heavy dose of homoerotic tension born from mutual resentment, stir in a forged manuscript that will inevitably come back to bite them, and bake until everyone is screaming in a damp archive at midnight. Will the prose elevate the cliché? Possibly. Will I roll my eyes so hard I give myself a migraine? Absolutely. 

The toddler has hidden my favorite fountain pen again.
