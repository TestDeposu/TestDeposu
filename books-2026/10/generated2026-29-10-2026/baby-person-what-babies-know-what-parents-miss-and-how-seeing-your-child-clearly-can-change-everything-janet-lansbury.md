**Page Count:** Unknown

The synopsis for *Baby Person* reads like a corporate press release for a self-help franchise—so much so that it makes me wonder if Lansbury’s team consulted the same ghostwriters who turned *The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck* into a TikTok trend. The idea that a baby is a "thinking, expressive person" from birth is not exactly groundbreaking—it’s the kind of platitude you’d expect from a wellness influencer who’s never held a screaming infant at 3 AM. And yet, here we are, treating this as if it’s some kind of radical manifesto, like if *The Bell Curve* had been rewritten by a yoga teacher who’s never actually done a downward dog.

The RIE philosophy, which Lansbury cites, is fine—if you enjoy the kind of parenting that treats children like delicate porcelain dolls you’re afraid to drop. But the synopsis doesn’t even attempt to grapple with the fact that most parents don’t have the time, patience, or emotional bandwidth to engage in this kind of "emotionally present" parenting. It’s all very well to say that babies are "capable of being in relationship with you from birth," but what about the parents who are exhausted, overwhelmed, and just trying to keep their own sanity intact? Lansbury’s approach seems to assume that parenting is a spiritual discipline rather than the chaotic, often frustrating reality it is.

The mention of "pressure points"—sleep, eating, tantrums—feels like a nod to the fact that this book is likely to be marketed as a solution to the very human problems of new parenthood. But the synopsis doesn’t offer any real insight into how Lansbury plans to address these issues beyond vague advice about "boundaries" and "joyful connection." It’s all very *The Secret* meets *What to Expect When You’re Expecting*, but without the same level of practicality. Where’s the dark humor? Where’s the acknowledgment that some days, you just want to scream into a pillow and call it a win?

And then there’s the tone—soothing, almost saccharine, like a lullaby written by someone who’s never actually been up all night with a colicky baby. It’s the kind of parenting advice that would make *The Notebook* look like a manual for emotional detachment. Lansbury’s work is often praised for its "gentle parenting" approach, but gentle parenting can easily become a euphemism for permissive parenting, and that’s a line this book seems determined not to cross—though it’s not clear how it avoids it, since the synopsis doesn’t actually say anything controversial.

The most glaring omission is any mention of the fact that Lansbury is also a podcaster. This book feels like it’s trying to position itself as a standalone work, but the reality is that it’s likely just an expansion of her existing content. It’s the kind of book that makes you wonder if Lansbury’s team is more concerned with branding than substance. After all, how many parenting books have been written by people who’ve never actually raised children? The answer is too many, and *Baby Person* risks joining that crowded shelf.

The synopsis also doesn’t do anything to distinguish this book from the dozens of other parenting guides that have come before it. Where’s the edge? Where’s the provocation? Where’s the acknowledgment that parenting is as much about survival as it is about connection? Lansbury’s work is often described as "transformative," but the synopsis doesn’t offer any real sense of what that transformation might look like. It’s all very well to say that parents should "worry less and enjoy the relationship," but how? The book doesn’t even attempt to answer that question.

And yet, despite all this, there’s something undeniably appealing about the idea of a parenting book that doesn’t treat children as problems to be solved. It’s the kind of book that makes you long for a world where parenting wasn’t reduced to a series of checklists and milestones. But the synopsis doesn’t do enough to convince you that *Baby Person* is anything more than another in a long line of self-help books that promise more than they deliver.
