**Author:** Andrew Lewis Conn  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The heirs of literary monoliths are always such tedious bores, dragging their trust funds through the wreckage of a dead man's bibliography like pampered hounds at a pheasant shoot. Andrew Lewis Conn proposes to offer us yet another variation on this tired pedigree panic in *I Swore I'd Burn This Book*. Dmitri Boris Godunov inherits the classic burden: a patriarch who wrote the kind of scandalous, banned-in-Boston prose that only exists in novels written by men who never folded a fitted sheet in their lives. 

And now a forthcoming biography threatens to drag the old man's reputation through the mud. 

### The Ethics of Arson

One wonders if Conn watched the disastrous 2022 Fyre Festival documentary and thought, yes, this exact structural incompetence is how I shall model a Russian-American literary inheritance caper. Actually, no. That is a lie. The kinetic energy here feels much closer to a botched Christie's auction of dubious Faberge eggs. The central dilemma rests on an unlit match. To burn the manuscript or to cash the check? A choice so agonizingly conventional it reads like a creative writing prompt from the Iowa Writers' Workshop circa 1994. Nabokov’s son famously preserved *The Original of Laura*, saving the scraps from the incinerator against his father's dying wishes because, naturally, nobody ever actually burns the damn papers. Sentimentality always triumphs over posthumous obedience. 

Or perhaps Conn will subvert this entirely. Or perhaps he will wallow in it. The synopsis remains maddeningly coy about the actual mechanics of the plot, preferring to gesture vaguely toward the Swiss Alps and literary Easter eggs—the literary equivalent of name-dropping Walter Benjamin at a dinner party to mask the fact that you haven't finished *The Arcades Project*. 

### The Geography of Paternal Debt

The geopolitical canvas is aggressively wide. New York to Russia to the Swiss Alps. A grand tour of tax havens and ancestral trauma. We are promised a lyrical meditation on fathers and sons, which usually translates to two hundred pages of men shouting about manuscripts while drinking bad scotch in wood-paneled libraries. Conn clearly wants to capture the ambient dread of our current cultural moment—where every dead author is guilty of some retroactive heresy and every text is a crime scene waiting for a woke archivist with a clipboard. 

Yet the real mystery is whether Conn possesses the stylistic audacity to pull off a meta-fictional farce of this magnitude without sinking into academic self-indulgence. Picaresque capers require a light touch. Godunov senior sounds like a pastiche of every exiled dissident who ever chain-smoked Gauloises on a NPR podcast. One fears the prose will lean heavily on adjectives that sound expensive but taste like cardboard. 

I must run, my aesthetician is threatening to blacklist me if I miss my chemical peel for this wretched rosacea.
