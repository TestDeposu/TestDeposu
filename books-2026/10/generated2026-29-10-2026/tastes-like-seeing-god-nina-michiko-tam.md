**Author:** Nina Michiko Tam  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis of *Tastes Like Seeing God* is so aggressively *The Secret Life of Walter Mitty* meets *Eat Pray Love*—but with the existential dread of a chef who just lost her brother to a kitchen accident—you can practically taste the irony before you even crack the spine. There’s a certain *Benedict Cumberbatch in a Michelin-starred kitchen* vibe to the premise, where grief is measured in courses and the world is a buffet of unresolved questions. The idea of a culinary pilgrimage to "taste the divine" is either profound or a little too on-the-nose, depending on whether you’re the kind of person who believes in fate or just really loves a good metaphor.

Jessie’s journey—from the sterile silence of her parents’ conservative Chinese household to the chaotic, sensory-overload of global kitchens—feels like it could be a *Parasite*-level critique of class and belonging, if the execution isn’t just another *Chef’s Table* fantasy. The list of five meals is the book’s *one ring to rule them all*, a MacGuffin that could either elevate the narrative or collapse under its own weight if the author leans too hard into the "spiritual awakening" angle. Because let’s be real: if the meals are as transcendent as the synopsis suggests, we’re either in for a *Kitchen Confidential*-level confession or a *The God of Small Things* in a wok.

The comparison to *The Bear* isn’t just about the chef angle—it’s about the raw, unfiltered emotional chaos of grief. But where *The Bear* is a brutal, sweat-soaked scream into the void, this book seems to want to be a *sophisticated* scream, the kind that comes with a side of truffle oil and a glass of Bordeaux. The synopsis doesn’t say whether Jessie’s pilgrimage will be a cathartic mess or a carefully curated Instagram feed of her "journey," but the tone suggests the latter. And that’s where the risk lies: if the book tries to be *both* a coming-of-age story and a gourmet travelogue, it might end up tasting like overcooked risotto—rich, but with a sad, mushy center.

The thematic potential here is *huge*. Food as memory, food as rebellion, food as a way to confront the divine—it’s all there, waiting to be mined. But the synopsis is so vague that it’s hard to tell if this will be a *Wolf Hall*-level character study or a *Salt Fat Acid Heat*-style love letter to global cuisine. The mention of "illegal French delicacies" and "famed Senegalese dishes" is a nice nod to the kind of food writing that makes you want to bookmark a flight, but without more detail, it’s hard to say if this will be a *Braving the Wilderness* of the culinary world or just another *The Girl on the Train* with a fork.

And then there’s the Tommy angle—his list, his death, his *mysterious* phrasing. Is this a ghost story? A mystery? A meditation on legacy? The synopsis doesn’t say, but the way it’s framed, it feels like it could be all three. The danger is that the brother’s presence will become a plot device rather than a character, a ghost haunting the pages but never fully realized. Because if this is going to work, Tommy’s voice—his beliefs, his contradictions—has to be as vivid as the meals Jessie chases.

But here’s the thing: the synopsis is *so* good at selling the idea of the journey that it almost doesn’t matter if the execution is shaky. It’s the kind of premise that makes you want to believe in the magic of it all, even if the reality is more mundane. And that’s the real test—whether the book can live up to the hype of its own synopsis.
