**Page Count:** Unknown

Look at the way history always seems to anchor itself to our breath, clinging to the invisible architecture of memory until an old woman mutters a name from a forgotten era and cracks the foundation wide open. Vanessa McCausland understands this silent geography better than most, crafting a narrative in *The Heart Note* that stretches across three distinct epochs—2022, 2001, and 1532—like a bridge built out of sheer atmosphere, tethering a modern daughter grasping at ghosts to a Renaissance glove maker whose stained hands changed the course of scent forever. The premise relies heavily on the kind of generational haunting that Kate Morton or Kristin Hannah perfected, yet it pivots away from dusty family manors and lands squarely in the sun-drenched, herb-scented alleys of Grasse, France. Let's be real for a second. We have all read too many dual-timeline historical novels where the past merely serves as a convenient attic for family skeletons. 

Or maybe we haven't read enough of them, because tying anosmia—the literal loss of smell—to a failing marriage and a mother losing her mind to dementia is a stroke of conceptual brilliance that elevates this far above standard historical fiction fare. 

Brilliance. 

Wait. Is it brilliance, or is it merely a clever hook masking the familiar domestic friction of a mother-daughter rift that has been recycled in airport paperbacks for decades? 

Consider the 1532 timeline featuring Heli, whose toil over leather vats and floral tinctures introduces a gritty, tactile element to a genre that too often smells exclusively of potpourri and lavender soap. McCausland attempts to bind these fractured women together through the alchemy of distillation and confession, asking whether our deepest regrets linger in the olfactory bulb long after our conscious minds have let go. The synopsis promises a mystery reaching across centuries, but the true enigma lies in whether the prose can sustain the weight of such an ambitious structural leap without buckling under its own thematic ambition. Fans of Susanna Kearsley will find much to admire in these French landscapes, assuming the execution matches the undeniable allure of the pitch.
