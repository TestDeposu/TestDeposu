**Author:** Stephanie Krzywonos  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

Flour is currently dusting the kitchen counter because the boys demanded cinnamon rolls at six in the morning, which means my fingers are sticky and I am staring at this upcoming memoir synopsis with the exact brand of wary exhaustion reserved for dough that refuses to rise. 

Stephanie Krzywonos is heading down to the ice for six summers and a winter in *The Blue Hours: My Summers and Winter in Antarctica*, and honestly, I am already sweating just reading about whiteout storms while the oven preheats to four hundred degrees. 

And Ed Yong calls it stunning, which usually means I am going to be weeping into a mixing bowl by chapter three. 

Because grief memoirs are a dime a dozen these days—haven't we all read enough Joan Didion disciples trying to freeze their trauma in a glacier?—yet this one promises a mix of history, science, and the erased stories of queer, female, and BIPOC explorers who usually get left out of the old dead white guy polar narratives. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that last part. 

Actually, the historical critique angle might save it from being just another self-indulgent diary about someone running away from a dead best friend at the end of the earth. 

Or maybe the historical critique angle will make it feel like a textbook disguised as a heartbreak. 

Who knows. 

Books about extreme isolation always walk a razor-thin line between profound ecological meditation and someone complaining about the freeze-dried rations. 

Ice.

Actually, let's talk about the penguins for a second. 

Because every single book set in Antarctica has to feature the token adorable penguins to keep us from throwing the pages across the room in a fit of climate-anxiety despair, and I just know those birds are going to be doing some heavy lifting when the existential dread gets too thick. 

If this sounds anything like Kathleen Dean Moore or Melissa Febos—both of whom apparently blurbed or resemble the vibe here—we are looking at prose so heavy and gorgeous you need a crowbar just to pry open the paragraphs. 

Give me a break. 

Just give me the ice, the seals, the darkness, and skip the part where the author compares a melting glacier to her broken heart. 

Or maybe don't. 

Maybe that is precisely what we need in a wounded world, as the synopsis dramatically asks while reminding us of our rapidly warming planet. 

My youngest just tracked melted butter across the hardwood floor. 

Magnificent. 

The local independent bookstore has run out of my favorite literary magazine.
