**Author:** Stacy Sivinski  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

October wind rattles the chain-link fence outside the community center, carrying the distinct scent of decaying oak leaves and municipal exhaust, which feels like the exact right time to dissect Stacy Sivinski’s upcoming *Where Moonflowers Bloom*. Here we have another entry into the aggressively monetized cozy fantasy industrial complex, a genre that desperately needs to touch grass—or concrete, considering the Chicago setting. Sivinski previously gave us *The Crescent Moon Tearoom*, a book praised by people who think drinking herbal tea while ignoring city council budget cuts constitutes a personality. Now she returns with a wandering storyteller named Sadie and a prickly botanist named Tom, because apparently, modern fiction cannot sustain itself without pairing a manic pixie vagabond with an emotionally constipated hobbyist who hoards chlorophyll. The synopsis leans hard on the tired tropes of gentrification disguised as magical realism, treating urban infrastructure as a mere backdrop for bourgeois emotional healing instead of engaging with the actual material conditions of civic decline. But perhaps I am being too harsh on Sadie and her sudden loss of magic.

Contradictory as it sounds, maybe the magical greenhouse angle works better on paper than my cynical block-association brain wants to admit. 

Secret rooftop gardens in Chicago? Please. Anyone who has lived through a midwestern winter knows those plants would freeze into botanical popsicles by November, magic or no magic. Yet Sivinski asks us to suspend our disbelief not just for the supernatural elements, but for the premise that two adults with severe avoidance issues can save a fading metropolis by gardening and telling stories. Real community organizing involves endless zoning board meetings, arguing about property taxes, and fighting landlords over lead paint, not wandering through forgotten alleys finding glowing ferns. And yet, if we compare this to TJ Klune or Travis Baldree—authors who have successfully commodified the aesthetic of soft, low-stakes healing for an exhausted labor force—Sivinski is simply giving the masses what they demand. People want a fantasy where systemic collapse is fixed by two quirky individuals hugging their feelings out in a greenhouse instead of structural revolution. 

Predictable.

At least the synopsis commits to the bit. Two opposites forced to collaborate while confronting their deepest fears, code for predictable romantic friction manufactured by a lack of basic communication skills. One day, authors might let introverted botanists just enjoy their plants without forcing them to fall in love with nomadic fabulists, but today is clearly not that day. 

I have to go convince a toddler that taking a bath is not a punishment.
