**Author:** Samantha Kolber  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-10-17 APL

Memory is a slippery thing when you are sweeping up glass from a dropped stoneware bowl while your sixteen-year-old ignores three text messages about dinner. Samantha Kolber understands this ache. *What She Stole* promises a quiet wreckage, locking a teenager in a drab mall security room just long enough for the ghosts of a schizophrenic father and a hollowed-out mother to step out of the drywall. 

### The Architecture of an Empty Holding Room

And why do we always trap girls in small spaces to make them interesting? Give me a protagonist who does something genuinely inconvenient, like burning down the garage or stealing a vintage tractor, instead of lifting lip gloss at the suburban mall. We are drowning in delicate coming-of-age verse novels disguised as prose. Every autumn brings a fresh crop of sad girls clutching notebooks, weeping over maternal neglect while the rain taps on the windowpane like an unpaid bill. 

Which brings me to the absolute absurdity of the television programming my youngest insists on watching downstairs. There is an animated blue dog screaming about ducks for twenty-two continuous minutes while I am trying to edit three hundred pages on seventeenth-century agrarian tax law with cold coffee sitting on my desk. Nobody in that cartoon is grieving a fractured childhood. Nobody is staring at a crack in the linoleum and wondering if their father’s brain chemistry was just a cruel weather pattern they inherited. That blue dog gets a warm hug and a cheerful accordion solo by minute seven. Meanwhile, Eloise Goldstein sits in a beige room waiting for a narcissistic mother who probably forgot which exit the precinct is off. Life does not resolve with a moral about sharing crayons. 

### Ink on Bruised Skin

Yet Kolber’s background in poetry—specifically that chapbook *Birth of a Daughter*—suggests the prose will carry enough bruised lyricism to forgive the tired tropes of suburban alienation. The synopsis leans hard into the holy trinity of modern literary survival: writing, mothering, and bodywork. Notice that. Not rebellion. Not outright rage. Just the quiet labor of fixing oneself with a pen and some somatic stretching after the adults in the room utterly failed to keep the roof from caving in. 

I predict this book will be heralded as a masterclass in interiority by people who have never had to scrub dried porridge off a baseboard at midnight. It will sit comfortably on nightstands next to Ottessa Moshfegh and Carmen Maria Machado, worn as a badge of complicated womanhood. 

Stop running in the hallway. I have to piece together a shattered vase before anyone notices.
