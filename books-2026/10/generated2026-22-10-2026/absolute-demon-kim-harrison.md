The pumpkin patch’s scent of cinnamon and damp leaves is cloying, like the way *The Hollows* series always smells of ambition—sweet, but with an undercurrent of something rotten. Rachel Morgan’s latest gambit, then, is just another corporate coup disguised as a family crisis. Trent’s collapse isn’t just a plot device; it’s a power vacuum, and Rachel’s forced ascension to CEO of Kalamack Industries is the kind of move that makes you wonder if the author’s been reading too much *Parasite* while sipping espresso. Because let’s be honest, when a witch demon has to run a vampire-owned conglomerate, it’s not just about survival—it’s about who gets to decide what survival even looks like.

The synopsis leans hard into the "power plays" trope, which is fine, but where’s the *nuance*? Where’s the moment where Rachel isn’t just a chess piece but a player with her own board? The vampires want Trent’s hold broken—classic villainy, but the elves? They want his *children*? That’s not just political maneuvering; that’s the kind of grotesque family drama you’d expect from *The Witcher* if it had a side of *The Handmaid’s Tale*. And yet, the synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at whether Rachel’s going to fight back or just play along, because why would she? She’s already made a deal to save her life, and now she’s stuck in the middle of a boardroom brawl where the only currency is bloodlines and boardroom seats.

But here’s the thing—this isn’t just about Rachel. It’s about Trent’s daughters, who are suddenly collateral in this game. And that’s where the synopsis fails spectacularly. Are they pawns? Are they weapons? Or are they just another layer of emotional blackmail? Because if this follows the pattern of the series, they’ll be the ones left holding the bag while the adults scheme. And that’s not just lazy writing—it’s the kind of thing that makes you wonder if the author thinks women are just props in their own stories.

Speaking of lazy writing—why does every *Hollows* book feel like it’s been written by a committee of people who’ve never met a woman they didn’t want to either worship or control? Rachel’s a badass, sure, but she’s also a witch demon who’s spent the last few books either fighting or scheming, and now she’s got to run a business? That’s not just a plot twist; that’s a cop-out. Because if she’s really the CEO, then she’s got to make decisions that aren’t just about survival—they’ve got to be about *power*. And the synopsis doesn’t even *try* to explore that.

But let’s talk about the vampires. They’re the obvious villains here, but the elves? They’re the real wild card. Because in a world where vampires are the ruling class, the elves are the underdogs with all the charm and none of the power. And that’s where the real tension should be—because if the elves are after Trent’s daughters, it’s not just about politics. It’s about legacy. It’s about who gets to decide what the future looks like. And the synopsis doesn’t even *ask* the question.

And don’t get me started on the fact that Trent’s collapse is framed as suspicious, but the synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at who might have done it. Is it the vampires? The elves? Or is it just another one of Trent’s schemes gone wrong? Because if this is going to be another *Hollows* book, then we know the answer—it’s always someone you trust. But the synopsis doesn’t even *try* to make you care.

But here’s the thing—this isn’t just about the plot. It’s about the themes. It’s about power, and control, and what happens when the people you love become the things you fear. And the synopsis doesn’t even *try* to explore that. It’s just another corporate thriller with a witch demon in the lead, and that’s not enough.
