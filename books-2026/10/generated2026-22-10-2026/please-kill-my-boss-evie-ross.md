Drafting this preview on my kitchen island while desperately ignoring the cold October drizzle tapping against the glass panes, I cannot help but wonder if Evie Ross has been secretly lurking in corporate group chats. *Please Kill My Boss* leans entirely into that universal, teeth-grinding exhaustion of answering emails from men who call themselves "thought leaders" while contributing precisely zero actual labor to human civilization. Naturally, the synopsis trades in high-concept workplace farce, pairing the eternal dream of corporate uprising with the absurd logistical nightmare of accidentally discovering your tyrannical supervisor dead on the linoleum floor. 

We have all fantasized about our supervisors spontaneously combusting during a quarterly review. Yet, translating that universal passive-aggressive daydream into a full-scale homicide investigation requires a very specific brand of madcap energy—think *The Office* filtered through a heavy dose of early Janet Evanovich, or perhaps if Mhairi McFarlane suddenly decided to write a whodunit after three too many espresso martinis. 

Actually, wait. Let me backtrack on comparing this to Evanovich. 

Upon second thought, the romantic entanglement promised with "Hot Todd From Design" suggests a much softer, more explicitly cozy dynamic that might utterly ruin the pacing of an otherwise sharp satirical takedown of mid-level management culture.

Toxic office dynamics.

### The Spreadsheet of Infinite Resentment

Corporate structure breeds paranoia faster than a damp basement breeds mold. Ross clearly understands that the modern open-plan office is essentially a psychological pressure cooker where petty grievances fester into magnificent, spreadsheet-worthy conspiracies. Julia cataloging her tormentors feels less like a crime thriller trope and more like the only rational response to nine years of passive-aggressive calendar invites and stolen lunches from the shared fridge. And frankly, the inclusion of Poppy the intern—who clearly prioritizes amateur sleuthing over selling office furniture—provides a wonderful foil to Julia's frantic corporate anxiety. We are looking at a messy, character-driven romp about solidarity in the face of miserable middle management. 

Relatable.

Except for the part where Julia has to nail a PowerPoint presentation while evading a murder charge. That genuinely crosses the line into pure horror. Nobody deserves to present Q3 metrics with a body count hanging over their head. 

It is time to face the chaos of the PTA bake sale.
