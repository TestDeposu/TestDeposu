Looking at an empty calendar square always makes my chest tighten, pulling me right back to the autumns when the house stayed entirely too quiet after the school bus rounded the corner. September carries a particular weight, a heavy kind of nostalgia that smells like yellowing notebook paper and dust motes dancing in fading afternoon light. And then Nafkote Tamirat drops *Teret Teret* on us, dragging a grieving, tired journalist right back to September 10, 2007—the exact precipice of an ending. 

Belaynesh Tessema is wasting her days at a magazine called *Trivial Pursuits*, which feels like a painfully sharp joke about how we spend our fragile hours on earth when our hearts are actually breaking over what we lost yesterday. 

Speaking of wasting time, why on earth did my youngest subject me through three grueling hours of a cartoon about anthropomorphic dump trucks last Tuesday? The animation was flat. The dialogue sounded like it was written by an algorithm designed by a committee of terrified accountants. We are feeding our children narrative fast food while genuine, daring literature like this waits in the wings, and frankly, it keeps me awake at night staring at the ceiling fan. 

Tamirat clearly has no patience for conventional storytelling, thank goodness. 

### The Weight of Unwritten Tomorrows

A talking parrot named Teret. Fallen angels. Time-bending myths crashing into the cynical, hyper-caffeinated reality of a twenty-first-century Washington, DC. It sounds wonderfully unhinged. Critics will inevitably compare Tamirat's maximalist whimsy to Salman Rushdie or perhaps Karen Russell, but there is something much more intimate hiding beneath these cosmic mechanics. This is a book about the stories we tell ourselves to survive the quiet hours. 

We cling to ancient legends because our modern maps keep failing us. 

### Looking Backward to Move Forward

What arrests me about the premise is the sheer stubbornness of the architecture. Time is broken. September 10 repeats endlessly. We all know what that feels like—reliving a specific, agonizing twenty-four-hour loop in our minds, wishing we could grab the steering wheel of the past and yank it toward a different horizon. Henok, sweating over his apocryphal book while a sarcastic bird and a bewildered journalist disrupt his grand design, is the ultimate patron saint of anyone who ever tried to control the uncontrollable. 

I am already entirely, stubbornly, irrationally sold on this novel. It will either achieve a breathtaking, soaring brilliance or crash spectacularly under the weight of its own ambition. Either way, I refuse to accept mediocrity. 

I need to go reorganize my towering stacks of reference books before they avalanche across the hardwood floor and bury me alive.
