**Author:** Eilish Quin  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-17 APL

The synopsis for *All the Beastly Things* is a masterclass in controlled ambiguity—like holding a lantern too close to a map of uncharted territory. You can see the shapes, the jagged edges of mountains and the faintest suggestion of rivers, but the valleys remain shadowed. The post-apocalyptic matriarchy is a trope so well-worn it’s practically a genre in itself, yet Quin’s framing of it as a "harmonious" utopia—blissfully free of hierarchy, capitalism, and time—is the kind of subversion that makes you pause. Because if there’s one thing we’ve learned from *The Power* or *The Handmaid’s Tale*, it’s that utopia is just another word for a cage with a view. The Field isn’t just a monastery; it’s a gilded cage, and the sisters inside have been conditioned to mistake their restraints for liberation. The real question isn’t whether this system is oppressive—it’s whether Igraine will ever see the bars.

Ophelia’s visions of an archangel are the book’s most tantalizing blank slate. Is this a literal divine intervention? A psychological unraveling? A metaphor for the return of the repressed (and here, the repressed is literally the male gender)? The synopsis doesn’t say, and that’s the point—it’s the kind of ambiguity that makes you itch to read, the way a well-placed ellipsis in a text message can linger like a half-remembered dream. But here’s the problem: the archangel is framed as *damningly masculine*, which immediately sets up a binary that feels too neat. Is this a story about the return of the patriarchy, or is it something more nuanced? The synopsis leans into the forbidden fruit trope—faith vs. desire, order vs. chaos—without giving us enough to tell if it’s playing with those dynamics or just recycling them. It’s the difference between *The Left Hand of Darkness* and *The Giver*—one asks questions, the other just flips a switch.

And then there’s the Moon. The Lunar Abbey isn’t just a reward for devotion; it’s a literal escape pod, a celestial promise that the sisters can transcend their earthly limitations. But the Moon is also a place of gothic grandeur, which means it’s probably going to be a pressure cooker of symbolism—celestial purity vs. earthly corruption, the divine vs. the carnal. The synopsis doesn’t hint at whether this will be a story about ascension or another kind of fall, but given the title—*All the Beastly Things*—you can bet there’s going to be some kind of reckoning with the "beast" inside the sisters. Is it the wildness they’ve been taught to fear? Or the very thing that makes them human?

The real tension here isn’t just the crisis of faith—it’s the crisis of *knowledge*. The sisters are told they live in a world free of hierarchy, but the Lunar Abbey’s exclusivity suggests otherwise. The rumors about the Order’s "darker origins" are the kind of thing that could unravel the entire system, but they’re also the kind of thing that could be weaponized. Is this a story about the dangers of dogma, or is it about the dangers of questioning it? The synopsis doesn’t say, but the fact that it’s even hinting at this makes me wonder if Quin is playing with the idea that faith itself is a kind of violence—one that’s been normalized to the point of invisibility.

But then again, maybe the whole thing is just a setup for a good old-fashioned rebellion. Maybe Igraine’s crisis isn’t about faith at all—it’s about the fact that she’s been lied to, and now she’s going to burn it all down. Or maybe she won’t. The synopsis doesn’t say. And that’s the thing about ambiguity—it’s the literary equivalent of a half-empty glass. You can fill it with whatever you want, but you’ll never know if you’re right until you take a sip.
