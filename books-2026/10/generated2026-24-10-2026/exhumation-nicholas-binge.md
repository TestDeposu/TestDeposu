**Author:** Nicholas Binge  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-12-01 APL

October always brings a crisp, sobering chill to the windowpanes, making a quiet evening indoors feel like an invitation to evaluate what we invest our time into, which naturally brings me to Nicholas Binge’s forthcoming novel, *Exhumation*, a premise that smells faintly of damp concrete and the kind of emotional baggage we all swear we have unpacked but definitely still keep hidden in the back of the closet. We meet Arlo Abbas—a former Metropolitan Police detective who has somehow mastered the art of horizontal living while completely running out of breakfast options—only to have ten thousand crisp British pounds mysteriously deposited into his account alongside a three-word directive to track down a woman who has already been safely lowered into the earth. It is a delightfully cynical setup that trades on the universal fear of unresolved grief, positioning our protagonist not as a heroic sleuth with a gleaming moral compass, but as a fractured human being dragged down into London's subterranean belly by a shadowy syndicate tinkering with the fragile membrane separating slumber from flatlining. Binge clearly wants us to ponder how much of our past we are willing to exhume when a stranger flashes enough cash to pay off three months of rent arrears, yet I find myself wondering if we really need another brooding investigator learning to trust again through a haze of corporate sleep experiments—wait, scratch that, wanting someone to finally communicate their boundaries honestly in a noir thriller is perhaps asking for too much when the genre practically subsists on catastrophic emotional avoidance and men refusing to text back. 

Misleading.

The thematic architecture here leans heavily on the notion that secrets refuse to stay buried, which feels slightly cliché for a late-2026 release attempting to ride the coattails of atmospheric horror giants like T. Kingfisher or Christopher Golden, yet the claustrophobic descent beneath the British capital might just save it from feeling like generic procedural fluff if the prose actually digs into the psychological cost of keeping things unsaid.
