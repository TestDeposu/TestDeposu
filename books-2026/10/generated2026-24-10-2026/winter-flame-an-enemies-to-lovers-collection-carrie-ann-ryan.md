**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-16 APL

Ambassadors are paid to read state treaties drafted with infinitely more deception than Carrie Ann Ryan’s upcoming *Winter Flame: An Enemies to Lovers Collection*. Yet here we are. Staring down a synopsis that reads less like a narrative blueprint and more like a ransom note penned by a disgruntled romance publicist. Loathing. Anger. Wrong choices. One expects the Treaty of Utrecht to carry more narrative momentum. 

### The Protocol of Petty Animosity

Observe the architectural brilliance of the setup. First, rivals. Then, enemies. Before they inevitably tear off their garments in a snowdrift because the author refuses to invent a third emotional gear. It is the literary equivalent of the 1986 Reykjavik Summit—hours of cold posturing before someone caves under the weight of sheer proximity. Carrie Ann Ryan clearly believes that capitalizing on the tropes of three previously detached novellas (*From that Moment*, *Love Restored*, and *My Rebound*) will somehow forge a cohesive geopolitical strategy. But what happens when the snow melts? A total diplomatic collapse. 

### The Pathology of Heat and Fate

The marketing copy leans heavily on the crutch of elemental inevitability. Fate. Heat. As if molecular physics could solve a character arc. The prose promises temptation, yet offers nothing substantive to chew on. One might as well read the menu from a state dinner in Geneva and pretend one has tasted the pheasant. We are expected to swoon over men who presumably brood in leather armchairs while contemplating their tragic pasts. Give me the diplomatic memoirs of Talleyrand any day. At least he understood that true malice requires subtlety, not just a furrowed brow and a stubborn jaw. 

Readers will undoubtedly devour this confection on their private flights to Gstaad, turning the pages with manic desperation while ignoring the turbulence outside. I predict it will sell a million copies to people who buy cashmere throws they never actually drape over their shoulders. And why? Because the modern literary marketplace rewards the predictable cadence of mutual loathing turned into mandatory affection. It is the same exhausting waltz we have watched since Jane Austen put pen to paper, except stripped of wit and dressed in cheap polyester. 

Drop the grand proclamations about fate. Call it what it is—an excuse to put attractive people in enclosed spaces until they exhaust their vocabulary of insults. One shudders to think of the editorial meetings that greenlit this compilation. Did they draw buzzwords from a velvet pouch? One suspects they did. 

Must fly. My former college roommate who is now a senator is waiting at the bistro, and she insists on dissecting the latest ambassadorial scandal over mimosas.
