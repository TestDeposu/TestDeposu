The soil out by the south pasture is heavy this week, caked dry and stubborn beneath a brutal sun that refuses to offer any mercy to the squash vines. Or perhaps the dirt is actually fine, and I am just tired of hauling thirty-pound irrigation hoses across four acres of stubborn clay while people in cities invent problems for paper people. Amanda Chapman wants us to worry about a stolen rare edition of *Murder in the Mews* and a corpse in an alley. Because apparently, the absolute height of human crisis is losing a book about a fictional murder while a fake Hercule Poirot stands around looking vain. 

Give me strength. 

### The Cult of the Nostalgic Artifact

Look at the premise. A book conservator—always a conservator, never someone out here breaking their back mending a broken tractor axle or clearing deadfall—gets dragged into a spectral murder mystery alongside a ghost who looks like Agatha Christie. It is safe. It is aggressively, antiseptically safe. The publishing industry pushes out this cozy fluff because nobody wants to look at the actual rot in the dirt anymore. We prefer our corpses neatly tucked into library alleys where a snarky librarian and a hot detective named Sebastian Mendez-Cruz can sort it all out with a few witty one-liners. 

Actually, wait. Let me walk that back. Maybe the librarian isn't the problem here. Maybe I am just projecting my own hatred of bureaucratic red tape onto a fictional library fifty-year anniversary. 

Chapman relies on the absolute cheapest tricks in the cozy genre toolbox. A flighty cousin. A computer whiz kid. A ghost with a penchant for domestic wit. It reads like a focus-grouped algorithm designed by someone who has never touched a spade or sweated through a linen shirt in July. Richard Osman and Thursday Murder Club clones have ruined a generation of readers who demand their cozy little murders wrapped in twee little bows. We are drowning in ghosts of dead writers because nobody has the courage to write about the actual silence of the land. 

### The Specter of Irrelevance

The dead do not come back to help you find rare editions of books. They stay dead, and the soil swallows them whole. Yet here we are, pretending that a spectral Agatha Christie is going to drop wisdom on a bunch of library patrons standing around in tweed. The thematic undercurrent here is pure escapism of the most cowardly order. When the world is burning—or in my case, when the irrigation pump sputters for the third time in forty-eight hours—we retreat into locked-room mysteries where the stakes are entirely manufactured and easily resolved by page three hundred. 

I predict this book will sell a million copies to people sitting in air-conditioned condominiums who have never smelled manure in their lives. It will top the bestseller lists alongside every other formulaic bit of comfort food churned out by an industry terrified of reality. 

The sun is finally dipping below the tree line, leaving the western sky the color of a bruised peach. I am leaving this keyboard right now, walking into the kitchen, grabbing a very cold beer, and staring blankly at the lawn until I forget anyone ever wrote a sentence about a mews.
