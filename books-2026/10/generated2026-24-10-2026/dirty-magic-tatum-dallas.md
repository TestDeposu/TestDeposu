**Author:** Tatum Dallas  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Dirty Magic* reads like a love letter to the kind of paranormal romance that’s been quietly dominating the genre for years—think *The Love Hypothesis* meets *The Secret History*, but with the kind of sexual tension that makes you want to unplug the Wi-Fi and just *feel* something. The problem? It’s so vague it could be describing a bad Tinder date or a cult revival meeting. Where’s the specificity? The synopsis leans on the kind of broad, atmospheric language that feels like it was written by a committee of people who’ve never actually been to a male revue—let alone one where the performers might be *actual* witches. "Strange things begin to happen" is the literary equivalent of a shrug. It’s the kind of prose that makes you wonder if the author’s ever read *The Turn of the Screw* or if they just Googled "how to make a romance sound mysterious."

Ellie Beckett, the self-proclaimed introvert, is a trope so well-worn it’s practically a brand—like if *Beach Read* and *The Hating Game* had a baby, but the baby was too shy to ever leave the house. The idea that she’d drag herself to a lap dance for her best friends’ bachelorette is fine, but it’s the kind of plot device that feels like it was cobbled together in a focus group. What’s missing? A sense of *why* she’s there. Is she desperate for connection? Is she running from something? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just drops her into the scene like a plot dump from a YA novel where the protagonist’s emotional state is a side effect of the plot, not the other way around. Meanwhile, Ethan Riley is the kind of brooding, morally ambiguous love interest who exists solely to make the heroine question her life choices—because of course he’s the one who makes her skin heat up like she’s been dipped in lava. But is he a villain? A victim? A man who’s just really good at seduction? The synopsis doesn’t tell us. It just lets us assume he’s the kind of guy who’d make her forget her own name.

The snowstorm trope is so overused it’s practically a cliché at this point—like if *The Shining* and *Frozen* had a lovechild and then that lovechild got stuck in a small town during a blizzard. But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t even *try* to make it feel fresh. It just says, "A darkness may have followed Ethan to Mary’s Crossing," which is the kind of vague, ominous language that makes you want to roll your eyes. Is this darkness literal? Metaphorical? A literal demon? A metaphor for his past? The synopsis doesn’t say. It just lets us imagine whatever we want, which is the literary equivalent of serving a buffet with no labels. And yet, despite all this vagueness, the synopsis sells it like it’s the next big thing—because in the world of paranormal romance, a little ambiguity goes a long way. It’s the same reason *The Love Hypothesis* worked: the reader fills in the blanks with their own desires, and suddenly, it’s not just a book—it’s a *feeling*.

But here’s where the synopsis really falls apart: the idea that Ethan and Ellie will have to "decide whether to give in to the passion between them, or risk dancing around their feelings forever" is the kind of cliché that makes you want to scream. It’s the literary equivalent of a bad pickup line—so predictable it’s almost funny. And yet, the synopsis sells it like it’s the next big thing, which is why I can’t help but wonder if this is the kind of book that’s going to be *just* good enough to be forgettable, or if it’s going to be so bad it’s actually *funny*. Because let’s be honest: the only thing more overdone than the snowstorm trope is the "will they/won’t they" romance arc. And yet, despite all this, I can’t help but feel like there’s something here—some kernel of a story that could be *interesting* if it weren’t so wrapped up in its own vagueness.

Yet—here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t *have* to be specific. Sometimes, the best stories are the ones that leave you wanting more, that make you fill in the blanks with your own imagination. And in that sense, *Dirty Magic* might just be the kind of book that works—if it can resist the urge to spell everything out. Because the truth is, the most compelling romances aren’t the ones that tell you everything upfront. They’re the ones that make you *feel* something, that make you wonder what’s going to happen next. And if *Dirty Magic* can deliver on that promise, then maybe it’ll be worth the wait.

But then again, maybe it won’t. Maybe it’ll be the kind of book that leaves you feeling like you’ve been sold a bill of goods, like you’ve been promised a magic show and all you got was a lap dance. And that’s the real question, isn’t it? Can a book live up to its own hype?
