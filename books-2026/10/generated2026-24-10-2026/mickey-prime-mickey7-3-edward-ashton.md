The porch boards are cold beneath my slippers, and the nutmeg floating at the rim of this ceramic mug has gone entirely powdery. Cinnamon and clove do remarkably little to soothe an aching chest when the sunlight is this brittle and gray. 

We look for ourselves in the strangest places. Sometimes in the bottom of a coffee cup. Sometimes in the premise of a science fiction novel about a man who keeps dying and getting printed out again like a defective receipt. 

Edward Ashton clearly understands the recursive loop of loss. *Mickey7* trades on a grotesque kind of recycling, asking what remains of a person once the edges have been trimmed away and the consciousness poured into a fresh mold. Grief does that, you know. It prints out a hollowed-out version of your house, your kitchen, your morning routine, and expects you to inhabit the shell without complaining about the missing furniture. 

### The Arithmetic of Erasure

What strikes me—irritates me, really—is the sheer casualness of disposability. The premise hinges on a bureaucratic nightmare where human life is measured in print cartridges. And yet, the industry insists on marketing this as quirky space-faring comedy. It is not comedy. It is a slow bleed. When Mickey7 walks back into the base to find Mickey8 already warming his bunk, the horror isn't the sci-fi cloning logic. The horror is the absolute interchangeability of a human soul. 

Nobody asked for a trilogy. Or maybe they did, because publishers are terrified of letting a single narrative lie still in the dirt. Look at what they did to *Red Rising*—stretching a tight, angry little dystopian sprint into an endless, bloated parade of galactic misery that reads like a teenager's military history fanfiction. I swear, if I have to read one more brick-thick space opera where characters monologue about honor while planets explode in the background, I am going to throw this mug straight through the screen door. 

Yet Ashton manages a dry, mordant detachment that saves the text from drowning in its own bleakness. Niflheim is a terrible place to die. It is an even worse place to keep waking up. The ice world doesn't care about your grief metrics. It just freezes the blood in your veins and waits for the next iteration to wander out into the snow. 

The synopsis promises corporate dread wrapped in alien curiosity. Commander Marshall is terrified of the natives. She should be. Fear is the only honest reaction left when you realize you are entirely expendable to the people paying your wages. 

And now I have to go untangle the golden retriever's leash from the wrought-iron table legs because he apparently decided to run three laps around the patio furniture while I was meditating on mortality.
