**Author:** Ellie May  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Someone just dropped a stainless-steel fork against a Formica tray three tables over, and I am ready to commit actual felonies over the auditory assault. But my irritation is nothing compared to the violent eye-roll induced by Ellie May’s forthcoming 2026 endeavor, *Scoring Position*. 

Listen. I collect first editions bound in calfskin, resting safely in a climate-controlled study smelling of old tobacco and mahogany. I do not come here—to this cacophonous fluorescent hellscape of microwaved sadness and crying toddlers—to read about another overworked collegiate protagonist trying to escape the bourgeois prison of a "cubicle-gray future." Spare me the proletarian cosplay. Maggie Parker and her desperately engineered crisis scream millennial-pandering fluff designed for TikTok girls who buy iced lattes they cannot afford. 

### The Commodification of Collegiate Cliche

The premise relies on a transactional romance. Groundbreaking. Revolutionary. Truly a paradigm shift in contemporary fiction. Elliot Vance needs an internship at Parker & Klein—because nepotism or sheer desperation dictates that last names must match corporate letterheads in these universes—and Maggie needs a crash course in "normal college life." The transactional dynamic drips with cynical manipulation, yet the synopsis desperately wants us to swoon over their "brainy banter." 

Please. The chemistry is about as organic as a plastic greenhouse. We are looking at a carbon copy of every *Off Campus* knockoff currently flooding the market, just swapping the hockey stick for a baseball bat and praying the readership is too lobotomized by algorithms to notice the lack of prose styling. 

### The Aesthetic of Manufactured Rebellion

Maggie’s father is holding the purse strings, and she wants "technicolor life." Gross. The juxtaposition of corporate authoritarianism against the pristine emerald grass of a collegiate baseball diamond is utterly derivative. May attempts to mask a thoroughly pedestrian plot beneath the guise of "emotional growth." Yet, based strictly on the marketing copy, the narrative trajectory is more predictable than the morning commute on the subway. They will fake date. They will drop some forced baseball superstitions into casual dialogue. They will catch real feelings right before the contract expires. And the internet will lose its collective mind, calling it an absolute masterpiece of emotional devastation. 

Make it make sense. We are regressing into infantile literary consumption where characters are mere vessels for wish-fulfillment and low-stakes domesticity. Elliot is drowning in debt with no pro career prospects, yet he has time to play pretend boyfriend to a rich girl for career advancement? The socioeconomic implications are deeply unserious. But do the masses care about structural realism? Absolutely not. They want the dopamine hit of the slow-burn realization. They want the tropes hammered into their skulls until nothing original remains. 

I would rather chew tin foil than sit through another scene where a brooding athlete with a heart of gold learns how to communicate his feelings through the medium of forced proximity. The genre is rotting from the inside out, and *Scoring Position* looks poised to deliver another fatal blow to sophisticated storytelling. 

And for the record, the oysters sitting in the corner of this wretched office cafeteria are entirely subpar, completely ruining my creative flow.
