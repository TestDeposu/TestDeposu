**Author:** Fiona Warnick  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The first thing that makes this synopsis feel like a fever dream written by someone who’s been reading too much *The Bell Jar* while high on a particularly bitter espresso—because, of course, it’s not just about a crush, is it? It’s about the *idea* of a crush, the *aesthetic* of it, the way Willa’s obsession is less a psychological study and more a performance art piece where the audience is the reader, and the only real performance is her own self-sabotage. The idea that she pursued Alex *to feel like a character*—that’s not just a confession, that’s a manifesto for the kind of woman who treats her life like a bad rom-com draft she’s too stubborn to abandon. And then there’s the kicker: the sex scenes are replaced by *endnotes in sonnets*. Oh, you *brat*.

This isn’t just a novel about unrequited love—it’s a novel about the *illusion* of unrequited love, the way we mythologize our own suffering into something grander than it was. Willa’s postmortem isn’t just an exercise in self-reflection; it’s a *deconstruction*, a way of holding up the wreckage of her own choices and saying, *“Look, I did this for the plot.”* And that’s the real genius—or the real *audacity*—of the premise. It’s not just that she’s writing about her own foolishness; she’s *framing* it as a literary device, which immediately puts her on the same level as the women who’ve been making fools of themselves for centuries—only here, the fool is the one doing the fooling *on purpose*. It’s like if *The Bell Jar* and *Her Smell* had a baby, and that baby was raised by a particularly cynical, rhyming-sonnet-loving version of *The Paris Review*.

But here’s where the synopsis starts to feel like it’s been written by someone who’s never actually *lived* through a situationship. Because what happens when the crush isn’t just unrequited—what happens when it’s *complicated*? What happens when Alex isn’t just a blank slate but a real person with their own agency, their own messiness? The synopsis doesn’t even *hint* at that. It’s all Willa, all the time, and that’s not just lazy—it’s *thematically lazy*. Because if this is supposed to be an ode to queer desire and friendship, where are the *other* characters? Where’s the actual *relationship* beyond the crush? The synopsis reads like a Twitter thread from someone who’s spent too much time in their own head, where the only conflict is the internal one, and the only stakes are the ones Willa assigns herself. It’s the literary equivalent of a TikTok where the whole video is just you staring into the camera and saying, *“This is so deep.”*

And then there’s the *form*. Endnotes in sonnets? That’s not just a gimmick—it’s a *statement*. It’s saying, *“I’m not just writing about sex; I’m writing about the way we *talk* about sex, the way we *perform* it, the way we *erase* it when it doesn’t fit the narrative.”* But it’s also a *risk*. Because if the sonnets aren’t *sharp*, if they’re not *funny*, if they’re not *better* than the prose, then they’re just a gimmick. And if they’re just a gimmick, then the whole book is just a gimmick, which is the last thing you want in a novel that’s supposed to be about *meaning*. It’s like if *Infinite Jest* tried to write itself in memes—it would either be *brilliant* or it would be *painfully obvious*.

The synopsis also does this weird thing where it keeps circling back to the idea that Willa did it for the plot, as if that’s some kind of revelation. But isn’t that just what *all* of us do? Isn’t that the whole point of storytelling? We don’t live our lives for the plot—we *invent* the plot to make our lives feel like they have one. That’s not a deep insight; that’s just the basic premise of *any* novel. So why does it feel like the synopsis is treating it like some kind of *breakthrough*? It’s like if someone wrote a blurb for *Pride and Prejudice* and said, *“Elizabeth Bennet fell in love with Mr. Darcy because she wanted to be the heroine of her own story.”* Yeah, *duh*.

But here’s the thing: if this book *does* pull it off, it could be *something*. It could be the kind of novel that makes you laugh, then makes you think, then makes you *feel* like you’ve been let in on a secret. It could be the kind of book that makes you realize you’ve spent your whole life chasing the same kind of narrative, the same kind of *drama*, and that maybe—just maybe—that’s not a bad thing. But if it doesn’t? If the sonnets are just filler, if the character is just a caricature, if the whole thing is just a really long meta-commentary on how we all act like idiots for the sake of a good story? Then it’s just another book about a woman who can’t let go of a crush, which is a trope so old it’s practically a *cliché*.

I have to go now because I’m expected to bid heavily at a charity auction where the highlight is a first-edition *The Catcher in the Rye* with a note from Salinger’s cat. And honestly? If this book is half as *funny* as the synopsis suggests, I’ll be there, pen in hand, ready to outbid everyone for a copy. But if it’s just another sad little story about a woman who can’t move on?
