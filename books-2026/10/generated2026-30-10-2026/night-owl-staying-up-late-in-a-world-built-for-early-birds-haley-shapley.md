Staring at the glowing orange LED of my desk lamp while the instant ramen water refuses to boil for the fourth time tonight — seriously, my hot plate is an absolute war crime — I am forced to contemplate *Night Owl: Staying Up Late in a World Built for Early Birds* by Haley Shapley. Because apparently, society still needs a literal scientific defense just to justify why some of us function past sunset without turning into brain-rotted goblins. Give me a break. Or rather, give me some goddamn funding for my thesis. 

### The Chronobiological Grievance

The premise hinges on a basic biological reality that academia has ignored in favor of morning-person propaganda for centuries. Shapley’s text looks at how our circadian timing is hardwired into our DNA—not some moral failing or lack of discipline. Groundbreaking stuff. Truly. Next thing you know, researchers will publish a peer-reviewed monograph proving that water is, in fact, wet. Yet, we desperately need this validation. We live under the tyranny of the corporate 9-to-5, a temporal dictatorship designed by people who probably wake up at 5:00 AM to jog in freezing rain for fun. 

### The IQ and Evolution Flex

And look, the synopsis promises a deep dive into how nocturnal humans supposedly possess higher IQs and heightened creativity. Let us hope the author avoids pop-science clichés. If I have to read one more pop-psychology essay claiming every late-night coder is a misunderstood genius instead of just someone procrastinating on their rent, I am going to throw my laptop out the window—which I technically can't do because the landlord keeps the window nailed shut. But the thematic core here hits hard. It tackles systemic temporal discrimination. Yes, temporal discrimination. Because getting side-eyed for logging onto Slack at midnight while your manager praises the drone who logs on at dawn is capitalist gaslighting of the highest order. 

If this book doesn't cite proper chronobiology journals alongside its cultural anecdotes, I will riot. We deserve better than pop-sci fluff. We need rigorous physiological anthropology that dismantles the sunrise fetishism dominating modern productivity culture. Think Matthew Walker’s *Why We Sleep* meets Mary Roach’s biting sociological snark, but entirely fueled by cold brew and existential dread. If Shapley actually delivers on that caliber of critique, she might just liberate the noctonauts from centuries of literary slander. 

Now, if you will excuse me, I am going to go drink absinthe and pretend I am in 1920s Paris.
