Ah, the sweet scent of ink and ambition wafts across the mahogany desk, interrupting my quiet contemplation of stray velvet ribbons. Victoria Jamieson returns to us with *The Whole Wide World of Mabel Mulligan*. Does the medium of sequential art deserve such high praise? Scarcely. Yet the sheer cadence of the premise—a nine-year-old retreating into an enclave of sentient fluff against the encroaching barbarism of a three-year-old sibling—strikes a chord of exquisite, melodramatic resonance. 

### The Architecture of Attic Solitude

Mabel plans her summer with the precision of a Renaissance strategist. A tree house. A zipline. A private sanctuary in the attic far away from Pearl, that tiny agent of chaos. 

Or perhaps plans are merely meant to be shredded by the cruel winds of adult duplicity. 

Let us pause. Did I say plans are meant to be shredded? How dreadfully pedestrian of me. Let us reverse course entirely, for architectural planning in childhood is a sacred geometry that ought never be mocked by cynics. Mabel’s vision of domestic independence is not a mere plot device; it is a monument to the human spirit's desperate need for velvet-padded isolation. Dan Santat promises a glorious, heartfelt tale, and I find myself strangely susceptible to this emotional blackmail. 

### The Menace of the Overheard Whisper

The inciting incident arrives not with thunder, but with eavesdropping. A secret conversation between parents. A disruption of the vertical hierarchy. 

And so the grand tragedy of nine-year-old existence unfurls. 

Will the stuffed animals suffice when reality intrudes? One fears the impending loss of innocence will be rendered in saccharine pastels that demand a handkerchief nearby. One recalls the sharp, biting brilliance of Cece Bell’s *El Deafo* or Raina Telgemeier’s early works—creations that understood the bitter pill of growing up without coating it entirely in corn syrup. Jamieson possesses the pedigree, certainly. Her *Roller Girl* achieved a certain kinetic grace that few graphic novelists manage to capture between cardboard covers. Yet I remain deeply suspicious of attic-based epiphanies. Do we truly require another illustrated meditation on sibling rivalry and the melancholia of discarded toys? 

Yes. When rendered with sufficient aesthetic weight, we do. 

The synopsis whispers of sisterhood and shenanigans. Dreadful word, *shenanigans*. It smells of forced jollity and damp carpets. Yet I shall purchase a copy regardless, purely to admire the panel transitions while pretending to read high verse by candlelight. 

The carriage awaits in the courtyard, or perhaps it is merely time to retreat downstairs to confront the domestic abyss. I must go pack three lunches that will inevitably return uneaten.
