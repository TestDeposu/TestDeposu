**Author:** Shirin Yim Leos  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Of Wind and Dust* is so aggressively *vague* it reads like a corporate mission statement for a historical soap opera—except with the emotional range of a tax form. Three women, one man, decades of oceanic drift, and a plot that somehow involves Seattle? Congratulations, you’ve got the DNA of a *Water for Elephants* meets *The Song of Achilles* but with the narrative ambition of a *Downton Abbey* fanfic written by someone who Googled "polygamy in 19th-century China."

Let’s start with the *characters*—or what we know of them, which is basically a laundry list of victimhood with a side of "historical context." Yook Lan, the "Great Wife" abandoned by her husband, sounds like she was designed to be the emotional anchor of this thing, the kind of character who’ll probably weep into a silk pillowcase while her husband’s mistresses (or whatever they’re called in this setup) get to have the *adventure* of being ransomed. Guai Fong, the "enslaved" woman, is the classic "tragic mulatto" of the historical novel—neither fish nor fowl, just a human-shaped plot device to make the other women feel guilty. And Mary, the "free" woman, is the wild card, the one who’ll presumably get to ride off into the sunset with the husband, because nothing says "redemption arc" like a white woman falling in love with a Chinese man in 1850s Seattle.

The *themes*—oh, the themes. "Duty vs. desire," "belonging vs. freedom," "sacrifice, defiance, love, and loyalty." Christ, if this were a TED Talk, it’d be titled *"How to Feel Deeply About Your Life While Also Being a Slave."* The problem isn’t that these themes are clichéd—it’s that they’re *serving* as the entire plot. There’s no tension here, just a slow-motion train wreck of emotional whiplash, where every character is either waiting for permission to be unhappy or waiting for someone else to die so they can be happy. It’s the literary equivalent of a slow-cooker meal: you know it’s going to be there when you get home, but you also know it’s going to be the same thing you had last week.

And then there’s the *setting*—China, Seattle, the Pacific Ocean. Three locations, zero stakes. This could be *The Last of Us* but with more tea ceremonies and less zombie apocalypse. The synopsis doesn’t even *try* to sell us on why we should care about any of this. Is this a love story? A family saga? A historical epic? A feminist manifesto? A cautionary tale about colonialism? A rom-com? At this point, it’s just a series of *possibilities*, like a menu where every dish is "historical drama" and the sides are "emotional turmoil."

The only thing that gives me any hope is that Shirin Yim Leos is name-dropping her own family history as inspiration. That’s either a sign of authenticity or a desperate attempt to sell this as "based on a true story" when it’s really just "based on a Wikipedia page." Either way, if this book is half as messy as the synopsis, it’ll either be the next *The Night Circus*—or the next *The Guernsey Literary and Potato Peel Pie Society* but with the pacing of a molasses flood.

But here’s the thing—this is the kind of book that gets *praised* for its "richness" and "complexity" by critics who’ve never actually read anything. It’s the literary equivalent of a "slow burn" that never actually burns. And right now, I’m sitting here, trying to write this review, while the subway outside my apartment is making enough noise to drown out the sound of my own cynicism. Maybe I’ll just wait until 2026 and see if anyone actually reads it. Or maybe I’ll just wait for the next *historical novel about three women and one man* to come along and pretend this one never happened.
