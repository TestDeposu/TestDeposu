Another year, another manuscript packaged precisely for suburban kitchen islands where the artisanal sourdough starter bubbling on the counter is the only spiritual discipline left intact. *Ordinary Altars* arrives in 2026 courtesy of Sarah Bessey—a veteran purveyor of the gentle-deconstruction industrial complex—promising us that when the headlines are apocalyptic and your retirement fund is a smoking crater, you should simply look down at your house slippers and call them holy. 

### The Cult of the Mundane Manifesto

The premise relies entirely on a recycled cultural exhaustion. We are tired. The systems are crumbling. The algorithms are eating our frontal lobes. So what is the prescribed salve? Radical mediocrity wrapped in liturgical language. The synopsis leans heavily on the comforting fiction that if you just water your house ferns with enough earnest sincerity, historical collapse somehow pauses out of respect for your emotional authenticity. 

### Marketing the Apocalypse

Let us be entirely serious for a single microsecond. The entire thesis of finding the divine in the dish soap and the laundry pile is a luxury product. Try telling someone working three gig-economy shifts that their sink full of unwashed cutlery is an ordinary altar. They will likely throw a mug at your head. Yet this book will sell three hundred thousand copies before the first quarter ends, neatly slotted onto nightstands right next to the latest Glennon Doyle screed and an unread copy of *Atomic Habits*. 

Actually, scratch that critique entirely. Who am I to mock the desperate need for comfort? The world *is* genuinely on fire, and pretending that irony is an adequate fire extinguisher makes you look like an insufferable jerk while the living room burns. Maybe we *do* need someone to tell us how to love a broken planet without demanding we storm a corporate boardroom first. Despair is cheap. Stubborn hope is exhausting manual labor.

Expect plenty of lyrical hand-wringing about motherhood, spiritual fatigue, and the quiet resilience of folding fitted sheets—because nothing says defying late-stage capitalism quite like wrestling a polyester rectangle into a linen closet. 

I would love to stay and dissect the commodification of domestic holiness further, but I have a high-stakes negotiation with my publisher in twenty minutes where I plan to demand an exorbitant advance for a book consisting entirely of blank pages bound in salvaged leather.
