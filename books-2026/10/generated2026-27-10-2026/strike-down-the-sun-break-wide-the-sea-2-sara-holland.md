**Author:** Sara Holland  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

There is a biting October chill cutting right through the drafty windows of this community hall, reminding me why I refuse to turn on the municipal heating until November out of sheer stubbornness, while I stare down the synopsis for *Strike Down the Sun*. Let us look at what Sara Holland is handing us for the conclusion of her *Break Wide the Sea* duology, because it reads like a checklist torn straight from a marketing department's white-gaze board meeting on profitable YA formulas. We have Annie Fairfax hiding out in the north—classic isolation trope, completely detached from accountability—until a convenient rumor about bloodline curses drags her right back to Kirkrell, which honestly feels like the narrative equivalent of spinning the wheel on a tired fantasy tropes generator just to see what sticks on a Tuesday afternoon. And what about Silas Price? He is out there fighting alongside the finfolk, because apparently mandatory community service takes the form of aquatic warfare these days, only to commit some generic battle blunder that instantly loses him the queen's trust, leading to a mandatory fetch quest for a legendary artifact that everyone and their mother knows is going to tear the world apart just like every other magical shiny object in speculative fiction since Tolkien died. 

Yet, I said earlier this duology had potential. 

Correction. 

It never had potential; it had a premise, which is entirely different and infinitely cheaper. 

The synopsis leans so hard on the enemies-to-reluctant-allies dynamic that you can hear the structural groans of the plot from three blocks away, pitching Annie and Silas against August's unspecified machinations and the heavy, lingering ghost of their own thwarted romance—because heaven forbid characters solve political upheaval without awkwardly rubbing their shared past in each other's faces while the coastal ecosystem collapses around them. People are trying to organize real mutual aid out here in these neighborhoods, fighting actual zoning boards and corporate landlords, yet we are supposed to chew on this prefabricated tension between whalers and finfolk as if it holds some profound sociological mirror to our modern fractures. Give me a break. Holland is leaning heavily into the V.E. Schwab or Holly Black playbook of high-stakes romantic fantasy, but without the teeth to back up the world-building mechanics. 

Predictable.

At least the cover design will probably look nice on a coffee table next to a cooling mug of black coffee, though none of that matters when a single roll of Ilford HP5 plus film has gone up another three dollars this week and I honestly do not know how I am going to afford documenting the upcoming neighborhood council meetings before the winter frost sets in for good.
