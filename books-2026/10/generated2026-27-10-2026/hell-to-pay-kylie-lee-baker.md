**Author:** Kylie Lee Baker  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *Hell to Pay* is the kind of thing that makes you wonder if Kylie Lee Baker has been reading your diary—because it’s *that* specific in its grotesque charm. Three roommates, a demon with a seven-day deadline, and a cast of personal horrors that feel like they were plucked straight from a fever dream. But here’s the thing: the synopsis is so *lean* it’s almost cruel. It doesn’t tell you whether the demon’s sigil is a child’s scribble or a surgical incision, whether the faceless Girl Scout is a hallucination or a neighbor’s cursed pet. It doesn’t say if Leni’s triple reflections are a curse or a gift—or if she even *wants* to poison people, or if that’s just the demon’s way of testing her loyalty. That’s the beauty of it, though. The vagueness is deliberate, like a door left ajar in a haunted house. You’re supposed to bring your own fears in.

And yet—*and yet*—the synopsis leans *so hard* into the "darkly funny" tagline that it risks diluting the dread. Because let’s be honest: if you’re laughing at a demon eating intestines, you’ve already lost. The humor here should feel like a knife twist, not a punchline. Compare it to *The Fisherman* by John Langan, where the horror is so slow and creeping that the laughter is a reflex you can’t suppress until it’s too late. Baker’s work—*Bat Eater*, *Japanese Gothic*—proves she knows how to balance the grotesque with the tender, but this synopsis feels like it’s trying to be *too* clever, like a magician who waves a handkerchief instead of a rabbit. Where’s the weight? Where’s the *cost* of the humor?

Emerson’s severed fingers in his water bottle? Classic. The man in the shadows who likes blood? *So* 2010s folk horror. But the synopsis doesn’t tell you *why* these things matter. Is Emerson’s guilt over the surgical error the reason the demon chose *him*? Is Jasper’s criminal record the price he paid to keep his family together, or is it just another layer of debt in a city that already owes him everything? The synopsis hints at depth, but it doesn’t *dig*. It’s like reading a menu where the dishes are described as "delicious" and "surprising" instead of "a seared scallop with a whisper of smoke and a hint of something you can’t quite name."

And don’t even get me started on the found family trope. Oh, the *audacity*. Like we haven’t seen this before: three misfits, one demon, zero escape. It’s the same structure as *The Only Good Indians* by Stephen Graham Jones, but where Jones makes you *feel* the weight of the bonds between them, Baker’s synopsis just *tells* you they’re "chosen family." Where’s the tension? Where’s the moment when Jasper realizes he’d do *anything* for them—and that anything might include selling his own soul? The synopsis doesn’t even *ask* the question. It just *states* it, like a fact, like a grocery list.

But here’s the thing I love about this: the synopsis doesn’t *need* to explain everything. It doesn’t need to be a roadmap. It just needs to *hurtle* you forward, like a train with no brakes. And in that, it succeeds. Because the best horror isn’t about the details—it’s about the *imagination*. The fact that you’re already imagining Leni’s reflections arguing in the mirror. The fact that you’re already wondering if the Girl Scout is real, or if Emerson’s hallucinating her because his guilt is eating him alive. The fact that you’re already rooting for Jasper, even though you know he’s going to do something stupid.

And that’s the real genius of this synopsis: it doesn’t tell you what to think. It just *shows* you the door, and then it slams it shut behind you. You’re left standing there, breathless, wondering if you’re ready for what’s inside.
