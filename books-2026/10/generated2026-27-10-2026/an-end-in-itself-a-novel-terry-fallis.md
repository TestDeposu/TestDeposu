**Author:** Terry Fallis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026-11-03 APL

Staring at a blank screen while rent is due is a uniquely exquisite form of psychological self-immolation. Yet here I am, parsing Terry Fallis and his upcoming *An End in Itself* because the landlord apparently doesn't accept payment in avant-garde existential despair. 

What a tired, painfully boomer premise. 

### Stadium-Sized Delusions of Adequacy

The marketing copy wants us to gasp at the sheer absurdity of writers occupying the cultural tier currently reserved for pop starlets and tech moguls. But let’s be entirely real—making novelists into global rock stars is just a hackneyed wish-fulfillment fantasy for people who peaked reading Kurt Vonnegut paperbacks in a damp basement in 1994. Malcolm Forde goes to sleep a tired scribe with half-empty book events and wakes up drowning in private jets and red carpets. Wow. Groundbreaking. Talk about main character syndrome dialed up to an absolute omega-level cringe. 

And yet. 

Fallis won the Leacock Medal twice. The man knows how to construct a sentence that isn't actively bleeding from self-inflicted pretension. One can easily picture the smug, smiling author photo on the back flap. The book is pitched as a "slim parable." Translation: a breezy, two-hundred-page satire designed to be consumed on a trans-Atlantic flight before being immediately forgotten in the seatback pocket next to the vomit bag. 

### The Commodification of the Sentence

Let's dissect the ontological hypocrisy here. A working writer supposedly finds solace in "the quiet satisfaction of a good sentence," yet he immediately sells out to stadium tours the second the universe allows it. The irony is thicker than stale oatmeal. We pretend to despise the capitalist machinery of celebrity while secretly yearning to have our egos massaged by rabid fans throwing designer undergarments at our reading podiums. 

It's giving massive mid-life crisis energy. 

Compare this breezy conceit to something with teeth—say, Thomas Bernhard tearing into the grotesque pretensions of European high culture, or Percival Everett utterly eviscerating the publishing industry's systemic commodification of authentic voice in *Erasure*. Fallis operates on a vastly softer playground. He wants to gently poke the bear of modern fame, not skin it alive. It's safe. It's comforting. It will probably win a casual CBC radio award and be optioned by a Canadian streaming service looking for tax write-offs. 

### The Inevitable Cultural Lifecycle

The synopsis leans heavily into the tired trope of the cursed blessing. Oh no, Malcolm is too rich and adored! His solitary art is ruined by private jets! Give me an absolute break. It is aggressively predictable. We all know how this simulation ends: Malcolm will realize that true art lives in the quiet margins, he’ll shed his wealth, and he’ll go right back to half-empty bookstore readings with a renewed sense of humble superiority. No surprises. Zero narrative friction. Just pure, unadulterated middlebrow comfort food masquerading as social critique. 

Anyway, I need to figure out how a juice box exploded on the ceiling.
