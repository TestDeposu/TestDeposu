**Author:** Ally Wilkes  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Minerva Sandford is dragging us back to 1901, and frankly, I am already exhausted by the sheer audacity of botanical horror tropes. *Man Eater* promises the usual colonial-era malaise mixed with some vegetative grotesquerie, but I am praying the biology actually holds up. If the orchids are just generic tentacle-monsters with a side of Victorian repression, I’m going to lose my mind. Botany is not just "spooky vines" appearing in the Amazon; it is a brutal, hyper-competitive struggle for light and nitrogen. If Wilkes doesn't address the actual physiological adaptations of epiphytic flora—or at least pretend to care about the taxonomic nightmare of early 20th-century plant hunting—the whole thing feels like a lazy grab at the *Mexican Gothic* aesthetic.

### Vegetative Vengeance and Patriarchy

The premise leans hard into the "feminist Gothic" label, which is modern publishing shorthand for a woman getting mad at her husband while the environment reacts to her internal state. Groundbreaking. Or maybe it’s just tired. Minerva wants autonomy, and nature is hungry; naturally, these two forces will collide in a metaphorical slurry of reproductive horror. It is classic, sure. But is it smart? I am bored to tears by the implication that the jungle serves as a mere mirror for a woman’s "untold dangers from within." Give me a parasite that actually acts like a parasite, not just a metaphor for a shitty marriage. If the ghost woman in green turns out to be a projection of Minerva’s womb-trauma, I’m putting the book in the microwave.

### The Problem with Uncharted Tropes

Wilkes has a history with *All the White Spaces*, and she understands atmospheric dread better than most. The synopsis is frustratingly vague—ghostly women, impossible artifacts, moving vines. It’s a laundry list of "spooky" ingredients without a hint of the mechanism behind the madness. Is there a fungal component? Viral? If you’re going to write about the rubber industry’s encroachment into the wilderness, deal with the actual chemistry of latex extraction or the socioeconomic rot of the era. Don't just slap a "Man Eater" label on a plant and call it a day. I suspect this will be a high-gloss, low-substance affair—very atmospheric, very wet, very destined to be a BookTok darling—but missing the rigorous, nasty edge that made Jeff VanderMeer’s *Annihilation* actually unsettling.

It is undoubtedly going to be a bestseller, likely sitting on the same shelf as the latest Silvia Moreno-Garcia, feeding the current obsession with women reclaiming agency through supernatural violence. I just hope the "Man Eater" is scientifically fascinating rather than just a narrative device to punish the men. I’m tired of reading books that treat nature as a metaphor. Sometimes a plant is just an organism that wants to eat you. That’s scary enough. Now, I’m going to sit on a park bench and aggressively people-watch.
