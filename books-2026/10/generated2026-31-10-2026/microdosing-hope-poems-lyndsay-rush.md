**Author:** Lyndsay Rush  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

My seventh espresso of the morning is currently dissolving the lining of my stomach. And yet, here I am, typing out words about yet another poetry collection designed to comfort us while the structural integrity of modern civilization quietly collapses. Lyndsay Rush—the mastermind behind the handle `@maryoliversdrunkcousin`—is back with *Microdosing Hope*. Or she will be in 2026. Because apparently, we need advance marketing for our impending doom. 

The synopsis tells us everything and nothing. Mostly, it leans hard into the millennial coping mechanism of self-deprecating humor wrapped in pastel nihilism. Think Kate Baer meets Kate Kennedy, but with a sharper caffeine dependency. 

### The Anatomy of a Doom-Scroll Antidote

What are we actually looking at here? A series of poems with titles like “I Feel God in This Chili’s Tonight” and “As a Little Girl I Always Dreamed of Collaborating in Google Docs.” I actually take back my skepticism—those titles are objectively brilliant. They capture the specific, hollow-eyed absurdity of existing as a creative professional in an era where an algorithm is trying to steal your commission while rent goes up another twenty percent. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that. I shouldn't dismiss the humor. Actually, scratch that, humor is a pathetic defense mechanism. No, wait. Humor is the *only* thing keeping us from leaping off the nearest overpass. Let's go with the defense mechanism angle. 

The synopsis asks the heavy hitters. *How do we keep breaking news from breaking our spirit?* *What do we owe each other?* These are exhausting questions. Real art doesn't ask them; it just bleeds onto the page and leaves the mess for the cleaning crew. But Rush isn't writing high tragedy. She’s writing for the cynics and the idealists who are too tired to riot but still care enough to buy a paperback with a clever, irony-poisoned title. 

### Surviving the Apocalypse in Helvetica

The thematic core here is transparent. Nontoxic positivity. Whimsiness as armor. We are dealing with parenting during the apocalypse and aging ungracefully—topics that usually breed dreadful, earnest manifestos. Rush allegedly skips the earnestness entirely. She uses wit like a blunt instrument. 

Actually, I might be projecting. The synopsis is vague enough to fit any projected angst you bring to the table. That is the genius of modern confessional verse. It acts as an Rorschach test for people who spend too much time on their phones. 

Will this book save us? Absolutely not. Will it distract you for forty-five minutes while you pretend to work? Probably. And frankly, in this economy, that counts as a miracle. 

My brush is drying out and I need to finish staring at this aggressively bleak self-portrait before the light completely dies in the studio.
