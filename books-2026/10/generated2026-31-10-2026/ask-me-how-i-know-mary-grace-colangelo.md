The gray drizzle of October is slicking the kitchen windowpane, blurring the backyard maples that refuse to drop their stubbornly stubborn amber leaves. God, I am tired. Not just the bone-deep weariness of folding mountains of laundry while orchestrating three separate school carpools in the dark, but the specific, heavy exhaustion of watching people grapple with the messy architecture of familial duty. 

### The Weight of Unwashed Grief

Mary Grace Colangelo’s upcoming *Ask Me How I Know* lands squarely in that brutal literary tradition where five grieving sisters are forced into a pressure cooker by the death of a patriarch. And honestly? I am ready to throw my lukewarm mug of chamomile tea across the room at the mere concept of another multi-perspective family saga blaming everything on a distant mother. Evelyn sounds like a piece of work—withdrawing into her church volunteerism while leaving the girls to raise themselves. But are we really doing the emotionally stunted matriarch trope again in 2026? It feels as tired as these rain-streaked windows. Give me a protagonist who actively burns down the family homestead instead of dutifully taking on the burden of a moody mother they secretly resent.

### Daughters on the Verge of a Breakdown

Every single one of these Carpenter sisters is ticking like a cheap Timex. Morgan is having a cliché affair with a married co-worker—because of course she is. Caroline's perfectly coiffed existence is crumbling because she dared to want control. Beth is thrust into the political spotlight. Jillian is marrying a man who feels like a brother—Freud would have a field day with that one—and Ashlyn is sitting on a terrible secret just waiting to burst forth before she becomes a mother herself. 

And speaking of children keeping secrets, my youngest just confessed she hid a moldy clementine at the very back of the pantry since last Tuesday, and the resulting smell is frankly more traumatic than any generational trauma these fictional women are whining about. Why is nobody simply eating a crust of dry toast and moving on with their Tuesday? 

Colangelo better bring some serious stylistic teeth to this debut, or it risks drowning in the exact kind of domestic melodrama that makes you want to lock the bathroom door and scream into a hand towel. Yet—there is a grim, hypnotic curiosity pulling me toward it. We read these books to see if our own cracked foundations are normal. 

I need to go scrape mysterious sticky residue off the kitchen island.
