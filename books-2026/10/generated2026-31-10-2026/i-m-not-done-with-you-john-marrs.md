The hearth is burning down to gray embers this afternoon, and my joints ache with that familiar winter dampness that makes you stare out the window for hours, watching the bare oak branches scratch against a gray sky like old parchment. Remember when stories used to be carved into stone or passed around a fire until everyone knew the cadence by heart? Now we get John Marrs dreaming up digital age parables where state secrets are shoved inside human flesh like contraband at a border crossing. *I'm Not Done With You* trades the oral tradition for a thumb-drive biology experiment. 

Yet the premise is delightfully unhinged. Take the most vulnerable state secrets—the dirt on political scandals, the whispers about royalty—and turn five random citizens into living flesh-drives by encoding data into their DNA. It reminds me of the absolute garbage television they put on for the little ones these days, where cartoon puppies have magic jetpacks that defy every law of physics just to retrieve a lost bone, except instead of a cartoon bone, it's the truth about Lady Di's final hours stitched directly into someone's genetic code. My grandson sat there yesterday staring blankly at a glowing rectangle while some neon-colored feline solved quantum physics with a fish biscuit, and I thought, lord above, we are raising a generation that thinks everything can be solved in twenty-two minutes with a catchy moral song. Marrs offers no such comfort.

### The Meat-Puppet Server Farm

Information is the only currency left that truly matters. But storing it in bone and blood? That is a level of biological paranoia that makes George Orwell look like a cheerful optimist. And the rule is deliciously cruel: these five walking hard drives must never cross paths. Naturally, they are going to find each other. Paranoia breeds obsession. Marrs is fantastic at writing characters who smile politely while plotting your slow, agonizing demise—think of the psychological claustrophobia in *The One*, scaled up to a geopolitical disaster. 

And everyone has an agenda. You cannot trust the government that put the code there, and you certainly cannot trust the person sitting across from you on the tube who happens to be carrying the missing half of a royal indiscretion. 

### The Cost of Living Off the Grid

What interests me beneath the high-concept thriller sheen is the utter indignity of ownership. We spend our lives renting our data to invisible corporations, but selling your actual biology to keep the British cabinet out of a scandal? That is a profound degradation wrapped in a financial bailout. You get a new face, a new name, and a bank account—all while carrying the political equivalent of a live grenade in your DNA strand. 

I predict this will either be a masterclass in claustrophobic tension or a complete narrative car crash where the premise collapses under the sheer weight of its own ridiculous logistics. There is no middle ground with Marrs. You either buy into the absurdity or you roll your eyes at page fifty. Personally, I am leaning toward the chaos. 

The light is fading fast across the pasture now, and the tea has gone entirely cold in the mug beside my chair. I really must haul myself up and chop some firewood before the winter chill truly sets in.
