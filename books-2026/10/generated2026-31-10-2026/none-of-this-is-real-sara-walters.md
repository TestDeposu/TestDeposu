**Author:** Sara Walters  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The synopsis for *None of This Is Real* is a masterclass in how to sell a ghost story without actually telling you what the ghost is—because the real horror isn’t the supernatural, it’s the way the past *refuses to die*. Walters, who’s already proven she can turn domestic dread into something visceral (*The Violent Season*), is leaning into the kind of psychological unraveling that feels like standing in a room where the walls are breathing. The note in Roe’s locker isn’t just a threat; it’s a *diagnosis*—a reminder that some traumas don’t just follow you, they *infect* you, rewriting your identity like a virus. And the fact that the family changed their name? That’s not running; that’s a desperate attempt to outsmart something that doesn’t operate on logic. It’s the same logic behind *The Babadook*, where the monster isn’t just in the house—it’s in the way grief distorts perception.

But here’s where the synopsis fails: it doesn’t tell us *how* the darkness manifests. Is it literal? A ghost? A hallucination? A collective trauma that’s been passed down like a curse? The ambiguity is deliberate, but it also risks feeling like a gimmick if Walters doesn’t ground it in something *specific*. Compare this to *Mexican Gothic*—where the horror is as much about systemic neglect as it is about the supernatural—and you realize the difference between a story that *feels* haunted and one that *is* haunted. The synopsis hints at a descent into paranoia, but does Roe’s investigation lead her to uncover a conspiracy, or is the real horror that she’s already complicit in the secrets she’s trying to expose? That’s the question that needs answering, because right now, it’s just a setup for a haunted house trope with a twist.

And speaking of tropes—why does every damn book about a family fleeing their past have to involve a *massacre*? It’s like the publishing industry has a rule: “If you want to sell a ghost story, make the inciting incident a school shooting.” It’s exhausting. I was just reading *The Last House on Needless Street* and its entire premise was built on a family’s guilt over a death, but at least that one had the decency to make the horror feel *earned*. This synopsis, meanwhile, leans into the shock value of a brother’s triple homicide like it’s a gimmick, not a character beat. But then again, maybe that’s the point—maybe the horror isn’t the shooting, it’s the way the family’s attempt to outrun it only makes it worse. That’s where the real tension should lie.

Yet—here’s the thing—if Walters can pull off the kind of slow-burn dread that makes you question every shadow, every silence, then this could be something. The synopsis doesn’t give us enough to judge, but it does give us enough to *want*. The fact that the threat is tied to Roe’s *name*—that it’s not just about the past, but about the way identity is tied to trauma—is a smart move. It’s the same logic behind *The Only Living Boy in New York*, where the protagonist’s name becomes a weapon. But does Walters have the chops to make it more than just a clever hook? Or will it feel like another book where the supernatural is just a stand-in for the author’s own anxiety about family secrets?

I don’t know. But I do know that the synopsis is so vague it could be about anything—ghosts, gaslighting, a family business cover-up. And that’s not necessarily a bad thing. Sometimes the best horror stories are the ones that refuse to spell things out, that let the reader piece together the horror themselves. But then again, sometimes they’re just lazy. And if this is lazy, it’ll feel like another book where the scares are just a way to avoid actually *writing* about the real terror: the way people lie to themselves to survive.

Literally.
