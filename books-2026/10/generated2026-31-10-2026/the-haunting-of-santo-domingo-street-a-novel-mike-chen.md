Mike Chen wants us to believe that immortality comes with an allergy to bats and a mandatory night job. *The Haunting of Santo Domingo Street* leans heavily into the domesticity of the undead—blood bags in the fridge, the quiet ache of alienation, and the eternal curse of rent. Or mortgage payments. Honestly, the synopsis remains wonderfully vague about the real estate logistics of ancient bloodsuckers. 

And yet, I find myself staring at the water stain on the ceiling, wondering why every speculative writer feels the urgent need to make monsters relatable. 

Vampires used to be terrifying. They were aristocrats of decay, draped in velvet and existential dread. Now? They are just underpaid freelancers who happen to burn in direct sunlight. Louise Chao just wants to play punk rock chords in a damp garage. Who hasn't felt that exact brand of pathetic longing? 

Wait. Let me rephrase that. Perhaps stripping away the gothic grandeur is precisely the point. Chen isn't writing horror. He is writing about the crushing weight of middle management eternity. 

Or maybe he isn't. Truth be told, I am typing this at three in the morning while my neighbor argues with a toaster, desperately trying to stretch these observations into five hundred words so the invoice clears by Tuesday. It is an honest living. Sort of. 

### Notes on the Margin of Eternity

The addition of a long-lost teenage relative named Ian introduces the classic tropes of forced kinship. The stubborn elder meets the abrasive youth. Sparks fly. Guilt surfaces. Booklist promises a "strong emotional core," which usually translates to characters crying into their lukewarm synthetic blood. 

Yet, I take it back. The punk rock angle saves it from standard family-drama tedium. Or perhaps it makes it worse. There is a distinct flavor of adolescent rebellion that sours when the rebel has been dodging the sun since the Reagan administration. If Ian asks Louise for the "ultimate favor," I sincerely hope it involves blowing up a municipal tax office rather than curing her emotional baggage. We have enough books about healing from old wounds. Give us a book about ripping the stitches out entirely. 

Fans of Nick Harkaway or maybe even early Grady Hendrix will find familiar ground here. Chen balances humor with melancholy remarkably well. That's what the starred reviews say, anyway. I wouldn't know. I am merely a vessel for caffeine and deadlines, reacting to a press release while the radiator hisses its Morse code of impending winter. 

The narrative hinges on a secret that threatens the entire vampire hierarchy. A high-stakes mystery tethered to a garage band aesthetic. A juxtaposition that either works brilliantly or collapses under its own ironic weight. 

I need to close this document now because I just heard thunder, and I completely forgot to bring my vintage Peugeot bicycle inside from the balcony.
