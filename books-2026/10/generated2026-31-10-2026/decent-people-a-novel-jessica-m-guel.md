The dashboard clock is stuck at 2:44, and the minivan smells faintly of stale cheddar popcorn and wet athletic socks while I stare at the synopsis for Jessica M. Guel’s *Decent People* and try to figure out if we are actually ready for another multi-generational border saga that leans heavily on the ghosts of the Mexican Revolution to explain why teenage girls in Dallas cannot catch a break. We get it, history is an anchor, and patriarchy is an unbroken chain spanning centuries—trust me, I grade essays about cyclical trauma every single Tuesday morning before my coffee has even cooled down—yet the publisher's pitch relies so heavily on the ghosts of soldaderas riding with Pancho Villa that I am instantly exhausted by the sheer pedagogical weight being dropped onto this poor protagonist named Martha, who just wants to finish high school without everyone staring at her body while she maps out her future potential. 

Actually, wait. Let me back up. 

Maybe the historical framing isn't just a heavy-handed framing device. Maybe Guel actually manages to weave Mariana, Imelda, and Ana into the narrative without turning the whole book into a history syllabus disguised as fiction, unlike half the historical fiction currently dominating the bestseller lists alongside writers like Isabel Allende or Ana Castillo who walked so these modern generational epics could aggressively run. 

Unearned. 

### The Burden of Inheritance

The tension between a girl wanting to architect her own life and a century of systemic rot snapping at her heels feels painfully real to anyone who watches kids try to survive the halls of a Texas public school every single day. 

Except the synopsis makes the men—specifically this childhood friend Omar—sound entirely too self-aware about their own complicity, as if a teenage boy in the nineties or early aughts is going to suddenly grasp the full weight of systemic misogyny just because his bookish friend refuses to let him distract her from her GPA. 

Unrealistic. 

### Mapping the Borderlands

The geography here is doing a lot of heavy lifting. Spanning East Dallas and Mexico across a hundred years gives the prose plenty of room to breathe, or choke, depending entirely on whether Guel trusts her readers to pick up on the thematic subtext without a neon sign flashing words like entitlement and quiet violence. 

Stop honking the silver SUV behind me. I am literally trying to think.
