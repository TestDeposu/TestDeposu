**Page Count:** Unknown

There is something genuinely exhausting about fantasy characters who think emotional self-immolation is the highest form of devotion, as though refusing to communicate saves anyone from the pyre. In *God of Thieves*, Katie Shepard brings us to the closing act of *The Younger Gods*, and based strictly on the publisher's description, I am bracing myself for a masterclass in martyrdom disguised as romance. We get Taran, who would apparently watch the entire immortal hierarchy dissolve into brine rather than let Iona scratch a knee in combat, and Iona, who promptly marches herself straight into the Underworld as a willing hostage because she feels guilty about getting married. Seriously. They survived the bloody rebellion, chose each other over cosmic duty, and immediately decided that carrying secrets and making unilateral sacrifices for the other's own good is the best way to honor a partnership. 

Healthy communication? Never heard of her. Apparently, true love means locking yourself in Death's citadel so your husband doesn't have to worry his pretty immortal head while you suffer alone. I mean, my own partner and I had a twenty-minute domestic dispute yesterday morning simply because he used the wrong skillet for the omelet eggs—which, frankly, is a mortal offense that nearly fractured our foundations—and yet we managed to hash it out over fresh coffee without either of us dramatically surrendering to a skeletal deity in an Underworld fortress. It is wild to watch fictional couples invent entirely new stratagems of isolation just to keep the angst engine running at maximum capacity when a simple five-minute conversation could clear up the whole divine mess. 

### The Architecture of Stubborn Devotion

And yet, I will be pre-ordering this the second I can find an open link because I am endlessly weak for the particular brand of cosmic yearning where two idiots burn down the heavens just to hold hands in the ashes. 

Tension.

The synopsis leans heavily on the friction between public duty and private obsession—a classic trope that Sarah J. Maas and Jennifer L. Armentrout love to exploit until the paper shreds. 

Except here, the stakes are delightfully messy. Taran is left holding the bag for a corrupt pantheon he actively helped dismantle, while Iona plays chess with literal Death just to reclaim her ideological purity. 

I just hope the narrative lets them actually talk instead of suffering in heroic silence for three hundred pages. 

Because mutual pining is fun, but mutual vulnerability is how adults build something that lasts. 

Or maybe I am just projecting my deep-seated allergic reaction to emotional stubbornness onto characters who literally live in a rotting mythological ecosystem. 

Whatever. 

I am currently operating on three hours of sleep and pure espresso.
