**Author:** Patricia Cornwell  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

Another billionaire couple bites the dust. Or so the blurb for *Rapid and Fatal* breathlessly insists, as if my morning espresso needs the extra seasoning of corporate manslaughter by insect. Patricia Cornwell is back at it, dragging our favorite Virginia medical examiner into another convoluted dance with a microscopic menace. 

### The Art of the Allergy

Let us examine the premise. Two founders of an elite security firm—because apparently regular wealth lacks sufficient narrative tension—are found dead in their mansion. The murder weapon? Yellow jackets. A biological assassination by stinging insect. How wonderfully quaint. It reads like Joe Abercrombie got too high and decided to write a procedural about suburban gardening. And yet, there are no signs of foul play. Naturally. Because a multi-million-dollar security apparatus apparently fails the moment someone brings a spicy bee into the foyer. 

Dr. Kay Scarpetta steps in, as she always does, weaponizing her microscope against the infinite void of human depravity. But perhaps we are looking at this all wrong. The synopsis tries desperately to convince us that this is a sophisticated, undetectable crime—the work of a true monster. Yet, the perpetrator chose *bees*. 

### Lethal Hymenoptera and Other Absurdities

Actually, scrap that entirely. The notion of a sophisticated criminal relying on anaphylaxis feels distinctly mid-tier, a lazy contrivance cooked up on a private yacht between gulps of vintage burgundy. 

What we have here is a quintessential exercise in high-society ennui. The rich die. The doctor looks grave. Everyone acts shocked that money cannot bribe an allergic reaction. Cornwell is leaning heavily into the grimdark of the upper crust, where even an afternoon on the patio carries the grim prospect of sudden, thorax-driven doom. It is utterly devoid of joy. Which is precisely why I will buy it on hardcover and toss it into the infinity pool when I reach page fifty. We crave devastation, after all. We want our fictional elites to suffer baroque demises just to feel a fleeting pulse beneath our own tailored cashmere. 

Give me more bloodless poisons and less backyard fauna. Or better yet, give me an ending where the bees form a union and sue the estate for emotional damages. 

My youngest has just discovered the permanent markers.
