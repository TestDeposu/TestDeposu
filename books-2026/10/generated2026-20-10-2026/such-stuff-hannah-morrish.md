The hum of the overhead fluorescent tube has reached a rather belligerent frequency. Outside, the rain insists on drumming against the reinforced glass of this civic sanctuary—an architectural triumph of the late seventies designed exclusively to make taxpayers feel thoroughly investigated. But let us ignore the damp chill seeping through the tweed. Let us direct our focus toward Hannah Morrish’s forthcoming *Such Stuff*. 

### The Geometry of Pretense

We are promised an hour. Sixty minutes of theatrical purgatory before an actor assumes the skin of Miranda. The synopsis trades heavily in the currency of psychological collapse, setting its stakes in the narrow corridor between personal grief and professional mimicry. On paper, it sounds like a Tuesday at the Hague. 

Wait. Let me rephrase that entirely. Actually, it sounds more like the third act of *The Seagull* directed by someone who just discovered the concept of somatic trauma on a podcast. 

The premise rests on an excruciatingly neat thematic parallel: the grieving daughter playing a grieving daughter. Or rather, a daughter to a charismatic patriarch stepping into the shoes of Prospero’s progeny. It is almost too symmetrical. Real life rarely respects such tidy dramaturgy. Real life is messier, characterized by the sudden loss of an encrypted diplomatic cable or an unfortunate incident involving a stray dachshund at a state dinner. Morrish—or at least the press release that heralds her arrival—wants us to believe in the absolute friction between art and biography. 

Yet, I suspect the book will avoid the trap of sentimentality. The brief word count—a "short, hypnotic work"—suggests discipline. One hopes for the economic precision of Penelope Fitzgerald rather than the bloated naval-gazing typical of modern debut fiction trying desperately to secure a film option before the galley proofs are even dry. Think of the tragic pacing of the 1985 Heysel Stadium disaster; everything compressed into a terrifying, unyielding window where choices evaporate. That is the kind of pressure this narrative requires to justify its own existence. 

### The Burden of the Boards

The theater has always been a rather pathetic asylum for people who refuse to negotiate with reality. To write a novel about an actor preparing for *The Tempest* is to flirt dangerously with theatrical cliché. Every actor thinks they are the first person to discover that playing a role involves masking personal trauma. It is the literary equivalent of a minor European minister publishing memoirs titled *The Weight of State*. 

And yet. 

The collision of the calendar—anniversary of death meeting the anniversary of birth—adds a sharp, cruel edge to the backstage clockwork. Time refuses to remain compartmentalized. It bleeds through the greasepaint. 

I must depart before the drizzle turns to sleet. There is an obscure chapbook launch for an Estonian dissident in a damp basement off Atlantic Avenue, and my presence has been demanded by someone whose favor I cannot afford to lose just yet.
