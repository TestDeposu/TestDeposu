**Author:** Jess Kidd  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Expected publication 2026 APL

The marsh mist clings to the edges of the page like a half-remembered dream—thick, cloying, the kind that makes your teeth ache if you breathe too deep. Jess Kidd’s *Little Spark* isn’t just a gothic novel; it’s a wet, electric shock to the system, the kind that leaves you wondering if you’ve been standing too close to a live wire. The synopsis alone is a masterclass in controlled ambiguity, the kind of prose that makes you suspect the author is playing a long game with the reader’s expectations—like a magician who never lets you see the trick, only the *feeling* of being fooled.

Bodkin Bell is the kind of protagonist who feels like she’s been plucked from a Victorian penny dreadful, but with the jagged edges of a modern antihero. Orphaned, electrified, and burdened with a past that’s been sewn shut with lies—she’s the literary equivalent of a half-burned letter, the kind you can still read if you squint just right. The fact that she’s framed as both victim and survivor in the same breath is *chef’s kiss*—Kidd knows how to walk the tightrope between pathos and menace. But here’s the thing: the synopsis doesn’t *tell* you what makes her dangerous. It *shows* you the sparks flying from her fingertips, the way gaslights flicker at her approach. That’s the kind of visceral storytelling that makes you lean in, fingers curled around the spine like you’re afraid she’ll vanish if you let go.

And then there’s the automata. Oh, the automata. Kidd’s got a knack for turning mechanical marvels into something far more unsettling than mere spectacle. Think *Frankenstein* meets *The Cabinet of Curiosities*, but with the psychological weight of a woman who’s spent her life being *displayed* rather than *seen*. The idea of Bodkin being lured into a house of wonders—only to find the wonders are *hers*—is the kind of twist that makes you suspect Kidd’s been reading too much Lovecraft by candlelight. But where Lovecraft’s horrors are cosmic, Kidd’s are intimate, personal. The marsh isn’t just a setting; it’s a character, a silent witness to the rot beneath the surface. And the family of inventors? They’re the kind of people who’d sell their own soul for a patent, if they had one to sell.

The vagueness here is *deliberate*—and that’s both the book’s greatest strength and its most frustrating flaw. Kidd leaves too many doors ajar, too many secrets half-buried. It’s the literary equivalent of a gothic mansion with every window boarded up except one, just enough to make you wonder what’s lurking in the dark. But then again, maybe that’s the point. Maybe the truth isn’t meant to be found, only *felt*. And if that’s the case, then *Little Spark* is already winning the game before it’s even begun.

There’s a trend right now—one that’s been simmering for years—of gothic fiction that’s less about castles and more about *people*. Books like *The Essex Serpent* and *The Night Circus* prove that the genre doesn’t need to be dusty and dead; it just needs to be *alive*. Kidd’s work sits squarely in that camp, but with a sharper edge. Where others might go for atmosphere, she goes for *electricity*—both literal and metaphorical. And if the rest of the book lives up to the promise of its synopsis, it’ll be the kind of read that leaves you humming with static, your fingers twitching like they’ve been plugged into a live circuit.

But here’s the thing no one’s talking about: the automata aren’t just machines. They’re *replacements*. And Bodkin? She’s the original, the one they tried to unmake. That’s the kind of psychological depth that makes you suspect Kidd’s been reading too much Freud by the glow of a flickering gas lamp. And if she’s half as clever with her prose as she is with her themes, *Little Spark* won’t just be a book. It’ll be an *experience*—the kind that lingers like the taste of burnt sugar on your tongue.

My home office has been invaded by action figures. Not the usual plastic junk—these are the kind that move when no one’s looking, the kind that whisper secrets in the dark. And if I had to bet, I’d say they’re here to tell me *Little Spark* is going to be the next big thing. But then again, maybe they’re just trying to sell me a new desk. Either way, I’m not touching them.
