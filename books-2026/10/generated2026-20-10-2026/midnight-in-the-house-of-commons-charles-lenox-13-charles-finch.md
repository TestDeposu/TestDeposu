Autumn air smells faintly of damp wool and roasted chestnuts outside the window, while inside, the radiator clanks with a menace that suggests another long winter of overpriced flat whites. Yet here we are, staring down the barrel of yet another Victorian procedural where the dead body is conveniently male and politically connected. *Midnight in the House of Commons* arrives with all the predictability of falling leaves. Charles Finch knows his sandbox. He builds neat little castles of gaslit nostalgia for a readership that presumably finds modern headlines too distressing. But let us look past the velvet drapes and the clinking champagne glasses at what is actually happening in the margins. A dead politician. Naturally, the blame crawls toward the women. His wife. His mistress. Because in the nineteenth century, female survival apparently required an active hand in poison management—or so historical fiction insists. 

And what a tired little trope. 

Wait. Let me be entirely fair to Finch for a fleeting second. 

Perhaps giving the women agency—even murderous agency—is a subversive nod to the claustrophobia of Victorian domesticity. Or perhaps it is just lazy plotting draped in silk. 

### The Parlor Game of Power

We have seen this precise blueprint a hundred times before. Think of Anne Perry’s gaslit moralizing, or the polite, bloodless murders of a thousand cozy mysteries that comfort readers who prefer their historical oppression sanitized. The synopsis practically begs us to admire the scenery. Ballrooms! Palaces! The sacred halls of Parliament! Because nothing screams dramatic tension quite like an aristocratic property dispute mixed with a dash of arsenic. The men hold the power. The women orbit them like anxious moths until someone drinks the wrong vintage of claret. Lenox himself is caught between solving the murder and managing the domestic friction of his ward Sari's teenage heartbreak. Groundbreaking stakes. Truly, the empire teeters. 

Except the real emotional gravity here—if the synopsis has the courage to sustain it—lies not in the stiff collar of the House of Commons, but in the domestic sphere where women are expected to quietly endure. A missing fiancé. A heartbroken ward. These are not mere subplots to pad out the page count until the big reveal in the library; they are the actual architecture of female captivity. When someone close to Lenox gets arrested at the climax, the narrative stakes suddenly shift from abstract political intrigue to personal peril. How terribly inconvenient for the gentleman detective. 

Expect the usual clever deduction. Expect the satisfying click of a closed case before the final page. But do not expect structural critique. Finch writes for the comforting cadence of the past, not its brutal truths. My favorite indie cafe has replaced their scarred wooden tables with sterile marble.
