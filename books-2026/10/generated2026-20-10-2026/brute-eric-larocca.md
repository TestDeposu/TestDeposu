Sit closer to the hearth, little one; the fire is dying down, but I have a strange tale to whisper before you run off to play in the dust. The world outside is cold and very old, yet sometimes a modern voice cuts through the wind like a jagged piece of flint. Eric LaRocca is sharpening that flint again for his upcoming novel, *Brute*, a book that promises to drag our quiet domestic horrors screaming into the digital age. 

### The Architecture of a Broken Mirror

We are told to expect a collision of *The Shining* and *The Truman Show* viewed entirely through the distorted lens of modern technology. Splatterpunk meets existential dread. And yet, the synopsis gives us so terribly little to hold onto, forcing us to squint into the fog to see the shape of the beast. Ephraim Mayerling stands in his all-black attire, a mortician of romance who secretly yearns to trade floral arrangements for the cold steel of an executioner's block. 

Actually, scratch that initial thought about the digital age entirely. Technology is merely a distraction here; the real machinery of the book is the quiet, rotting floorboards of human obsession. 

When a pair of grooms named Nicholas and Sebastian hire Ephraim, the boundary between his secret bloodshed fantasies and his professional life begins to dissolve. A stalker lingers in the shadows. The walls have eyes. Or perhaps algorithms. The premise flirts dangerously with the absurdity of curated queer joy in a surveillance-obsessed culture. 

Bizarre.

Writers today love to weaponize domestic spaces, transforming cozy cottages into panopticons where every whispered secret is recorded for the invisible audience. LaRocca is following in the slippery footsteps of Clive Barker and perhaps a touch of Shirley Jackson, though with a distinct penchant for bodily ruin that makes my old bones ache. We watch the wedding planner from afar, wondering if the blood on his hands is literal or merely the residue of his fevered imagination. 

Watch the shadows by the porch.

There is a profound silence in contemplating a wedding that doubles as a slaughterhouse. Love and death have always danced closely in the oldest myths I used to tell you by candlelight—think of Hades and Persephone, or Orpheus looking back too soon—yet translating that ancient tragedy into a splatterpunk critique of modern voyeurism feels wildly unpredictable. Will it collapse under the weight of its own cynicism? Quite possibly. But the risk alone makes the pulse race like it used to when the wolves howled near the village edge. 

Wait. The ink is smudging. 

I must go rescue my favorite tortoiseshell fountain pen before you smear it all over your hands again.
