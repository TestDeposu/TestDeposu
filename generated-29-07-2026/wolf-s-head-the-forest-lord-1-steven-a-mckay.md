# Wolf's Head (The Forest Lord, #1)

**Author:** Steven A. McKay  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2024

Steeped in the bloody, mud-soaked reality of ninth-century Britain, Steven A. McKay’s Wolf’s Head thrusts readers headfirst into the crucible of a nation’s birth. The opening installment of The Forest Lord series takes one of history's most celebrated monarchs—Alfred the Great—and strips away the romantic patina of legend to reveal a desperate, brilliant tactician fighting tooth and nail for the survival of his people. This is historical fiction at its most visceral, where the stakes are absolute, and the margin between triumph and total annihilation is razor-thin.

The novel’s central theme revolves around resilience in the face of insurmountable odds and the heavy, often crushing burden of destiny. McKay deftly explores the psychological transformation of King Alfred. Far from the untouchable hero of schoolbook tales, this Alfred is cornered, betrayed by his own kin, and forced to lick his wounds in the treacherous, mist-shrouded marshes of Wessex. The theme of rebirth permeates the narrative: as Alfred retreats into the shadows, he must shed the conventional expectations of royalty and adopt the cunning of the hunted. He becomes, metaphorically, a wolf’s head—an outlaw in his own land—before he can rise to reclaim his crown. Juxtaposed against him is the looming, terrifying presence of the Viking warlord Guthrum, whose heathen horde embodies the brutal chaos threatening to swallow civilization whole. 

McKay’s writing style is punchy, relentlessly fast-paced, and unapologetically gritty. He possesses a rare talent for balancing grand, sweeping geopolitical strategy with intimate, bone-crunching combat. The prose does not linger unnecessarily in purple description; instead, it relies on sensory details—the stench of stagnant marsh water, the sickening crunch of iron on bone, the biting chill of an English winter—to immerse the reader completely in the dark ages. The dialogue is sharp and purposeful, reflecting the urgency of a people living on borrowed time. Furthermore, McKay’s pacing ensures that the tension never flags. Even during the quieter moments of reconnaissance and alliance-building in the swamps, the threat of the Viking war machine hangs heavy in the air like an impending storm.

What makes Wolf’s Head stand out in the crowded genre of historical adventure is McKay’s deep respect for historical texture combined with modern storytelling sensibilities. He honors the historical record of the Anglo-Saxon chronicles while breathing vibrant, pulsing life into the historical figures who usually remain flat names on a page. The battle scenes are choreographed with brutal clarity, avoiding Hollywood gloss in favor of the desperate, chaotic reality of shield-wall warfare. 

In Wolf’s Head, Steven A. McKay has crafted an utterly ferocious and gripping adventure that lays a formidable foundation for The Forest Lord series. It is a thrilling testament to human endurance, tactical brilliance, and the unbreakable spirit of an underdog king facing the twilight of his civilization. For fans of Bernard Cornwell and historical combat fiction, this is an unmissable journey into the forge where England was hammered into shape.
