# The Moon and Sixpence

**Author:** W. Somerset Maugham  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

To trade the suffocating comfort of bourgeois respectability for the ruthless, consuming fires of artistic creation—this is the transgressive leap at the heart of W. Somerset Maugham’s 1919 masterpiece, The Moon and Sixpence. Loosely inspired by the tumultuous life of French Post-Impressionist Paul Gauguin, Maugham’s novel is not a polite biography, but a searing psychological dissection of genius, obsession, and the absolute cost of beauty.

The narrative unfolds through the observant eyes of an aspiring writer who is first pulled into the orbit of Charles Strickland through the London literary soirées hosted by Strickland’s ambitious wife. When Strickland abruptly abandons his conventional life as a well-to-do English stockbroker, leaving behind a wife and two children with chilling indifference, society assumes another woman is to blame. The truth, however, is far more shocking: Strickland has gone to Paris simply to paint. He possesses no formal training, no charming affability, and no apparent sentimental affection for his past. He possesses only a fierce, demonic compulsion to create.

Maugham’s stylistic brilliance lies in his framing of this descent. Through a first-person episodic structure, the narrator acts as both a chronicler and a bewildered proxy for the reader. We watch Strickland’s transformation from a dull, unassuming suburbanite into a man living in abject squalor—enduring freezing Parisian garrets, chronic illness, and gnawing hunger, all without a shred of self-pity or remorse. Maugham refuses to romanticize the artist’s struggle in the traditional sense; Strickland is neither a misunderstood romantic hero nor a sympathetic figure. He is cold, cruel, and singularly driven, treating human lives as disposable kindling for the fire of his art. 

The central theme of the novel examines the eternal friction between the mundane world—symbolized by the sixpence, representing the material, practical aspects of life—and the transcendent, unreachable realm of the moon, representing art, beauty, and the sublime. Maugham questions whether the pursuit of this sublime justification excuses profound cruelty. As Strickland eventually drifts toward the exotic isolation of Tahiti, seeking a primal landscape where his genius can finally explode onto the canvas without the constraints of European civilization, the reader is left wrestling with an unsettling question: Is the total surrender to one's calling a form of supreme liberation, or a monstrous form of madness?

Maugham’s prose is characteristically crisp, detached, and deeply psychological, offering a masterclass in character study. He captures human hypocrisy with dry wit while simultaneously acknowledging forces far wilder and darker than polite society can comprehend. The Moon and Sixpence remains a striking, provocative critique of conventional morality and a timeless exploration of the price one must pay to touch the moon.
