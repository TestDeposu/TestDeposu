# Catherine, Called Birdy

**Author:** Karen Cushman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1994

Step back into the mud, manure, and rigid expectations of thirteenth-century England, and meet a girl who refuses to tiptoe quietly through history. Karen Cushman’s 1994 Newbery Honor classic, Catherine, Called Birdy, is a masterclass in historical fiction, breathing vibrant, irreverent life into the year 1290 through the sharp eyes and sharper tongue of its unforgettable fourteen-year-old diarist. 

At its core, the novel pulses with a timeless and universal theme: the claustrophobia of societal expectation and the fierce, enduring quest for autonomy. Catherine feels trapped in the inescapable cage of medieval womanhood, where her father views her merely as currency to be traded for land, livestock, or political alliance. Yet, Cushman avoids the trap of historical sentimentality. Instead of a passive damsel, Birdy is a spitfire armed with wit, mischief, and sheer stubbornness. Whether she is sabotaging suitors by feigning madness, smearing herself with foul-smelling concoctions, or simply recording the tedious rhythm of embroidery and feast preparations, Birdy’s rebellion is both hilarious and deeply poignant. As illustrator Vera Brosgol aptly noted, Birdy speaks to every generation of readers because her struggle is fundamental: when life builds cages around you, the ultimate power lies in refusing to let those cages define your spirit.

Cushman’s authorial style is nothing short of brilliant. Written entirely in the form of a journal, the book relies on Birdy’s unique, authentic voice—a delightful blend of medieval piety, youthful angst, and razor-sharp cynicism. Birdy’s entries frequently begin with observations about the saints' days and end with exasperated sighs directed at her unfortunate reality, punctuated by her favorite exclamation, "Deus!" Through this intimate diary format, Cushman effortlessly bridges the vast chronological chasm between 1290 and the modern day. She paints a richly textured, sensory-heavy backdrop of medieval manor life without ever bogging the narrative down in dry exposition. The smells of the rush-strewn floor, the grease of roasted meats, and the terror of looming arranged marriages feel startlingly immediate. 

Ultimately, Catherine, Called Birdy is a triumph of character and voice. It reminds readers young and old that courage isn't always about slaying dragons; sometimes, it is simply about holding fast to your own mind when the whole world is trying to sell you off.
