# Suicide Notes

**Author:** Michael Thomas Ford  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

To wake up on New Year’s Day with your wrists bandaged in a psychiatric ward is a stark way to begin any year, yet for fifteen-year-old Jeff, the protagonist of Michael Thomas Ford’s 2011 novel Suicide Notes, the immediate reaction is sheer denial. Jeff insists there has been a massive mistake. He is not like the other kids in Ward 4 North; he is normal, fine, and entirely out of place among teenagers battling severe mental health crises. But over the next forty-five days, the armor of denial begins to crack, revealing a profound and necessary journey toward self-acceptance. 

Suicide Notes stands shoulder-to-shoulder with modern YA classics like It’s Kind of a Funny Story and The Perks of Being a Wallflower, capturing the dizzying, terrifying essence of adolescent angst. Yet, Ford carves out a distinct space for his narrative by refusing to wallow exclusively in despair. Instead, the novel’s greatest triumph lies in its tonal balance, masterfully blending an unflinching look at teen depression with a sharp, idiosyncratic dark humor. 

Ford’s authorial style is defined by Jeff’s narrative voice, which is fiercely witty, deeply sarcastic, and surprisingly funny. Jeff uses humor as a psychological defense mechanism, a way to keep the terrifying reality of his suicide attempt at arm's length. By filtering the clinical sterility of a psychiatric ward through Jeff’s cynical yet observant lens, Ford manages to find absurdity in the darkest of places. This gallows humor never feels disrespectful to the gravity of the subject matter; rather, it anchors the emotional realism of the book. Teenagers facing profound distress rarely speak in grand, poetic monologues; they deflect, they joke, and they mask their pain, and Ford captures this psychological truth with remarkable precision.

At its core, however, Suicide Notes is more than just a story about surviving a mental health crisis—it is an unforgettable LGBTQ+ coming-of-age novel. Beneath Jeff’s wisecracks and resistance lies the suffocating weight of a secret he has barely dared to articulate to himself, let alone to the world. His internal struggle with his identity is inextricably linked to his despair, exploring the profound isolation that comes from feeling entirely alone in who you are. Ford handles the intersection of teen mental health and coming out with tenderness and urgency, demonstrating how self-loathing often masquerades as depression when a person is forced to hide their true self.

The novel also excels in its portrayal of institutional community. Confined to the ward, Jeff is forced to interact with a memorable cast of fellow patients—individuals he initially dismisses as "whack-jobs," ranging from an arsonist named Alice to a quiet boy called Bone. Through these relationships, the book explores the theme of finding your people in the most unexpected places. Jeff slowly realizes that vulnerability is not a weakness and that his fellow patients, with all their jagged edges and unpolished trauma, understand him in a way the outside world never could. 

Ford’s writing makes a powerful emotional impact because it refuses to offer easy, wrapped-in-plastic resolutions. Healing is portrayed as messy, non-linear, and painfully slow. The forty-five days Jeff spends in the hospital do not magically cure his pain, but they do equip him with the tools to confront the person he has been lying to the most: himself. 

Suicide Notes is a vital, provocative, and deeply human read. It speaks directly to anyone who has ever felt like an alien in their own skin, offering both validation and a glimmer of hope. By pairing harrowing honesty with unexpected warmth and wit, Michael Thomas Ford has crafted a timeless exploration of what it means to step out of the shadows of self-deception and finally begin the work of living.
