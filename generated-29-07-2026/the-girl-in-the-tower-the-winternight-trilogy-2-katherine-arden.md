# The Girl in the Tower (The Winternight Trilogy, #2)

**Author:** Katherine Arden  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

In The Girl in the Tower, the mesmerizing second installment of Katherine Arden’s critically acclaimed Winternight Trilogy, the harsh, snow-swept wilderness of medieval Rus gives way to the glittering, yet equally perilous, halls of Moscow power. Building upon the rich folkloric foundations laid in The Bear and the Nightingale, Arden crafts a breathtaking evolution of her narrative, pivoting from a rustic fable into a sweeping historical fantasy of court intrigue, identity, and sacrifice. 

At the pulsating heart of the novel is Vasilisa Petrovna, a heroine who refuses to be silenced, caged, or defined by the rigid patriarchal expectations of her era. Having been branded a witch by the superstitious inhabitants of her northern village, Vasya stands at a crossroads that many women of her time faced: the suffocating confinement of a convent or the equally oppressive walls of a forced marriage. Both paths lead metaphorically and literally to a tower, cutting her off from the wind, the wild, and the magic in her blood. True to her fierce spirit, Vasya chooses a third path. She cuts her hair, binds her chest, dons men's clothing, and rides out into the vast, unforgiving Russian frontier atop her loyal stallion, Solovey. 

Arden’s thematic exploration centers heavily on the tension between freedom and confinement, the old pagan ways and the encroaching shadow of the church, and the duality of human nature. The "tower" of the title is a potent symbol of female subjugation, representing how society attempts to lock away anything it does not understand—whether that is magic, untamed nature, or independent women. Through Vasya’s cross-dressing disguise in Moscow, Arden brilliantly deconstructs gender roles of medieval Russia, allowing her protagonist to move through the political spheres usually barred to her, while simultaneously heightening the suspense as she dances on the razor’s edge of discovery. 

Equally compelling is Arden’s signature literary style, which effortlessly bridges historical fiction and high fantasy. Her prose is lyrical, atmospheric, and sensorially rich, carrying the chill of the northern wind and the smell of woodsmoke right off the page. She does not merely recount history; she infuses it with the pulsing life of Russian mythology. The boundary between the natural and the supernatural is delightfully porous in Arden’s universe, where household spirits, forest demons, and vengeful gods are as real and influential as Grand Princes and warring khans. 

The relationship between Vasya and Morozko, the enigmatic winter demon, deepens into a complex, morally ambiguous waltz. Their dynamic avoids cliché, grounded instead in mutual survival, ancient debts, and a slow-burn tension that crackles with frost and unspoken longing. Furthermore, Vasya’s reunion with her long-estranged siblings—her pious sister Olga and her brother Sasha, now a monk—injects a powerful emotional core into the story. Family loyalty clashes with the terrifying reality of Vasya’s supernatural gifts, forcing those who love her most to grapple with the fear that she might genuinely be damned. 

As dark, unseen forces gather around Moscow, threatening the realm from within and without, Vasya must step out of disguise and into her true power. Arden maintains a relentless, compelling pace, ensuring that the political machinations of the Grand Prince are just as gripping as the magical showdowns against malevolent spirits. 

The Girl in the Tower is a triumph of atmospheric world-building and character-driven storytelling. Katherine Arden has not merely written a sequel; she has expanded her fictional universe into something grander, darker, and profoundly enchanting. It is a striking testament to the strength of those who refuse to be locked away, making it an absolute must-read for lovers of lush historical fantasy and timeless folklore.
