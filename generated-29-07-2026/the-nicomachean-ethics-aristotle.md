# The Nicomachean Ethics

**Author:** Aristotle  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

What is the ultimate purpose of human existence? More than two millennia after it was first set down, Aristotle’s The Nicomachean Ethics remains arguably the most vital and enduring exploration of that profound question ever committed to paper. In this seminal work, the ancient Greek philosopher cuts through the noise of fleeting desires and material ambitions to arrive at a singular, striking conclusion: the highest good for man is happiness. 

Yet, Aristotle’s definition of happiness—or *eudaimonia*, as it was originally conceived—bears little resemblance to our modern, often superficial understandings of the word. Happiness, in Aristotle’s view, is not a passing emotional state, a burst of euphoria, or a passive mood. Rather, it is an active, lifelong endeavor. It is a specially good kind of life, meticulously forged through continuous action, moral excellence, and the deliberate exercise of our highest human capacities. 

The brilliance of the treatise lies in its deep practicality. Aristotle does not spin utopian fantasies disconnected from reality; instead, he grounds his philosophy in the messy, beautiful complexities of human society. He explores how we must navigate the world as community members, cultivating vital moral virtues like courage, temperance, and justice to ensure mutual flourishing. At the same time, he elevates the human spirit, arguing that our ultimate potential is realized when we engage in higher-order contemplation—a state he describes as god-like. 

Beyond its famous exploration of virtue, The Nicomachean Ethics is a masterclass in philosophical anthropology and moral psychology. Within its pages, Aristotle offers piercingly modern discussions regarding moral responsibility, examining how and why we are accountable for our actions. He investigates the intricate mechanics of practical reasoning—how we figure out the right thing to do in shifting circumstances—and devotes some of the most tender, insightful passages in classical literature to the anatomy of friendship and its indispensable role in the best possible life. 

This Oxford World’s Classics edition brings Aristotle’s timeless brilliance into sharp, accessible focus. It features a brilliant, light revision of David Ross’s justly admired translation, striking a rare balance between scholarly precision and readable prose. Accompanied by a valuable, illuminating introduction and carefully crafted explanatory notes, this volume serves as the ultimate gateway for both newcomers and seasoned philosophers to decode Aristotle’s dense, rigorous arguments. 

The author’s style is uniquely clinical yet deeply humane. Aristotle writes not as a detached mystic dictating dogma, but as an observant scientist of the human soul. He dissects ethics with the precision of an anatomist, mapping out extremes of behavior and locating the elusive 'golden mean'—the virtuous balance between deficiency and excess. His prose is analytical, systematic, and relentlessly logical, yet it pulsates with an urgent, deeply caring desire to help humanity live better. He wants to know how we can be better, act better, and ultimately, be fulfilled. 

In an era saturated with quick fixes, shallow self-help, and endless distractions, The Nicomachean Ethics stands as an uncompromising challenge. It demands that we look inward, evaluate our daily habits, and take active ownership of our character and destiny. It reminds us that a good life is not something that simply happens to us; it is a masterpiece we must actively build, day by deliberate day. This book is not merely a text to be read; it is a lifelong companion for anyone earnestly seeking the art of living well.
