# This Is How It Always Is

**Author:** Laurie Frankel  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

Parenting is a perpetual tightrope walk across an abyss of the unknown, but what happens when the wind shifts entirely? Laurie Frankel’s 2017 bestselling novel, This Is How It Always Is, tackles this profound question with fierce intelligence, boundless empathy, and a disarming dose of humor. It is a story that does not merely invite the reader in; it rearranges your perspective from the very first chapter.

At the heart of the narrative is Claude, the youngest of five brothers in a boisterous, loving family. At five years old, Claude loves peanut butter sandwiches, fairy tales, and wearing dresses. When he grows up, Claude wants to be a princess. More specifically, he wants to be a girl. Rosie and Penn, his fiercely devoted parents, adopt a progressive approach: they want Claude to be whoever Claude wants to be. But wanting your child to be happy in the privacy of your home is one thing; navigating a rigid, often unforgiving outside world is entirely another. 

To protect their child, the family begins to keep a secret. They move across the country, reinvent themselves, and let Claude live openly as Poppy. But as Frankel’s narrative deftly proves, secrets have a gravitational pull. They demand maintenance, they create fractures, and eventually, they explode. 

Frankel’s greatest triumph in this novel is her avoidance of cliché. This is not a trauma porn tragedy, nor is it a saccharine after-school special. Instead, Frankel explores the messy, beautiful reality of raising a transgender child through the lens of a wonderfully imperfect family. The brothers—noisy, protective, and sometimes embarrassingly honest—add a layer of grounded domestic chaos that keeps the heavy themes buoyant. Penn and Rosie are not saints; they are panicked, exhausted parents making mistakes in real-time, which makes their ultimate acts of fierce unconditional love all the more resonant.

The author’s style is sharp, witty, and deeply philosophical without ever feeling academic. She frames the narrative around fairy tales—the stories we tell our children, the lies we tell ourselves, and the terrifying leaps of faith required to let go of the scripts we wrote for our families. Frankel writes with a rhythmic cadence that mirrors the relentless forward motion of family life. She understands that change is rarely a singular, cinematic moment; it is a grinding, terrifying, miraculous daily practice. 

This Is How It Always Is is a masterclass in contemporary domestic fiction. It makes you laugh through your tears and forces you to confront your own assumptions about identity, community, and unconditional acceptance. Frankel has crafted a modern fairy tale for an era desperately in need of grace—a vital reminder that while parenting is never according to plan, love remains the only true compass.
