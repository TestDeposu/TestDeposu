# Going Clear: Scientology, Hollywood, and the Prison of Belief

**Author:** Lawrence Wright  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

In Lawrence Wright's gripping and meticulous exposé, "Going Clear: Scientology, Hollywood, and the Prison of Belief," the Pulitzer Prize-winning author sheds light on one of the most secretive and misunderstood organizations at work today: the Church of Scientology. Through extensive research and over 200 personal interviews with current and former Scientologists, Wright takes readers on a thought-provoking journey into the inner workings of the church, peeling back the layers of its enigmatic practices and revealing a complex web of power, manipulation, and deception.

Wright's narrative is a masterclass in investigative journalism, effortlessly weaving together history, psychology, and sociology to create a comprehensive picture of Scientology's rise to prominence and its subsequent transformation into a multi-billion-dollar industry. The author masterfully humanizes the characters at the heart of the story, from the charismatic but troubled founder L. Ron Hubbard to his successor, David Miscavige, a leader driven by a zealous determination to maintain the church's reputation at all costs.

Throughout the book, Wright poses profound questions about the nature of religion, probing what fundamentally defines a creed and whether Scientology's eclectic blend of science fiction, self-help, and spiritual dogma warrants its Constitutionally-protected status. This is a deeply thought-provoking inquiry, one that Wright addresses with remarkable nuance and sensitivity, never succumbing to sensationalism or polemics.

One of the greatest strengths of "Going Clear" lies in Wright's ability to distill complex ideas and institutions into clear, concise prose. The author's writing is characterized by a rare blend of lucidity and compassion, imbuing even the most disturbing episodes with a sense of context and understanding. Whether recounting the tragic tale of Lisa McPherson, a young woman who died under the watchful eye of the church's paramilitary organization, or dissecting the church's intricate system of control, Wright brings a measured empathy to his narrative, eschewing the impulse to condemn or vilify in favor of a more nuanced, even-handed approach.

Of course, Wright's work is not without its detractors, and "Going Clear" has been greeted by fierce criticism from Scientology's apologists and defenders. However, for those willing to challenge the church's carefully constructed myths, Wright's book is an absolute revelation – a meticulously crafted exposé that has been hailed by critics as "an utterly necessary story" (Wall Street Journal) and a Best Nonfiction Book of the Century by Kirkus Reviews.

Ultimately, "Going Clear: Scientology, Hollywood, and the Prison of Belief" is a testament to the transformative power of journalism, a triumph of dogged research and fearless inquiry that will leave readers both informed and unsettled. Like Hubbard's own science fiction novels, Wright's book challenges its readers to confront the blurred lines between truth and fiction, between reality and fantasy. In doing so, Wright delivers a work that is at once a scathing critique of Scientology's authoritarian culture and a profound exploration of the human condition – a reminder that, even in the darkest depths of faith and madness, there lies a profound capacity for resilience, hope, and redemption.
