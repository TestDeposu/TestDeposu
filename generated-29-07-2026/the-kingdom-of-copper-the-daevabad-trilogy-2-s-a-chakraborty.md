# The Kingdom of Copper (The Daevabad Trilogy, #2)

**Author:** S.A. Chakraborty  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

The Kingdom of Copper, the second installment of S.A. Chakraborty's Daevabad Trilogy, is a masterful continuation of the epic fantasy adventure that began in The City of Brass. Chakraborty's writing is as enchanting as the world she has created, weaving a tale of magic, politics, and self-discovery that will captivate readers from start to finish.

At the heart of the story is Nahri, a young con artist from Cairo who has found herself at the center of a treacherous web of royal politics in the magical city of Daevabad. As she navigates the complexities of her new life, Nahri must confront the secrets of her past and the power that lies within her. Chakraborty's portrayal of Nahri is nothing short of phenomenal, imbuing her with a depth and nuance that makes her one of the most compelling protagonists in modern fantasy.

One of the most striking aspects of Chakraborty's writing is her ability to craft a world that is at once familiar and yet utterly unique. The city of Daevabad, with its towering brass walls and ancient magic, is a place of wonder and discovery, where the boundaries between reality and myth blur and blend. The author's use of Middle Eastern and South Asian mythology adds a rich layer of depth to the story, drawing readers into a world that is both fantastical and deeply rooted in history and culture.

The Kingdom of Copper is also a story about the complexities of power and the dangers of blind ambition. The ruling family of Daevabad is plagued by secrets and lies, and the consequences of their actions threaten to destroy the very fabric of the city. Chakraborty's exploration of these themes is both timely and timeless, offering a commentary on the dangers of unchecked power and the importance of empathy and understanding.

Throughout the book, Chakraborty's writing is infused with a sense of urgency and tension, as the characters navigate the treacherous landscape of Daevabad's royal court. The pacing is expertly handled, with a narrative that flows smoothly from one scene to the next, drawing the reader deeper into the world of the story. The supporting cast is equally well-developed, with characters like Ali and Dara adding depth and complexity to the narrative.

If there is one criticism to be made, it is that the book can feel overwhelming at times, with a large cast of characters and a complex web of plot threads to keep track of. However, this is a minor quibble, and one that is more than offset by the sheer scope and ambition of Chakraborty's vision.

In conclusion, The Kingdom of Copper is a stunning achievement, a book that will appeal to fans of fantasy, adventure, and historical fiction alike. Chakraborty's writing is captivating, her world-building is impeccable, and her characters are complex and nuanced. This is a book that will keep you up late at night, turning the pages with a sense of wonder and awe. If you haven't already, do yourself a favor and immerse yourself in the world of Daevabad – you won't regret it.
