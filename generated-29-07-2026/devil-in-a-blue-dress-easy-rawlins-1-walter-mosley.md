# Devil in a Blue Dress (Easy Rawlins, #1)

**Author:** Walter Mosley  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

In the sweltering summer of 1948, Walter Mosley's 'Devil in a Blue Dress' unfolds like a slow-burning ember, casting a haunting glow over the African-American community of Watts, Los Angeles. This debut installment of the Easy Rawlins mystery series is a gripping tale of racial tension, moral ambiguity, and the search for identity in a society fraught with prejudice.

At the heart of the narrative is Ezekiel 'Easy' Rawlins, a black war veteran struggling to find his place in a world that seems determined to hold him back. When Easy is fired from his job at a defense plant, he is forced to navigate the treacherous landscape of post-war America, where the boundaries between right and wrong are constantly blurred. It is against this backdrop that Easy is approached by a mysterious white man in a linen suit, who offers him a substantial sum of money to locate the enigmatic Miss Daphne Monet, a blonde beauty with a penchant for black jazz clubs.

As Easy delves deeper into the mystery of Daphne's disappearance, he finds himself entangled in a complex web of deceit and corruption that threatens to destroy everything he holds dear. Mosley's masterful storytelling weaves together a rich tapestry of characters, each with their own secrets and motivations, creating a sense of tension that is both palpable and suffocating.

One of the most striking aspects of 'Devil in a Blue Dress' is Mosley's vivid portrayal of life in 1940s Los Angeles. The city is depicted as a place of stark contrasts, where the glamour of Hollywood and the jazz scene coexists with the harsh realities of racism and poverty. Through Easy's eyes, we experience the city's vibrant cultural landscape, from the smoke-filled jazz clubs of Central Avenue to the cramped, rundown apartments of Watts.

Mosley's prose is economical and evocative, with a strong emphasis on dialogue and character development. Easy's narrative voice is both poignant and humorous, offering a unique perspective on the world around him. The supporting cast is equally well-drawn, from the enigmatic Daphne Monet to the terrifying Mouse, Easy's childhood friend and sometime-ally.

At its core, 'Devil in a Blue Dress' is a novel about identity and belonging. Easy's search for Daphne Monet is also a search for himself, as he navigates the complexities of his own identity and grapples with the harsh realities of racism in America. Through Easy's journey, Mosley raises important questions about the nature of community and the search for justice in a society that often seems determined to deny it.

In conclusion, 'Devil in a Blue Dress' is a masterpiece of contemporary crime fiction, a gripping and thought-provoking novel that will appeal to fans of authors like Raymond Chandler and Chester Himes. With its vivid portrayal of life in 1940s Los Angeles, its complex characters, and its unflinching examination of racism and identity, this novel is a must-read for anyone interested in the crime genre.
