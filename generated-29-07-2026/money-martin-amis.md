# Money

**Author:** Martin Amis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2025

To open Martin Amis’s 1984 masterpiece is to plug directly into a high-voltage current of linguistic energy, consumerist delirium, and moral rot. Reissued with a sharp new introduction by Lorrie Moore, *Money: A Suicide Note* remains one of the most blistering, darkly hilarious indictments of late-stage capitalism ever committed to the page. Amis does not merely critique the materialist obsession of the twentieth century; he wallows in it, pulverizes it, and holds it up to the light as a glittering, toxic monument to human excess.

At the center of this grotesque carnival is John Self, a man whose very name is a monument to modern narcissism. Self is a successful London commercial director whose life is an unceasing, adrenaline-fueled blur of booze, fast food, pornography, and prostitutes. He is a creature defined entirely by his appetites—a walking stomach and libido, fueled by cash and driven by the terrifying belief that money is the ultimate metric of human worth. When the slick, enigmatic American filmmaker Fielding Goodney lures Self across the Atlantic to New York to direct his first feature film, titled, appropriately, *Good Money*, Self steps deeper into a vortex of corruption that threatens to consume what little is left of his soul.

Amis’s primary theme is the total commodification of existence. Long before the digital age hyper-accelerated our obsession with personal branding and instant gratification, Amis foresaw a world where human relationships, art, and identity are reduced to mere transactions. In the universe of *Money*, everything has a price tag, and everyone is for sale. Yet, despite his grotesque lifestyle, Self is not merely a villain; he is a tragicomic victim of the very system he worships. He is desperate, gullible, and painfully aware of his own degradation, making him a profoundly compelling, if utterly repulsive, protagonist.

Stylistically, *Money* is a tour de force. Amis writes with a breathtaking, linguistic bravura that risks everything and wins. His prose is a dazzling cocktail of street slang, high-literary pyrotechnics, and vulgar brilliance. The sentences snap, crackle, and boil with an aggressive vitality that mirrors the frantic pace of the consumer culture it skewers. It is a style that shocks, offends, and ultimately dazzles, forcing the reader to laugh at horrors we recognize all too well in our own contemporary landscape. 

Decades after its initial publication, *Money* has lost none of its bite. If anything, its frantic warnings resonate even more loudly today, in an era defined by influencers, crypto-mania, and relentless self-promotion. Amis’s classic novel is a dangerous, exhilarating, and essential read—a mirror held up to a world that continues to mistake the accumulation of things for the meaning of life.
