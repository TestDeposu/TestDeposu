# Beastly (Beastly, #1)

**Author:** Alex Flinn  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

**Beastly: A Dark, Modern Twist on a Timeless Tale**

Alex Flinn’s *Beastly* is not just another retelling of *Beauty and the Beast*—it’s a gritty, contemporary reimagining that strips away the fairy-tale romance to expose the raw, unfiltered ugliness of vanity and the redemptive power of love. Set in the concrete jungle of New York City, this 2009 novel transforms a classic into a sharp, psychological exploration of identity, punishment, and second chances. Flinn doesn’t just update the story; she weaponizes it, forcing readers to confront the monstrous within us all.

At its core, *Beastly* is a story about perception. Kyle Kingsbury, the novel’s protagonist, is the archetypal high school villain—a wealthy, handsome jock who thrives on superficial admiration. His cruelty isn’t born of malice but of indifference; he’s a boy who has never been told no, who treats people as disposable until the day a witch, in the form of his disfigured English teacher’s daughter, curses him into a beast. The irony? Kyle’s transformation isn’t just physical. It’s a mirror held up to his soul, forcing him to see the emptiness of his past life. The beast isn’t just a monster; he’s a walking metaphor for the consequences of unchecked ego.

Flinn’s genius lies in her ability to make Kyle’s suffering visceral. The curse doesn’t just change his appearance—it amplifies his loneliness, his fear, and his desperate need for connection. The beast’s existence is a paradox: he’s trapped in a body that repels others, yet his mind remains painfully human. His narration is raw, self-loathing, and darkly humorous, a mix of teenage angst and existential dread. When he laments, *“I am a beast. Not quite wolf or bear, gorilla or dog but a horrible new creature who walks upright—a creature with fangs and claws and hair springing from every pore,”* the horror isn’t just in the description but in the realization that he’s still Kyle beneath the fur and fangs—a boy who once thought himself untouchable.

The supporting cast is equally compelling. Lindy, the “Beauty” of the story, isn’t a passive damsel but a flawed, resilient girl who sees beyond Kyle’s monstrous exterior. Her own trauma—having been kidnapped by a drug dealer—mirrors Kyle’s, creating a bond that feels earned rather than contrived. The witch, Kendra, is a fascinating villain: not a hag in a tower but a disfigured outcast who wields her power with a mix of bitterness and reluctant compassion. Even Kyle’s father, a news anchor who can’t bear to look at his son’s new form, serves as a chilling reminder of how society abandons those who don’t fit its standards.

Flinn’s writing style is deceptively simple, but it’s this accessibility that makes *Beastly* so effective. The prose is clean, the dialogue snappy, and the pacing relentless. She doesn’t waste time on flowery descriptions; instead, she immerses the reader in Kyle’s world, where every glance in a mirror is a reminder of his fall from grace. The novel’s structure—alternating between Kyle’s first-person narration and third-person perspectives—keeps the story dynamic, allowing us to see the curse’s ripple effects on those around him.

What makes *Beastly* stand out is its refusal to romanticize the transformation. Unlike traditional fairy tales where the beast’s curse is a temporary trial, Flinn makes it clear that Kyle’s punishment is permanent unless he earns love through genuine change. There’s no magic quick fix here, no easy redemption. The beast must confront his past sins, learn humility, and prove that he’s more than his appearance. It’s a brutal, necessary journey—and one that feels all too real in a world obsessed with looks and status.

Ultimately, *Beastly* is a cautionary tale about the masks we wear and the monsters we become when we let vanity rule us. It’s a story for anyone who’s ever felt like an outsider, anyone who’s ever looked in the mirror and wondered, *Is this really me?* Flinn doesn’t just tell a fairy tale—she forces us to stare into the abyss of our own flaws and ask if we’re brave enough to change.

If you’ve ever loved a retelling that punches you in the gut while still leaving you hopeful, *Beastly* is a must-read. It’s not just a beast’s story—it’s ours.
