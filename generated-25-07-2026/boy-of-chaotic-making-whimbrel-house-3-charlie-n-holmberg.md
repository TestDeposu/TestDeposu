# Boy of Chaotic Making (Whimbrel House, #3)

**Author:** Charlie N. Holmberg  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2024

**A House That Loves You Back: *Boy of Chaotic Making* and the Allure of the Unwanted**

Charlie N. Holmberg’s *Boy of Chaotic Making* is a gothic fairy tale for the modern age, a story where a house doesn’t just haunt its inhabitants—it *chooses* them. In this third installment of the Whimbrel House series, Holmberg weaves a tale of possession, partnership, and the eerie intimacy of a home that refuses to let go. It’s a book that lingers in the mind like the scent of old wood and candle wax, haunting not just its characters but its readers long after the last page.

At its core, *Boy of Chaotic Making* is a story about control—or the illusion of it. Merritt Fernsby inherits Whimbrel House under the assumption that he can do with it as he pleases. But the house, a sentient, temperamental entity, has other plans. It slams shut behind him, trapping him inside, and it’s only through Hulda Larkin, a no-nonsense expert in haunted architecture, that he begins to understand: Whimbrel House isn’t just a building. It’s a living thing, and it has decided Merritt is its new caretaker. The twist? The house isn’t just angry—it’s *lonely*.

Holmberg’s genius lies in her ability to make the supernatural feel intimate. Whimbrel House isn’t a distant, shadowy menace; it’s a jealous lover, a demanding parent, a creature that pouts when ignored and rages when provoked. The house’s personality is as vivid as its human characters, and Holmberg’s prose makes it feel tangible. When Merritt and Hulda navigate its labyrinthine halls, the walls seem to breathe around them, the air thick with the weight of centuries. The house’s magic isn’t just in its ghosts—it’s in its *moods*, its whims, its sudden, inexplicable shifts from cold indifference to suffocating affection.

This is where Holmberg’s style shines. She blends historical detail with eerie fantasy, grounding the supernatural in the tangible. The 1846 Rhode Island setting isn’t just a backdrop; it’s a character itself, steeped in the dust of forgotten trades and the whispers of a time when magic was still a whispered secret. Merritt, a writer, is a perfect lens through which to explore this world—his skepticism makes the house’s antics more unsettling, while his growing bond with Whimbrel House forces him to confront the idea that some things can’t be controlled, only *understood*.

Hulda, meanwhile, is a delight—a sharp, pragmatic woman who knows the dangers of haunted houses but isn’t afraid to face them. Her dynamic with Merritt crackles with tension, both professional and personal. Their relationship evolves from wary alliance to something deeper, and Holmberg handles their growing intimacy with a deft touch. There’s no rushed romance here; instead, it’s a slow burn, built on shared danger and the quiet understanding that they’re both out of their depth. The house, ever the meddler, seems to approve.

But *Boy of Chaotic Making* isn’t just a love story between two people—it’s a love story between people and a place. The house’s demands are relentless, its affections as unpredictable as its temper. When something *else* begins to lurk outside its walls, something that wants to claim Whimbrel House for itself, the stakes rise. The threat isn’t just external; it’s the house’s own history, its buried secrets, the things it’s done to survive. Holmberg plays with the idea of possession in every sense—physical, emotional, magical—and the result is a story that’s as much about surrender as it is about defiance.

What makes *Boy of Chaotic Making* so compelling is its refusal to simplify. The house isn’t purely evil, nor is it purely benevolent. It’s a creature of contradictions, as capable of warmth as it is of cruelty. Merritt and Hulda’s journey isn’t just about surviving the house—it’s about learning to live with it, to negotiate with it, to accept that some bonds aren’t chosen so much as *destined*. In a world that often feels out of control, Whimbrel House offers a strange kind of comfort: the idea that even the most chaotic things can be loved, if you’re willing to meet them halfway.

Holmberg’s writing is lush but never overwrought, her world-building immersive without drowning the reader in exposition. She trusts her audience to piece things together, to feel the weight of the house’s presence without needing it spelled out. And when the climax arrives—tense, emotional, and deeply satisfying—it’s because the story has earned every moment of it.

*Boy of Chaotic Making* is a book that lingers, like the echo of a door slamming shut behind you. It’s a reminder that some places don’t just shelter us—they *claim* us. And once they do, there’s no going back.
