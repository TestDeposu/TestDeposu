# Specials (Uglies, #3)

**Author:** Scott Westerfeld  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2008

In the world of young adult dystopian fiction, few series have made as lasting an impact as Scott Westerfeld's Uglies. The third installment, Specials, is a gripping and thought-provoking exploration of the tension between free will and societal pressure. Tally, the protagonist, has undergone a transformation from an "Ugly" to a "Special", a super-soldier engineered to maintain the status quo in a world where conformity is the ultimate goal.

Westerfeld's writing is as sharp as ever, with a keen eye for the nuances of adolescent angst and the struggles of self-discovery. Tally's inner turmoil is palpable as she grapples with the remnants of her past life and the programming that now drives her. The author's use of vivid imagery and clever world-building creates a sense of immersion, drawing the reader into the eerie and unsettling world of the Specials.

At its core, Specials is a meditation on the nature of identity and the human condition. Tally's journey raises fundamental questions about the trade-offs between freedom and security, individuality and conformity. As she navigates the complex web of her own programming and the expectations placed upon her, Tally must confront the possibility that her newfound strength and focus may come at the cost of her very soul.

One of the most striking aspects of Westerfeld's style is his ability to balance action and introspection. The pacing is relentless, with Tally's missions and training exercises providing a thrilling backdrop for her inner struggles. Yet, the author never sacrifices depth for the sake of plot, instead using the narrative to explore the intricacies of Tally's psyche.

Throughout the series, Westerfeld has demonstrated a keen understanding of the adolescent experience, and Specials is no exception. Tally's relationships with her peers, particularly Shay and David, are multifaceted and authentic, reflecting the messy and often painful nature of teenage friendships.

If there's a criticism to be made, it's that the supporting cast sometimes feels slightly one-dimensional. Certain characters, such as the enigmatic Dr. Cable, feel more like plot devices than fully fleshed-out people. However, this is a minor quibble in an otherwise engaging and thought-provoking narrative.

Ultimately, Specials is a gripping and unsettling read that will appeal to fans of dystopian fiction and anyone interested in exploring the complexities of the human condition. Westerfeld's prose is engaging, his world-building is meticulous, and his characters are multidimensional and relatable.
