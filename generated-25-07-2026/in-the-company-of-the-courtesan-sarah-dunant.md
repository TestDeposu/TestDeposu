# In the Company of the Courtesan

**Author:** Sarah Dunant  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2006

Sarah Dunant’s *In the Company of the Courtesan* is a lush, intoxicating dive into the underbelly of Renaissance Italy, where beauty and brutality coexist in equal measure. Set against the backdrop of the 1527 Sack of Rome—a historical catastrophe that left the Eternal City in ruins—this novel follows the perilous journey of Fiammetta Bianchini, a courtesan of rare wit and resilience, and her loyal dwarf companion, Bucino. Their escape to Venice, that glittering yet grotesque marvel of trade and decadence, becomes a story of survival, reinvention, and the fragile bonds that tether people to one another in a world that values them only for what they can provide.

At its core, *In the Company of the Courtesan* is a meditation on power—who wields it, who is exploited by it, and how it shifts like the tides of the Venetian lagoon. Fiammetta is no passive victim; she is a survivor who has turned her body and mind into weapons, using charm and cunning to navigate a world that would otherwise discard her. Yet her agency is always precarious, a tightrope walk between the gilded cages of her clients and the violent streets of a city where a woman’s worth is measured in carats and ducats. Dunant doesn’t romanticize her protagonist’s life; instead, she exposes the cost of such existence—the way Fiammetta must swallow jewels to smuggle wealth, the way she must perform desire for men who see her as little more than a possession. It’s a brutal existence, but one Dunant renders with such vivid detail that you can almost taste the salt of the Venetian canals and feel the weight of the silks draped over Fiammetta’s skin.

Bucino, her dwarf companion, is the novel’s unsung hero—a figure of both pathos and sharp humor. His dwarfism is not treated as a tragedy but as a lens through which he observes the world with a mix of cynicism and tenderness. He is Fiammetta’s confidant, her protector, and sometimes her conscience, his voice a counterpoint to her more polished, performative persona. Their relationship is the beating heart of the book, a partnership built on mutual dependence and, ultimately, love. Dunant writes their dynamic with a deft hand, avoiding cliché while still allowing moments of genuine warmth to shine through. Bucino’s narration—wry, observant, and often darkly funny—adds a layer of complexity to the story, reminding us that even in a world obsessed with beauty, the "monstrous" and the "different" have their own forms of power.

The novel’s Venice is a character in itself, a city of contradictions where opulence and squalor exist side by side. Dunant’s prose captures this duality with breathtaking precision. One moment, you’re swept up in the grandeur of a nobleman’s palazzo, the next, you’re choking on the stench of the canals or the desperation of the poor huddled in alleyways. Venice is not just a setting; it’s a living, breathing entity that both sustains and consumes its inhabitants. The city’s labyrinthine streets and shadowy canals become a metaphor for Fiammetta’s own life—complex, treacherous, and full of hidden paths to survival.

Dunant’s style is rich and immersive, blending historical detail with a novelist’s flair for drama. She doesn’t shy away from the gritty realities of the period—the violence, the disease, the casual cruelty—but she also infuses the story with a sense of wonder, particularly in her descriptions of Venice’s art, its markets, and its cultural melting pot. The novel’s pacing is deliberate, allowing the reader to savor the intricacies of the world Dunant has created. Yet it never drags; the tension is always simmering beneath the surface, whether it’s the threat of discovery, the danger of betrayal, or the looming presence of the Ottoman Empire, whose representatives lurk in the shadows, hungry for novelties to present to their sultan.

The emotional core of the book, however, lies in the arrival of a blind healer named La Draga. Her introduction is one of the novel’s most striking moments—a woman who sees the world in ways others cannot, whose presence disrupts Fiammetta and Bucino’s carefully constructed lives. La Draga is neither a saint nor a villain; she is a force of nature, a woman who challenges their assumptions about power, desire, and what it means to be seen. Her relationship with Fiammetta is fraught with tension, a push-and-pull of attraction and resentment that forces both women to confront their own vulnerabilities. It’s a masterful exploration of how even the most resilient among us can be undone by love—or by the fear of losing control.

Ultimately, *In the Company of the Courtesan* is a story about reinvention. Fiammetta and Bucino are survivors, but survival comes at a cost. They must constantly adapt, shedding old identities and adopting new ones, all while navigating a world that is as likely to embrace them as it is to cast them aside. Dunant’s novel is a testament to the resilience of the human spirit, but it’s also a reminder of how fragile that resilience can be. In the end, the courtesan and her dwarf are not just survivors—they are artists of their own lives, painting their existence in bold strokes of defiance and cunning.

Sarah Dunant’s writing is a feast for the senses, a historical novel that feels as immediate and vital as the present. She doesn’t just tell a story; she invites you into a world where every glance, every whispered word, every stolen jewel carries weight. *In the Company of the Courtesan* is a book that lingers, its themes of power, desire, and survival echoing long after the final page.
