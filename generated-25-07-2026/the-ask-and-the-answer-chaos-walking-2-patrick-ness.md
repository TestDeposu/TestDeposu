# The Ask and the Answer (Chaos Walking, #2)

**Author:** Patrick Ness  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

*The Ask and the Answer* is a masterclass in dystopian storytelling, a brutal and unflinching exploration of power, morality, and the cost of survival. Patrick Ness’s second installment in the *Chaos Walking* trilogy doesn’t just continue the harrowing journey of Todd and Viola—it deepens the nightmare, forcing its young protagonists into a moral labyrinth where every choice is a trap. Where the first book (*The Knife of Never Letting Go*) was a frantic escape, this sequel is a slow, suffocating descent into complicity, where the real enemy isn’t just the oppressive regime of New Prentisstown but the creeping rot of compromise itself.

At its core, *The Ask and the Answer* is about the erosion of innocence—not through violence alone, but through the insidious ways power corrupts even the most well-intentioned souls. Todd and Viola, once fleeing the chaos of their old world, now find themselves trapped in a town where thoughts are audible and secrets are currency. The mayor, David Prentiss, is a chilling villain, a man who weaponizes ideology and manipulation with the calm precision of a surgeon. His son, Davy, is a darker reflection of Todd—charismatic, ruthless, and utterly convinced of his own righteousness. Meanwhile, Viola is drawn into the Answer, a rebel faction whose methods grow increasingly brutal, blurring the line between liberation and tyranny.

Ness’s genius lies in his refusal to offer easy answers. This isn’t a story where heroes and villains wear their allegiances on their sleeves; it’s a story where good people do terrible things, where survival often means betrayal, and where the line between right and wrong is drawn in shifting sand. The dual narrative—alternating between Todd’s imprisonment and Viola’s radicalization—heightens the tension, making their eventual reunion feel like a fragile lifeline in a world that has tried to drown them both.

Stylistically, Ness’s prose is lean and punchy, mirroring the relentless pace of the story. His world-building is immersive, with the ever-present noise of men’s thoughts—a cacophony of lies, fears, and half-truths—serving as a constant reminder of how fragile privacy and trust truly are. The short story *The Wide, Wide Sea* that follows the main text is a haunting coda, a glimpse into the psychological toll of Todd’s journey, reinforcing the book’s themes of isolation and the weight of one’s actions.

What makes *The Ask and the Answer* so devastating is its honesty. It doesn’t glorify rebellion or martyrdom; it shows how easily people become what they hate. Todd’s internal conflict—his struggle to reconcile his instincts with the horrors he’s forced to commit—is heartbreakingly real. Viola’s disillusionment with the Answer is equally poignant, a reminder that even those fighting for justice can become monsters in the process.

This is a book that lingers, not just because of its dystopian world or its high-stakes drama, but because of its raw emotional truth. It asks the hardest questions: *How much of yourself are you willing to sacrifice to survive? Can you ever truly trust someone when their thoughts are an open book?* And perhaps most chillingly: *What happens when the enemy isn’t just outside, but inside you?*

Ness doesn’t just tell a story here—he crafts an experience, one that leaves you breathless, questioning, and utterly drained. *The Ask and the Answer* isn’t just a sequel; it’s a crucible, and its characters emerge from it forever changed.
