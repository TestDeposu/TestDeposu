# Nausea

**Author:** Jean-Paul Sartre  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

In the realm of existentialist literature, few works are as profoundly unsettling and philosophically charged as Jean-Paul Sartre's "Nausea". This seminal novel, first published in French as "La Nausée" in 1938, has been re-introduced to readers with an insightful introduction by James Wood, allowing a new generation to grapple with the complexities of Sartre's masterpiece.

At its core, "Nausea" is an unflinching exploration of the human condition, delving into the innermost recesses of the protagonist's mind to reveal the abyss of uncertainty and dread that lies beneath the surface of modern existence. Antoine Roquentin, a French writer, serves as the vehicle for Sartre's philosophical inquiry, chronicling his every thought, feeling, and sensation in a series of diary entries that are both deeply personal and universally relatable.

Through Roquentin's narrative, Sartre masterfully conveys the existential angst that arises from the realization that our lives are devoid of inherent meaning or purpose. The protagonist's growing sense of nausea, which gradually permeates every aspect of his being, serves as a potent metaphor for the crushing awareness of our own mortality and the absurdity of human existence.

Sartre's writing style in "Nausea" is characterized by its unflinching honesty, lyrical prose, and a keen attention to detail that borders on the obsessive. His use of language is at once poetic and visceral, capable of evoking the full range of human emotions with unflinching precision. The text is replete with vivid imagery, from the "wide, soft instants" that spread like an oil stain, to the "purple suspenders and broken chair seats" that serve as a poignant reminder of the mundane reality that underlies our existence.

One of the most striking aspects of "Nausea" is its ability to balance philosophical introspection with a deeply personal and emotional narrative. Roquentin's struggles to come to terms with his own existence are both deeply relatable and profoundly unsettling, forcing the reader to confront the darkest recesses of their own psyche.

As a key text of existentialist literature, "Nausea" continues to exert a profound influence on modern thought and culture. Sartre's refusal to accept the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1964, citing his belief that a writer should not be transformed into an institution, only serves to underscore the enduring power and relevance of his work.

In conclusion, "Nausea" is a masterpiece of existentialist literature that continues to challenge and provoke readers to this day. Sartre's unflinching exploration of the human condition, combined with his unique writing style and philosophical insights, make for a reading experience that is both deeply unsettling and profoundly rewarding.
