# Antigone (Theban Plays, #3)

**Author:** Sophocles  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1973

**Title: Antigone: A Tragic Symphony of Dissent and Power**

Sophocles' "Antigone," the third installment of his Theban Plays, is a profound exploration of the human spirit's collision with tyranny and fate. This ancient Greek tragedy, translated by E.F. Watling, remains as resonant and thought-provoking today as it was in its 5th-century BCE premiere.

At the heart of "Antigone" lies the titular character, a young woman of unyielding principle, played against the backdrop of Thebes' new ruler, Creon. Following the brothers' duel, Creon decrees that Polynices, who fought against Thebes, shall remain unburied, his corpse left to rot as a warning to traitors. Antigone, however, believes that her brother, regardless of his actions, deserves a proper burial according to their gods and customs. Her defiance sets the stage for a tragic conflict between personal morality and state authority.

Sophocles' style is masterful, employing a blend of lyric poetry and prose that captures the raw emotion and intellectual rigor of his characters. The chorus, a collective voice of Thebes, serves as a moral compass, commenting on the unfolding drama and reflecting the audience's own ethical dilemmas. The dialogue is sharp and taut, each line a battle of wills, as Antigone and Creon clash over the meaning of justice and the limits of power.

The play's central theme is the struggle between individual conscience and state authority, a timeless debate that continues to echo in contemporary politics and society. Antigone, with her unwavering commitment to her moral code, stands as a symbol of resistance against oppressive power. Creon, meanwhile, embodies the dangers of absolute authority, his hubris leading him to dismiss the wisdom of his advisors and the will of the gods.

"Antigone" is not just a story of defiance and tyranny; it's a meditation on the human condition, exploring themes of family, loyalty, and the consequences of our actions. It's a testament to Sophocles' genius that, despite its ancient origins, "Antigone" feels as immediate and relevant as ever.
