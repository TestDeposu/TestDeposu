# Scythe (Arc of a Scythe, #1)

**Author:** Neal Shusterman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

Neal Shusterman’s *Scythe* is a masterclass in dystopian fiction, a genre that often explores the dark consequences of utopian ideals. But where most stories warn against the dangers of unchecked power or technological hubris, *Scythe* flips the script by presenting a world so perfected that death itself has been eradicated—only to be reintroduced by an elite class of killers. This paradox is the beating heart of the novel, a chilling meditation on morality, power, and the cost of immortality.

At its core, *Scythe* is about the ethics of control. In this future, humanity has conquered aging, disease, and violence, achieving a state of near-utopia. Yet, without death, society would collapse under its own weight. Enter the Scythes—an order of individuals tasked with "gleaning" (killing) people to maintain balance. The premise alone is enough to unsettle: a world where murder is not just legal but sacred, where the line between justice and tyranny blurs with every death. Shusterman forces readers to confront uncomfortable questions: If death is the ultimate equalizer, who gets to decide who dies? And what does it say about humanity that we need such a system to function?

The novel follows two protagonists, Citra and Rowan, who are reluctantly chosen as apprentices to a Scythe. Neither wants the role—Citra because she values life, Rowan because he’s haunted by his past. Their journey is one of moral awakening, as they grapple with the weight of their future duties. Shusterman excels at character development, making Citra and Rowan relatable in their flaws and fears. Their internal conflicts mirror the larger societal dilemma: Is it possible to be a Scythe without losing one’s humanity? The answer, as the book suggests, is a resounding no.

Shusterman’s writing style is sharp and immersive, blending philosophical depth with the pacing of a thriller. His prose is accessible yet thought-provoking, avoiding the dense exposition that plagues some dystopian novels. The world-building is meticulous but never overwhelming, with just enough detail to make the Scythedom feel real without bogging down the narrative. The dialogue crackles with tension, especially in scenes where Citra and Rowan debate the morality of their new roles. Shusterman also employs a clever narrative structure, alternating between the perspectives of the two apprentices to create suspense and contrast their differing viewpoints.

One of the novel’s greatest strengths is its refusal to offer easy answers. The Scythes are not villains, nor are they heroes—they are necessary evils in a world that has outgrown its own humanity. Shusterman challenges the reader to consider whether perfection is worth the price of our souls. The book’s climax is a masterstroke of moral ambiguity, leaving readers questioning not just the characters’ choices, but their own.

*Scythe* is more than just a dystopian novel; it’s a philosophical puzzle wrapped in a gripping story. It asks us to think about what we’re willing to sacrifice for a perfect world—and whether that world is even worth having. Shusterman’s genius lies in making us care about the answer, even when it terrifies us.
