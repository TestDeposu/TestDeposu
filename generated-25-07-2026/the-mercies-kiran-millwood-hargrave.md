# The Mercies

**Author:** Kiran Millwood Hargrave  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

The Mercies is a haunting, luminous novel that lingers like the Arctic mist over the jagged shores of Vardø. Kiran Millwood Hargrave crafts a story that is both a historical reckoning and a timeless meditation on power, fear, and the resilience of women pushed to the edge of survival. Inspired by real events—the 1617 storm that drowned forty fishermen and the subsequent witch trials of 1621—this novel doesn’t just retell history; it breathes life into it, making the past feel urgent, visceral, and deeply personal.

At its core, *The Mercies* is a story about what happens when women are left to their own devices in a world that refuses to trust them. When the sea claims the men of Vardø, the women—stripped of their usual roles as wives and daughters—must reinvent their lives. They fish, they trade, they build shelters, they govern. They thrive. But this newfound freedom is fragile. Into this fragile paradise arrives Absalom Cornet, a Scottish witch-hunter sent by the king to cleanse the town of its perceived evil. His arrival is a slow, creeping dread, a reminder that autonomy is never truly safe when it threatens the established order.

Hargrave’s prose is spare yet evocative, mirroring the stark, unyielding landscape of northern Norway. She doesn’t waste words, yet every sentence carries weight. The descriptions of the Arctic environment are breathtaking—how the wind howls through the fjords, how the sea can turn from calm to fury in an instant, how the long polar nights press down on the villagers like a physical weight. This isn’t just setting; it’s a character in itself, a force that shapes the lives of the women of Vardø.

The novel’s strength lies in its dual perspective. We follow Maren Magnusdatter, a young woman whose grief over her father and brother’s deaths has hardened into quiet resolve. She is practical, grounded, and deeply connected to the land. Then there’s Ursa Cornet, Absalom’s young wife, who arrives in Vardø with a mix of awe and terror. Ursa is sheltered, sheltered, and initially complicit in her husband’s cruelty, but as she witnesses the women of Vardø—her new neighbors—living freely, she begins to question everything she’s been taught. Their growing bond is tender and fraught, a delicate thread of hope in a world tightening around them.

Hargrave doesn’t shy away from the brutality of the era. The witch trials are depicted with chilling realism, and Absalom is a villain who believes wholeheartedly in his mission. Yet the novel isn’t just about the horrors of misogyny and religious fanaticism—it’s also about resistance. The women of Vardø refuse to be broken. They find strength in community, in storytelling, in the land itself. Maren’s journey is one of quiet defiance, a refusal to bow to the fear that Absalom tries to instill.

The novel’s pacing is deliberate, allowing the tension to build like the storm clouds over the horizon. The first half immerses us in the rhythms of Vardø life, making the later descent into paranoia and persecution all the more devastating. Hargrave’s writing is immersive, pulling the reader into a world where every glance, every whispered word, feels charged with meaning.

What makes *The Mercies* truly remarkable is its refusal to simplify its characters. Absalom is not a mustache-twirling villain; he’s a man who genuinely believes he’s saving souls. Ursa is not a helpless victim; she’s a woman trapped by her own ignorance and fear, but capable of growth. Maren is not a flawless heroine; she’s stubborn, sometimes reckless, and deeply human. This complexity makes the story resonate beyond its historical setting.

Ultimately, *The Mercies* is a story about the cost of freedom and the price of silence. It asks: How much will you give up to survive? How much will you fight to keep what’s yours? And when the world tries to tell you that your strength is a sin, will you listen?

Kiran Millwood Hargrave has written a novel that is as beautiful as it is brutal, as hopeful as it is heartbreaking. It’s a book that will stay with you long after the last page, like the echo of the Arctic wind.
