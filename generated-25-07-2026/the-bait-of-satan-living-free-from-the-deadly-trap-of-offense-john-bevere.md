# The Bait Of Satan: Living Free from the Deadly Trap of Offense

**Author:** John Bevere  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2014

John Bevere’s *The Bait of Satan: Living Free from the Deadly Trap of Offense* is a spiritual wake-up call disguised as a book—a book that doesn’t just challenge the reader but dismantles the very foundations of a mindset that has kept countless believers in chains. With over a million copies sold worldwide, Bevere doesn’t merely present a theory; he delivers a lifeline to those drowning in bitterness, suspicion, and self-justification. This isn’t just another self-help Christian book; it’s a prophetic confrontation with one of Satan’s most underrated yet devastating weapons: offense.

At its core, *The Bait of Satan* is about freedom—not the kind that comes from external circumstances changing, but the kind that erupts from within when a believer finally recognizes the trap they’ve been walking into for years. Bevere argues that offense is not just an emotional reaction but a spiritual snare, a bait Satan uses to lure believers away from God’s will, from unity, and from their destiny. The title itself is a masterstroke of metaphor: just as a fish sees a worm on a hook and swims toward it, unaware of the danger, so too do Christians often rush toward the "worm" of perceived injustice, only to find themselves hooked by resentment, division, and spiritual paralysis.

What makes Bevere’s approach so compelling is his unflinching honesty. He doesn’t soften the blow. He doesn’t offer platitudes like "just forgive and move on." Instead, he dissects the anatomy of offense with surgical precision, showing how it begins with a seed of hurt, grows into suspicion, and eventually blooms into full-blown bitterness. He pulls no punches when he writes, *"Offense is the devil’s favorite tool to keep you from fulfilling God’s purpose in your life."* This isn’t fear-mongering; it’s a hard truth that forces the reader to confront their own heart.

Bevere’s writing style is direct, declarative, and relentless—much like a prophet calling out sin in the camp. He doesn’t meander; he doesn’t dilute his message with fluff. Every chapter feels like a spiritual battle cry. His use of Scripture is not just illustrative but foundational, weaving biblical principles into a narrative that feels urgent and personal. He doesn’t just tell you to forgive; he explains *how* to break free from the cycle of offense by recognizing the lies that fuel it. One of the most powerful sections is where he dismantles the victim mentality, showing how clinging to the identity of being wronged actually gives the enemy legal ground in your life.

The book’s structure is both logical and convicting. Bevere starts by exposing the trap, then moves to the consequences of falling into it, and finally provides the keys to escape. His inclusion of real-life testimonies—people who have been set free from decades of bitterness—adds a layer of authenticity that mere theory could never achieve. These stories aren’t just anecdotes; they’re proof that this isn’t just a theoretical concept but a life-transforming truth.

One of the most striking aspects of Bevere’s style is his use of "declaration-style prayers." These aren’t passive affirmations; they’re bold, faith-filled declarations that challenge the reader to take ownership of their freedom. Phrases like *"I refuse to be a victim. I choose to forgive, not because they deserve it, but because I refuse to let offense control me"* are not just words on a page—they’re weapons against the enemy’s lies.

Bevere also doesn’t shy away from addressing the church’s role in this issue. He doesn’t let the body of Christ off the hook, pointing out how offense has fractured relationships, silenced ministries, and even kept people from stepping into their calling. His call to unity isn’t just sentimental; it’s a battle cry for the church to wake up and recognize how much ground it has lost to division.

The twentieth-anniversary edition of *The Bait of Satan* includes updated content, fresh testimonies, and even more urgency. It’s clear that Bevere hasn’t softened his message over the years—if anything, he’s sharpened it. The book doesn’t just speak to the individual; it speaks to the church, to families, and to leaders who are tired of seeing God’s people trapped in cycles of offense.

If there’s one criticism to be made, it’s that Bevere’s directness might come across as harsh to some readers. Those who are deeply entrenched in their grievances might feel defensive at first. But this is precisely why the book is so effective—it doesn’t coddle; it confronts. And in doing so, it offers a path to true freedom.

*The Bait of Satan* is more than a book; it’s a spiritual intervention. It doesn’t just tell you to let go of offense—it shows you how to recognize the bait before you take the hook. For anyone who has ever felt stuck in bitterness, for anyone who has replayed a conversation in their head a hundred times, for anyone who has wondered why they can’t seem to move forward in their faith—this book is a must-read.

John Bevere doesn’t just write about freedom; he equips you to walk in it.
