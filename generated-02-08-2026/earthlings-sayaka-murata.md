# Earthlings

**Author:** Sayaka Murata  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

Sayaka Murata’s *Earthlings* is a disorienting, hypnotic descent into the mind of a woman who believes she is an alien—a novel that blurs the line between surreal fantasy and brutal social critique. Like her earlier masterpiece *Convenience Store Woman*, Murata here dissects the suffocating expectations of modern life, but with a wild, almost hallucinatory intensity. Natsuki’s childhood conviction that she is from another planet isn’t just a quirky eccentricity; it’s a metaphor for the alienation of growing up in a world that demands conformity, happiness, and unquestioning participation in its rigid scripts. Murata doesn’t offer easy answers—she plunges us into the abyss of what it means to be an outsider, and whether rebellion is even possible in a society that punishes deviation with silence, shame, or worse.

The novel unfolds like a fever dream, shifting between Natsuki’s childhood in Nagano, where she bonds with a talking plush hedgehog named Piyyut (who claims to be a messenger from Popinpobopia), and her adulthood, where she lives a quiet, seemingly content life with her asexual husband—only to later spiral into a desperate, almost mythic quest for meaning. Murata’s prose is razor-sharp, oscillating between deadpan humor and unsettling grotesquery. A scene where Natsuki’s family dismisses her childhood fantasies with cold indifference is as chilling as it is revealing, exposing how society erases the strange, the different, the *other*. The hedgehog Piyyut, with his cryptic warnings and cosmic perspective, becomes a darkly comic foil to Natsuki’s growing paranoia, hinting at a universe where human norms are arbitrary and dangerous.

What makes *Earthlings* so compelling is its refusal to soften its edges. Murata doesn’t romanticize Natsuki’s alienation; she lays bare the cost of defiance in a world that rewards compliance. The novel’s climax is a visceral, almost mythic confrontation with the idea of happiness as a trap—a society that manufactures joy like a factory line, where dissent is met with violence or erasure. The final pages are jarring, brutal, and unforgettable, leaving the reader to grapple with whether Natsuki’s madness is a form of liberation or a tragic misunderstanding of the world.

Murata’s style is a masterclass in controlled chaos. She balances the absurd (a child who believes she’s an extraterrestrial) with the deeply human (the ache of not belonging, the fear of being left behind). The result is a novel that feels both intimate and vast, like a personal confession that suddenly expands into cosmic proportions. *Earthlings* isn’t just a story about an alien girl—it’s a meditation on how we all, in our own ways, feel like outsiders in a world that demands we fit.

For readers who loved the unsettling genius of *Convenience Store Woman*, this is Murata at her most daring. It’s a book that will linger in your mind long after the last page, a reminder that madness, like art, can be a form of truth. If you’re willing to embrace the weird, *Earthlings* is a revelatory, unsettling, and ultimately exhilarating experience.
