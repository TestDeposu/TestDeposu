# Perfect (Paradise, #2)

**Author:** Judith McNaught  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

Judith McNaught’s *Perfect*—the second installment in her Paradise series—delivers the lush, emotionally charged romance that has become her trademark, while threading a sharp corporate‑war backdrop that raises the stakes beyond the bedroom. Set against the glittering façade of Chicago’s storied Bancroft department‑store empire, the novel pits former lovers Matthew Farrell and Meredith Bancroft against each other in a high‑stakes takeover battle. Their history— a whirlwind marriage that began with electric chemistry and ended in a bitter betrayal— resurfaces with every boardroom glare and whispered office corridor encounter, turning a straightforward business duel into a battle of hearts as much as balance sheets.

The central theme of *Perfect* is the tension between ambition and vulnerability. McNaught explores how professional ruthlessness can mask deep‑seated insecurities, and how love, when rekindled after years of hurt, forces characters to confront the very parts of themselves they have tried to bury. Matthew, the self‑made “scruffy kid from steel‑town Indiana,” embodies the classic rags‑to‑riches archetype, yet his hardness is constantly softened by flashes of the tender man Meredith once knew. Meredith, poised and impeccably bred, struggles to reconcile her family legacy with her own desire for autonomy and genuine affection. Their push‑and‑pull dynamic fuels a narrative that is as much about reclaiming self‑trust as it is about rekindling passion.

McNaught’s style shines in her ability to fuse sensual detail with incisive social observation. Her prose is lush without being overwrought; she paints the opulence of Chicago’s elite— marble foyers, silk‑clad soirées, the scent of expensive perfume lingering in boardrooms— while simultaneously grounding the story in the gritty realism of Matthew’s Indiana roots. Dialogue crackles with wit and underlying tension, and the author’s trademark “slow‑burn” romance allows the attraction to simmer beneath corporate maneuvering before exploding in scenes that feel both inevitable and surprising. The pacing is deliberate: early chapters linger on the characters’ internal conflicts and the mechanics of the takeover, building a palpable sense of anticipation that pays off when the personal and professional worlds finally collide.

What elevates *Perfect* beyond a formulaic reunion romance is McNaught’s willingness to let her protagonists be flawed. Matthew’s vengeful drive and Meredith’s guarded pride are not merely obstacles to overcome; they are integral to the story’s emotional truth. The novel does not shy away from showing how past wounds can distort present judgments, nor does it offer easy forgiveness. Instead, it presents reconciliation as a hard‑won process that requires both parties to relinquish control— a theme that resonates strongly in today’s culture of high‑powered careers and personal sacrifice.

That said, the book is not without its minor shortcomings. The corporate intrigue, while effectively atmospheric, occasionally leans on familiar tropes— hostile takeovers, shadowy investors, and last‑minute rescues—that seasoned readers of the genre may anticipate. Additionally, some secondary characters, particularly members of the Bancroft family, feel somewhat underdeveloped, serving more as foils to the central romance than as fully realized individuals. These points, however, do not detract significantly from the overall enjoyment; they merely remind us that McNaught’s primary focus remains the emotional journey of her leads.

In sum, *Perfect* is a satisfying blend of sensual romance and corporate drama, showcasing Judith McNaught’s talent for weaving intimate character studies into a glittering, high‑stakes backdrop. Fans of her earlier works will recognize the familiar hallmarks— evocative settings, emotionally resonant dialogue, and a love story that feels both timeless and urgently contemporary— while newcomers will find an accessible entry point into her signature style. The novel reminds us that even in the cutthroat world of mergers and acquisitions, the most daring risk one can take is to open one’s heart again.
