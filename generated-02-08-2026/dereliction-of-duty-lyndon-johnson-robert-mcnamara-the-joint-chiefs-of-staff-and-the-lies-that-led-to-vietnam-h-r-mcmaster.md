# Dereliction of Duty: Lyndon Johnson, Robert McNamara, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, and the Lies That Led to Vietnam

**Author:** H.R. McMaster  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

The Vietnam War remains one of the most agonizing chapters in American history, a sprawling tragedy usually measured in jungle skirmishes, napalm, and the bitter protests of a divided home front. Yet, in his explosive and definitive work Dereliction of Duty, H.R. McMaster argues a far more radical and uncomfortable truth: the war was not lost in the humid paddies of Southeast Asia, but in the air-conditioned corridors of Washington, D.C. 

Published in 1997 and enduring as a modern classic, McMaster’s book shifts the lens entirely away from the battlefield and focuses it squarely upon the apex of American political and military power. The central theme of the book is a devastating autopsy of civilian control of the military gone catastrophically wrong. McMaster exposes how President Lyndon B. Johnson, Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara, and their inner circle orchestrated a systematic campaign of deception. They lied not only to the American public and Congress, but actively manipulated and marginalized the nation’s highest military authorities—the Joint Chiefs of Staff. 

McMaster, then an active-duty Army major and now a distinguished strategist, writes with a unique blend of scholarly rigor and institutional urgency. His style is meticulous, authoritative, and unsparing. Eschewing broad generalizations, McMaster builds his case upon a formidable mountain of primary sources, including declassified transcripts, personal diaries, and the stark records of high-stakes White House meetings. The narrative reads less like a dry historical text and more like a high-stakes political thriller, tracking a slow-motion catastrophe driven by hubris, careerism, and moral cowardice.

At the heart of the critique is a fascinating and tragic cast of characters. McNamara emerges as the quintessential technocrat, obsessed with quantifiable metrics and bureaucratic control, utterly blind to the human and political realities of guerrilla warfare. President Johnson, consumed by his vision for the Great Society domestic agenda, views foreign policy through the lens of political survival; he wants to avoid a messy war long enough to secure his presidency, yet fears the domestic fallout of appearing weak on communism. 

Most crucially, McMaster turns his analytical gaze onto the Joint Chiefs of Staff, finding them deeply culpable in their own marginalization. Bound by military subordination and fragmented by inter-service rivalries, the generals failed in their ultimate constitutional obligation. Rather than offering the unvarnished, professional military advice required to save the nation from a strategic quagmire, they compromised, acquiesced, and ultimately enabled a disastrous escalation. It is this specific failure of duty that gives the book its haunting title and its enduring relevance.

Dereliction of Duty is essential reading because it transcends its historical subject. It is not merely a post-mortem on Vietnam, but a timeless, cautionary tale about the dangers of institutional sycophancy, the perils of hubris in the halls of power, and the vital necessity of speaking truth to power when the stakes are life and death. McMaster has crafted a masterpiece of political and military history that demands to be read by anyone interested in leadership, accountability, and the heavy price of deception.
