# Submarine

**Author:** Joe Dunthorne  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2008

Hubris, hormones, and highly calculated melodrama: welcome to the delightfully murky waters of Joe Dunthorne’s 2008 debut, Submarine. In this sharp, fiercely funny coming-of-age novel, Dunthorne sidesteps the exhausted tropes of teenage angst to deliver something altogether rarer—a portrait of adolescence viewed through the lens of a protagonist who believes he is not merely living his life, but directing a cinematic masterpiece about it. 

At the center of this narrative is fifteen-year-old Oliver Tate, a self-anointed social scientist, cultural arbiter, and covert operative navigating the treacherous, hormonally charged landscape of a Welsh coastal town. Oliver is a boy of magnificent contradictions. He is simultaneously precocious and profoundly naive, clinically analytical and utterly drowning in teenage emotion. Dunthorne captures this dissonance with surgical precision, establishing a narrative voice that is both dead-on and uproarious. Oliver views the baffling adult world—and his peers—with the detached curiosity of an anthropologist, yet he remains entirely vulnerable to the petty humiliations and seismic heartbreaks unique to his age.

The novel’s brilliance lies in how seamlessly it balances two distinct, high-stakes missions: saving his parents’ teetering marriage and successfully deep-sixing his own virginity with Jordana Bevan, a classmate as brilliantly abrasive, pyromaniacally inclined, and compellingly quirky as Oliver himself. While lesser authors might treat these domestic and romantic plots as separate entities, Dunthorne weaves them together brilliantly. Oliver’s frantic attempts to sabotage his mother's baffling affair with a mystic neighbor named Graham run parallel to his agonizingly awkward courtship with Jordana. Through Oliver’s hyper-articulate and slightly devious perspective, mundane teenage struggles are elevated to epic, existential proportions. 

Dunthorne’s stylistic triumph in Submarine is his prose. It is preternaturally wise, dripping with dry British wit, and powered by a ferocious comic talent that immediately earned him well-deserved comparisons to literary heavyweights like Mark Haddon and Roddy Doyle. Yet, beneath the razor-sharp punchlines and Oliver’s meticulously curated vocabulary, there is a genuine, tender fondness for the awkwardness of the teenage male. Dunthorne does not mock his protagonist; instead, he invites us to laugh with Oliver while recognizing the universal, terrifying transition from the shores of childhood into the deep end of human experience. 

This is not merely a traditional coming-of-age tale; it is a brilliant reflection on what it means to possess an uncertain mind during a very certain, inflexible phase of life. Oliver Tate aims to damn the torpedoes and take the plunge into adulthood, and the result is an absolute win for contemporary fiction. Poised, poignant, and wildly entertaining, Submarine proves that the shallow end of youth is often where the deepest waters lie.
