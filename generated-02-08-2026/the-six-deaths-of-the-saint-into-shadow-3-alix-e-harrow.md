# The Six Deaths of the Saint (Into Shadow, #3)

**Author:** Alix E. Harrow  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2026

**Embracing the Shadows: A Review of "The Six Deaths of the Saint"**

Alix E. Harrow's latest collection, "The Six Deaths of the Saint," is a masterful weaving of historical reimaginations, speculative tales, and folkloric inspirations. The book's central theme, as suggested by its title, revolves around the concept of slantwise histories, a notion that the Harrow expertly explores through her diverse and captivating short stories.

From the opening pages, it becomes clear that Harrow's primary concern is to subvert traditional narratives, to take the familiar and twist it into unrecognizable forms. Her writing style is reminiscent of the great masters of the genre: Kafka, Borges, and Woolf, all of whom excelled at pushing the boundaries of language and form. Harrow's prose is equally innovative, blending elements of the fantastical, the surreal, and the historically grounded to create an unsettling, dreamlike atmosphere that is both disorienting and mesmerizing.

In "The Six Deaths of the Saint," we find stories that reimagine the past – medieval legends, folk tales, and historical events – while injecting them with a touch of speculative fiction. This blending of genres is both the strength and the weakness of the collection. On one hand, it allows Harrow to breathe new life into stale narratives, forcing us to see the familiar with fresh eyes. On the other hand, it sometimes renders the stories unevenly paced or jarringly disjointed.

A case in point is the title story, "The Six Deaths of the Saint." This tale, which forms the centerpiece of the collection, takes place in a world where the saints of old walk among us, their lives intertwined with ours in complex and mysterious ways. The story's use of multiple timelines and narrative threads is ingenious, but it also creates a dizzying effect that may leave some readers struggling to keep up.

However, even when the stories stumble, they are frequently rescued by Harrow's masterful storytelling and linguistic dexterity. Take, for instance, the delightful "Roadside Attraction," a tale of a mystical roadside diner where the staff can see into the hearts of their customers. This story showcases Harrow's ability to balance wit, humor, and pathos, creating a narrative that is both whimsical and profound.

Throughout the collection, Harrow's themes of memory, identity, and the power of storytelling resurface with uncanny regularity. Her characters often find themselves trapped in labyrinthine worlds, searching for clues that might lead them back to the present. This is more than just a metaphor for the search for meaning in life; it's an acknowledgment of the complex interplay between history, memory, and our own perceptions of reality.

Ultimately, "The Six Deaths of the Saint" is a book that defies easy summarization. Its stories are like shards of glass, each one reflecting a different aspect of the human experience, and none of them entirely coherent with the others. And yet, it is precisely this dissonance that makes the collection so compelling. For in the world of Alix E. Harrow, history is never quite fixed; it's always on the verge of slipping out of our grasp, like sand between our fingers.
