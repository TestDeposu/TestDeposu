# Sword of Destiny (The Witcher, #0.7)

**Author:** Andrzej Sapkowski  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

Sword of Destiny, the second collection of short stories in Andrzej Sapkowski’s Witcher saga, reads like a series of whispered legends told beside a flickering hearth—each tale sharp enough to cut, each character layered enough to haunt. Where The Last Wish introduced Geralt of Rivia as a lone monster‑slayer navigating a world that fears his very nature, Sword of Destiny deepens that portrait, turning the witcher from a mere hunter into a reluctant philosopher forced to confront the murky ethics of a continent teetering on the brink of war.

The central theme that pulses through every narrative is the tension between prejudice and empathy. Sapkowski repeatedly places Geralt in situations where the line between monster and man blurs: a striga cursed by royal betrayal, a doppler mimicking the visage of a beloved knight, a vampire who longs for art rather than blood. In each case, the witcher’s code—“evil is evil, lesser, greater, middling; it’s all the same”—is tested, not by the beasts he hunts, but by the humans who label them. The stories ask whether a creature’s actions stem from innate wickedness or from the cruelty inflicted upon them by a fearful society. Geralt, stoic and scarred, becomes the reluctant arbiter of that question, his silver sword a metaphor for the razor‑thin edge upon which justice balances.

Sapkowski’s style is a masterclass in economy and atmosphere. He writes in short, punchy sentences that echo the cadence of oral folklore, yet each line is laced with vivid, often brutal imagery. Descriptions are sparse but potent: a moonlit forest “where the trees seemed to hold their breath,” a tavern’s stale air “thick with the smell of cheap ale and desperation.” This minimalism allows the reader’s imagination to fill the gaps, making the horrors feel intimate and immediate. Dialogue crackles with wit and world‑weary sarcasm; Geralt’s dry remarks often serve as both comic relief and incisive commentary on the hypocrisy of kings, priests, and peasants alike. The author slips Slavic myth into the narrative like hidden runes—leshy, vilas, and strigas feel both authentically foreign and eerily familiar—grounding the fantasy in a cultural texture that distinguishes the Witcher universe from more generic high‑fantasy fare.

What truly distinguishes Sword of Destiny from its predecessor is the way Sapkowski weaves the notion of destiny itself into the fabric of each tale. Geralt constantly grapples with prophecies that seem to mock his agency: the Law of Surprise, the enigmatic prophecies of the elf Ithlinne, the ever‑looming shadow of Cintra’s fall. Yet rather than submitting to fate, Geralt’s choices—often small, compassionate acts—redefine what destiny means for him. In “A Shard of Ice,” he spares a young girl cursed to become a beast, recognizing that mercy can rewrite a foretold doom. In “The Edge of the World,” he aids a group of refugees, realizing that survival sometimes hinges on the willingness to defy prescribed paths. These moments suggest that Sapkowski views destiny not as an immutable script but as a river that can be redirected by individual will, a theme resonant with modern readers navigating their own societal pressures.

The collection also excels in its tonal shifts. While some stories lean into grim horror—think of the claustrophobic dread in “The Lesser Evil,” where a witcher must decide which evil to unleash—others flirt with romance and dark comedy, as seen in the playful banter between Geralt and the sorceress Yennefer in “A Question of Price.” This tonal versatility prevents the book from feeling monolithic; instead, it mirrors the unpredictable nature of the Continent itself, where beauty and brutality coexist in uneasy harmony.

Critics might argue that the short‑story format limits deep character arcs, but Sapkowski sidesteps this limitation by using each vignette as a lens through which we see facets of Geralt’s personality: his honor, his loneliness, his capacity for love, and his simmering frustration with a world that refuses to see him as anything more than a tool. The supporting cast—Dandelion’s flamboyant bardism, Yarpen Zigrin’s dwarven pragmatism, Milva’s fierce loyalty—emerge fully realized despite their limited page time, enriching the tapestry of the Witcher world.

Ultimately, Sword of Destiny is more than a bridge between The Last Wish and the full‑length novels; it is a philosophical inquiry cloaked in fantasy. Sapkowski’s terse, lyrical prose invites readers to question the nature of monstrosity, the weight of prejudice, and the power of choice in shaping one’s fate. For newcomers, it offers an accessible yet profound entry point; for veterans, it reaffirms why Geralt’s saga continues to captivate millions across books, games, and screen. The collection lingers long after the final page, echoing the witcher’s own refrain: “Maybe it’s better to be a monster than to pretend you’re human when you’re not.”
