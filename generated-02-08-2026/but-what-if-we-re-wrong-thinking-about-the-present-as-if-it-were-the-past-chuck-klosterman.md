# But What If We're Wrong? Thinking About the Present As If It Were the Past

**Author:** Chuck Klosterman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

History is a massive graveyard of absolute certainty. That is the unsettling, exhilarating premise of Chuck Klosterman’s 2016 thought-experiment, But What If We’re Wrong? Thinking About the Present As If It Were the Past. For decades, Klosterman has established himself as our preeminent cultural provocateur, a writer uniquely equipped to dissect the absurdities of modern media, music, and society. In this volume, however, he zooms out from the microscopic trends of pop culture to confront a terrifyingly vast macro-question: How will our current, unshakeable convictions look to the inhabitants of the twenty-second, twenty-fifth, or thirtieth centuries?

Klosterman operates not as a traditional historian or speculative scientist, but as a cultural archaeologist excavating our present moment while we are still standing squarely inside it. The book’s core theme is epistemic humility—the shocking likelihood that almost everything we "know" to be true, permanent, or objectively valuable today is fundamentally flawed. To explore this, Klosterman slingshots across an eclectic landscape of subjects, asking whether gravity will eventually be disproven, whether democracy will someday be viewed as an archaic governance fad, and which contemporary rock musician will be remembered five hundred years from now (hint: it probably isn't the one dominating the charts today). 

What makes the book compelling is how Klosterman avoids dry academic abstraction by grounding these cosmic uncertainties in accessible, deeply entertaining cultural touchstones. He structures his inquiries through a series of high-wire interviews with an impressive roster of creative and scientific heavyweights—including theoretical physicists like Brian Greene and Neil deGrasse Tyson, novelists like George Saunders and Junot Díaz, and musicians like David Byrne. Yet, these luminaries are not deployed to provide definitive answers; rather, they serve as sparring partners for Klosterman’s relentless, sideways logic. He uses their expertise to corner our most deeply ingrained biases, forcing us to confront the temporal provincialism that assumes our current epoch is the absolute pinnacle of human evolution.

Klosterman’s signature style is on full display here: conversational yet razor-sharp, intellectually rigorous yet devoid of pretentious jargon. He possesses a rare gift for making existential dread feel like a fun Saturday afternoon conversation at a dive bar. His prose is laced with self-deprecating wit and uncanny pop-cultural fluency, treating the works of Kafka and the trajectory of the National Football League with the exact same level of fascinated, analytical gravity. He doesn't lecture the reader; instead, he invites us into the uncomfortable realization that our grandchildren will likely look back at our most cherished certainties with the same gentle, patronizing amusement we reserve for medieval doctors applying leeches.

Ultimately, But What If We’re Wrong? is less about predicting the future than it is about curing our collective present-day arrogance. It is a brilliant, disorienting mental exercise that rattles the cage of modern dogmatism. Klosterman leaves us with a lingering, productive paranoia: If we are almost certainly wrong about almost everything, how should that realization change the way we live, create, and think right now? For anyone who loves ideas that gently tilt the axis of their worldview, this book is an absolute must-read.
