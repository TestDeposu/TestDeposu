Wodehouse’s style here is a masterclass in controlled chaos. His prose dances on the edge of absurdity through Bertie’s frantic, digressive voice ("I’ve gone off the rails... Hold the line a minute"), layered with Jeeves’ deadpan erudition ("The aquatic members of the family Salamandridae which constitute the genus Molge"). The humor arises from the chasm between Bertie’s melodramatic self-perception ("I am inclined to think that there must be some mistake... the chap I know wears horn-rimmed spectacles and has a face like a fish") and the mundane reality of Gussie’s newt-induced social paralysis. Wodehouse weaponizes bathos: a crisis of epic proportions (a marriage proposal!) hinges on the study of amphibians and the propriety of a mess-jacket. The dialogue sparkles with rhythmic repetition ("Yes, sir," "Precisely, sir"), faux-philosophical musings on Life’s banana skins, and the sheer joie de vivre of linguistic play—all while dissecting English class anxieties and the terror of romantic vulnerability beneath a veneer of whimsy. This is not spiritual fervor; it is the comedy of manners perfected, where the stakes feel universe-shatteringly high to the characters yet are delightfully trivial to the reader.

Critiquing this as a *Jesus Freaks* piece misses the point entirely. The excerpt’s power lies in its relentless focus on human frailty viewed through a lens of affectionate mockery—not divine courage. Gussie’s struggle isn’t with faith but with crippling shyness exacerbated by an eccentric hobby; his "freakishness" is social awkwardness, not holy boldness. Madeline Bassett’s idealism ("the stars are God’s daisy-chain") is treated as a charming quirk that paralyzes Bertie’s wit, not a shared spiritual bond. Jeeves resolves everything through practical intervention (recommending Gussie to Jeeves via Sipperley), not prayer or testimony. To force a Christian narrative here is to ignore Wodehouse’s core achievement: finding profundity in the ridiculous, and grace in the meticulous observance of social ritual (even if that ritual involves newt-watching). The true "freak" celebrated is not the martyr, but the utterly ordinary soul navigating life’s absurdities with a bit of help from their friends—and an impeccable valet.
