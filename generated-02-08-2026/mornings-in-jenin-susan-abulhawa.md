# Mornings in Jenin

**Author:** Susan Abulhawa  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

**Mornings in Jenin: A Novel That Burns with the Unyielding Spirit of Palestine**

Susan Abulhawa’s *Mornings in Jenin* is not merely a novel—it is a searing, poetic testament to resilience, a family saga that unfolds against the brutal backdrop of Zionist colonization and the relentless dispossession of Palestine. With a prose that is both lyrical and unflinching, Abulhawa weaves together generations of the Al-Araj family, tracing their journey from the fertile lands of Ein Hod to the besieged refugee camps of Jenin, the shattered streets of Jerusalem, the exile of Lebanon, and the hollow anonymity of America—only to return, time and again, to the unbroken promise of home. This is a story that refuses to be silenced, a novel that lingers in the mind like the scent of olive trees after a storm.

At its core, *Mornings in Jenin* is a meditation on survival. It is the story of a people who have been erased from maps, whose names have been scrubbed from history, yet whose spirit remains indomitable. Abulhawa does not offer easy answers or neat resolutions; instead, she presents a raw, unvarnished portrait of loss, resistance, and the quiet defiance of those who refuse to be broken. The novel spans decades, from the early 20th century to the present, but its power lies not in chronological precision but in its emotional immediacy. Each chapter is a blow to the heart—whether it’s the quiet despair of a mother watching her son disappear into the Israeli military’s grip, the fury of a father who refuses to kneel, or the quiet dignity of a grandmother who remembers the land even as her people are scattered.

Abulhawa’s prose is a masterclass in restraint and intensity. She does not indulge in melodrama; instead, she crafts sentences that are sharp as a knife, each word carrying the weight of centuries of struggle. There is a musicality to her writing, a rhythm that mirrors the cadence of Palestinian oral tradition—stories passed down through generations, whispered in the dark, sung in defiance. Yet beneath the beauty of her language lies a searing truth: the novel is a reckoning. It does not shy away from the horrors of occupation, the brutality of displacement, or the psychological toll of exile. But it also refuses to let the reader look away. Through the eyes of the Al-Arajs, we witness the birth of a nation, its theft, and the unshakable belief that one day, it will rise again.

The novel’s strength lies in its humanity. Abulhawa does not reduce her characters to symbols of struggle; they are flesh and blood, flawed and fierce, loving and angry. There is the fierce intelligence of Layla, the young woman who becomes a symbol of resistance; the quiet strength of her mother, who carries the weight of memory; the defiance of her father, who refuses to be cowed. Even in exile, in the sterile landscapes of America, the family’s connection to Palestine remains unbroken—a thread that pulls them back, again and again, to the land they cannot forget.

What makes *Mornings in Jenin* so devastatingly relevant is its refusal to let the reader forget. In an era where Palestinian voices are often drowned out by propaganda and political rhetoric, Abulhawa’s novel cuts through the noise. It is a story that demands to be heard, that forces the reader to confront the humanity of those who have been reduced to statistics, to footnotes in a history written by conquerors. It is a novel that reminds us that behind every policy, every border, every checkpoint, there are real people—mothers, fathers, children—who dream of return, who refuse to accept erasure.

The novel’s title itself is a metaphor. The mornings in Jenin are not just the hours before dawn; they are the moments of quiet defiance, the small acts of resistance that keep hope alive. Abulhawa’s work is a celebration of those mornings, a testament to the fact that even in the darkest nights, the sun will rise again.

*Mornings in Jenin* is more than a novel; it is a cry of the soul. It is a book that will haunt you long after the last page, a story that you will return to, again and again, because it is not just about Palestine—it is about the unbreakable human spirit, about the refusal to surrender, about the quiet fire that burns in every person who has ever known displacement, who has ever loved a land that was not theirs to keep.

If you read one book this year that will change how you see the world, let it be this one. Let it be *Mornings in Jenin*.
