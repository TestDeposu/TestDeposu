# The Vast Fields of Ordinary

**Author:** Nick Burd  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

**The Vast Fields of Ordinary: A Luminous, Heart-Wrenching Exploration of Love and Loss**

Nick Burd’s *The Vast Fields of Ordinary* is a novel that lingers like a half-remembered dream—beautiful in its fragility, haunting in its inevitability. Winner of the ALA Stonewall Book Award and a *New York Times* Notable Book, this coming-of-age story is less about the grand gestures of adolescence and more about the quiet, suffocating weight of growing up in a world that feels both vast and impossibly small. Burd’s prose is a masterclass in restraint and emotional precision, weaving together the mundane and the transcendent with a poet’s eye. The result is a book that feels both intimate and expansive, a meditation on love, identity, and the ways in which we sabotage our own happiness.

At its core, *The Vast Fields of Ordinary* is a story about Dade, a young man adrift in the suburban wasteland of his own life. His existence is a series of dead-end jobs, a toxic relationship with a partner who reduces him to a prop, and the slow unraveling of his parents’ marriage—each a symptom of a larger disillusionment with the world. Burd doesn’t romanticize Dade’s despair; instead, he renders it with brutal honesty, showing how easily hope can be crushed under the weight of expectation and circumstance. Yet, it is in this very ordinariness that the novel’s magic lies. Burd doesn’t seek to elevate Dade’s struggles into something extraordinary; he makes them feel universal, a testament to the quiet rebellion of those who refuse to be defined by their circumstances.

The turning point comes when Dade meets Alex Kincaid, a figure as enigmatic as he is transformative. Alex is the first person who sees Dade—not as a failure, not as a burden, but as someone worthy of love. Their relationship is electric, a fleeting moment of clarity in a life that has felt otherwise gray. Burd’s portrayal of this romance is both tender and dangerous, a reminder that love, even in its purest form, is never without consequence. The novel’s tragedy isn’t just that love is fleeting; it’s that love, when it arrives, forces us to confront the chaos of our own lives. Alex becomes a catalyst, exposing the cracks in Dade’s world and setting in motion a chain of events that will reshape him forever.

What makes *The Vast Fields of Ordinary* so compelling is Burd’s ability to balance the mundane with the profound. The novel’s setting—a suburban landscape of strip malls and empty driveways—feels like a character in itself, a place where dreams go to die. Yet, Burd doesn’t wallow in cynicism. Instead, he finds beauty in the overlooked: in the way sunlight filters through trees, in the way a song can suddenly make everything feel possible, in the way a single moment of connection can feel like salvation. His writing is lyrical without being pretentious, each sentence carefully crafted to evoke both the beauty and the brutality of existence.

The novel’s structure mirrors its themes. Burd doesn’t rush the story; he allows it to unfold at the pace of a slow, inevitable tide. There are no grand revelations, no neat resolutions—just the quiet, devastating truth that life is often a series of small, irreversible choices. The tragedy isn’t just that Dade’s love story ends in heartbreak; it’s that the heartbreak is a necessary part of his growth, a price paid for the brief, intoxicating taste of something real.

Burd’s style is a study in contrast. He juxtaposes the raw, unfiltered emotions of his characters with a prose that is cool and controlled, almost clinical at times. This distance allows the reader to experience the story’s intensity without being overwhelmed, creating a sense of detachment that is both unsettling and deeply moving. It’s a technique that serves the novel’s themes well, reminding us that even in our most desperate moments, we are still observers of our own lives.

Ultimately, *The Vast Fields of Ordinary* is a book about the search for meaning in a world that often feels indifferent. It’s a story about the ways in which we create our own narratives, even when the world around us seems determined to write a different one. Burd doesn’t offer easy answers; he doesn’t promise that love will save us or that happiness is just around the corner. Instead, he offers something far more valuable: the acknowledgment that our struggles are real, our desires are valid, and our pain is worth bearing.

For readers who have ever felt adrift, who have ever loved someone with a love that was both their salvation and their undoing, this novel will resonate like a long-lost truth. It’s a book that doesn’t just tell a story; it makes you feel the weight of it, the beauty of it, the tragedy of it. In the vast fields of ordinary life, Burd finds something extraordinary—proof that even in the most ordinary moments, we are capable of profound, life-altering love.
