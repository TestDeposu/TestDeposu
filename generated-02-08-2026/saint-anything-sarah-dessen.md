# Saint Anything

**Author:** Sarah Dessen  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

Saint Anything invites readers into the quiet ache of a girl who has spent her life in the shadow of a charismatic, self‑destructive brother. When Peyton’s drunk‑driving crash lands him behind bars, Sydney is left to navigate the sudden vacuum of attention—and the unsettling freedom of being truly on her own. Sarah Dessen handles this premise with her trademark blend of keen observation and tender honesty, turning a familiar YA setup into a nuanced exploration of how we inherit both the burdens and the blessings of those we love.

The novel’s central theme revolves around visibility and belonging. Sydney’s feeling of invisibility isn’t just a teenage gripe; it’s a profound existential question about worth when the family narrative is rewritten by another’s mistake. Dessen shows how Sydney’s quest for recognition leads her to the Chatham household—a boisterous, imperfect clan that offers acceptance without conditions. Through Layla’s relentless optimism, Rosie’s hard‑won resilience, and Mrs. Chatham’s quiet strength, the Chathams become a surrogate family that teaches Sydney that love can be chosen as fiercely as it is born. Mac, the watchful older brother, embodies the novel’s gentle promise: being truly seen can be both a rescue and a catalyst for growth.

Dessen’s style shines in the delicate balance she strikes between interiority and external detail. Her prose flows with a conversational ease that makes Sydney’s voice feel immediate and authentic, yet she layers it with subtle metaphors—light filtering through cracked windows, the rhythm of a kitchen bustling with life—that echo the protagonist’s shifting sense of self. The dialogue crackles with teenage spontaneity, never slipping into caricature, and the pacing allows moments of silence to resonate as loudly as the conversations. Rather than relying on melodrama, Dessen lets the emotional weight accumulate in small, telling gestures: a shared meal, a hesitant touch, a lingering glance. This restraint makes the eventual breakthroughs feel earned, not forced.

What sets Saint Anything apart from many of Dessen’s earlier works is its willingness to sit with ambiguity. The novel refuses a tidy redemption arc for Peyton; instead, it asks readers to consider how guilt, forgiveness, and the possibility of change coexist in a fractured family. Sydney’s journey is less about finding a definitive answer than about learning to live with the questions—a stance that feels especially resonant in today’s climate of mental‑health awareness and redefined notions of kinship.

Overall, Saint Anything is a poignant, character‑driven testament to the power of chosen bonds and the quiet courage required to step out of another’s shadow. Dessen’s empathetic storytelling and keen eye for the everyday make this novel a compelling read for anyone who has ever wondered where they fit when the world seems to be looking elsewhere.
