# The Autobiography of Henry VIII: With Notes by His Fool, Will Somers

**Author:** Margaret George  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

History has long cast Henry VIII as a monstrous caricature—a bloated tyrant who danced through wives, shattered the religious fabric of a nation, and left a trail of political terror in his wake. Yet, beneath the heavy velvet, the jewels, and the blood-soaked crown lies the beating heart of a man haunted by his own mortality and an obsessive, desperate longing for a male heir. In The Autobiography of Henry VIII: With Notes by His Fool, Will Somers, author Margaret George achieves a staggering literary feat: she strips away the myth of the Tudor monarch and replaces it with a breathing, flawed, fiercely human voice. 

The crowning triumph of George’s novel lies in its daring structural choice. Written as the private, posthumously discovered journals of the king himself, the narrative grants readers unprecedented intimacy with a monarch at the height of his power and the depths of his despair. This is not a detached historical recounting; it is a raw, impassioned defense written from the inside out. Henry speaks directly to us—justifying his divorces, lamenting his lost sons, agonizing over the execution of Thomas More, and wrestling with the monumental weight of his divine right. We witness the transformation of a golden Renaissance prince, athletic and charismatic, into the ailing, paranoid tyrant of popular imagination. But because we walk beside him through every triumph and heartbreak, we understand the terror and the loneliness that forged him.

To counterbalance the heavy, self-aggrandizing introspection of the king, George employs a brilliant narrative foil: Will Somers, the royal jester. Interspersed throughout Henry’s grand memoirs are Will’s acerbic, witty, and devastatingly clear-eyed marginal notes. Where Henry sees a strategic political maneuver, Will sees petty cruelty. Where the king laments his burden of state, the fool highlights the collateral damage left in his wake. This clever juxtaposition prevents the novel from becoming a mere apologia for a despot. Will Somers provides the moral compass and the satirical sting, grounding Henry’s grandiosity in the messy, painful reality of Tudor England. 

Margaret George’s stylistic brilliance is rooted in her staggering fifteen years of meticulous research. Every page breathes with the authentic textures of the sixteenth century—the scent of roasting meats and burning incense, the intricate dance of European diplomacy, the terrifying volatility of the royal court, and the archaic rhythms of Tudor speech without ever sacrificing readability. She captures the duality of Henry’s character with breathtaking nuance: he is a poet, a musician, a theologian, and a ruthless politician all at once. He loves deeply, yet destroys ruthlessly. 

Ultimately, The Autobiography of Henry VIII is more than a historical novel; it is a masterclass in character study and psychological depth. George dares us to look past the six wives, the severed heads, and the ruined monasteries to find the terrified boy who just wanted to please his father and leave a lasting dynasty. Riveting, monumental, and brilliantly conceived, this modern classic proves that the most fascinating monster in history is the one who believes he is doing God’s work.
