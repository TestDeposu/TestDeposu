"The Poetic Edda: Stories of the Norse Gods and Heroes" arrives as a venerable conduit between the pagan world of early Scandinavia and the scholarly curiosity of modern readers. At its core, the Edda is not merely a collection of myths; it is a cultural artifact that encodes the ethical framework of a society poised on the brink of Christian conversion. The translator’s choice to retain the original alliterative verse, rather than smoothing it into free‑verse prose, preserves the incantatory quality that made the Edda a performance piece in the mead‑halls of old. Stylistically, the work balances two competing demands: fidelity to the source and accessibility for a contemporary audience. The translator achieves this by maintaining the kennings—those dense, metaphorical compounds like “wave‑steed” for ship or “battle‑sweat” for blood—that give Norse poetry its distinctive texture, while supplying brief, unobtrusive footnotes that clarify obscure references without breaking the poem’s flow. This approach respects the poet’s craftsmanship, allowing readers to sense the original’s auditory appeal even as they navigate the semantic distance between Old Norse and modern English.
