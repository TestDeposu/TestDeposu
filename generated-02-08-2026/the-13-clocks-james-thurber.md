# The 13 Clocks

**Author:** James Thurber  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

Time, that relentless and orderly march, is the first casualty in James Thurber’s peerless masterpiece, The 13 Clocks. In the gloomy, echoing halls of a lonely castle, a wicked Duke has murdered time, freezing all thirteen clocks at ten minutes to five forever. He does so out of a chilling paranoia, desperate to thwart any prince who might dare to win the hand of the Princess Saralinda, whose touch remains the only warmth in a fortress of absolute ice. But fairy tales, as Thurber brilliantly reminds us, possess a stubborn elasticity of their own.

Long celebrated by literary luminaries—Neil Gaiman famously dubbed it “probably the best book in the world”—this enchanting hybrid of high whimsy and dark folklore defies easy categorization. While ostensibly a children’s book, Thurber’s 1950 creation operates on a frequency that is profoundly sophisticated, balancing whimsical nonsense with a sharp, literary bite. 

At the heart of The 13 Clocks lies the powerful theme of temporal tyranny versus the persistent warmth of human agency. The Duke is a terrifyingly brilliant villain, a tyrant who fancies himself the master of existence simply because he can control the aesthetic of the passing hours. Yet time, much like love, cannot truly be slain by malice. When the bold Prince Zorn arrives disguised as a minstrel, aided by the wonderfully enigmatic and eccentric Golux, the narrative transforms into an exquisite dance of impossible tasks, shadowed corridors, and poetic justice. 

Thurber’s authorial style is nothing short of alchemy. He weaves a spell using language that feels both ancestral and startlingly modern. His prose is musical, rhythmic, and dripping with delicious alliteration and inventive vocabulary. Words like "glorp," "toddy," and "Todal"—a faceless, gelatinous terror that creeps about listening for sighs—demonstrate a linguistic playfulness that rivals Lewis Carroll. Yet, beneath the linguistic pyrotechnics lies a subtle melancholy, a lingering twilight mood that gives the narrative its profound emotional resonance. Thurber, celebrated primarily as a giant of American humor and a pillar of The New Yorker, poured his own anxieties, eyesight struggles, and profound imagination into this singular fairy tale, resulting in a work that sparkles with bittersweet brilliance.

This definitive edition is further elevated by Marc Simont’s original, full-color illustrations, which capture the eerie, gothic beauty of the Duke’s domain and the sparkling defiance of its heroes. The artwork does not merely accompany the text; it breathes alongside it, anchoring Thurber's surreal flights of fancy in a tangible, visual wonder.

To step into The 13 Clocks is to enter a dreamscape where logic bends, humor reigns, and the coldest hearts are eventually forced to thaw. It is a triumphant reminder that even when time is broken and tasks seem impossible, a clever mind, a brave spirit, and a little bit of magic can set the universe back in motion. Essential, timeless, and utterly bewitching, this is a classic that continues to cast its spell long after the final page is turned.
