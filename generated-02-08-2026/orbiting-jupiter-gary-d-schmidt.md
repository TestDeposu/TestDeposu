# Orbiting Jupiter

**Author:** Gary D. Schmidt  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2015

Gary D. Schmidt’s *Orbiting Jupiter* is a searing, lyrical exploration of love in its most raw and unflinching form—a story where family isn’t defined by blood but by the quiet, relentless devotion that binds two broken boys together. At its heart, this novel is a meditation on sacrifice, the weight of the past, and the fragile, fragile hope that can emerge from the deepest wounds. Schmidt, a master of emotional precision, crafts a narrative that is both devastating and deeply moving, proving once again why he is one of the most compelling voices in contemporary YA literature.

The story follows Jack, a twelve-year-old narrator whose life takes an unexpected turn when he is placed in a foster home with Joseph, a thirteen-year-old father who has never seen his daughter, Jupiter. Joseph is a boy haunted by his past—by a violent act that sent him to a juvenile facility, by the silence of his own mother, and by the gnawing, inescapable need to reclaim the child he never knew. Jack, initially wary of this volatile, intense boy, gradually comes to understand Joseph’s desperation. But what begins as a tentative bond soon deepens into something far more profound: a brotherhood forged in the crucible of shared trauma and mutual sacrifice.

Schmidt’s genius lies in his ability to strip away the veneer of conventional coming-of-age stories, exposing instead the messy, often brutal realities of adolescence. Joseph is not a hero; he is a boy who has been shaped by cruelty, by neglect, and by the crushing weight of responsibility. His love for Jupiter is not romanticized—it is a love that borders on obsession, one that demands everything from him, including his own redemption. Jack, on the other hand, is a more traditional protagonist in some ways: curious, observant, and deeply empathetic. Yet even he is not immune to the novel’s emotional toll. As the two boys navigate their newfound connection, they must confront the ghosts of their pasts, the lies they’ve told themselves, and the sacrifices that will be required to move forward.

The prose is Schmidt’s signature—simple, unadorned, yet rich with subtext. His sentences carry the weight of unsaid words, the kind of quiet power that lingers long after the last page. There are no grand gestures here; instead, Schmidt trusts his readers to feel the ache in the spaces between lines. A single sentence can convey years of silence, a glance can speak volumes, and the tension between Joseph and Jack is palpable, a dance of trust and betrayal that unfolds with heartbreaking inevitability.

What makes *Orbiting Jupiter* so unforgettable is its unflinching honesty. Schmidt does not shy away from the darkness—from the violence, the despair, the moments of profound loneliness. But neither does he dwell in it. Instead, he balances these heavy themes with moments of unexpected tenderness, of small, human connections that remind us of the capacity for love even in the most broken of souls. The relationship between Joseph and Jack is the novel’s beating heart, a testament to the idea that family is not something we are born into, but something we choose, again and again, despite the cost.

The novel’s title, *Orbiting Jupiter*, is a metaphor for the way Joseph’s life has been defined by his daughter—how he has revolved around her, even from a distance, even when he couldn’t see her. But it also speaks to the way Jack, and the reader, are drawn into Joseph’s orbit, pulled into his story, his pain, and ultimately, his redemption. The title is poetic, but it is also a reminder that love, in all its forms, is a force that can pull us toward both destruction and salvation.

At its core, *Orbiting Jupiter* is a story about the sacrifices we make for those we love. Joseph’s love for Jupiter is a love that demands everything—his freedom, his dignity, his very sense of self. Jack’s love for Joseph, in turn, is a love that requires him to confront his own fears, his own limitations, and the ways in which he has been complicit in the cycles of pain that surround him. Schmidt never romanticizes these sacrifices; he presents them as they are—hard, painful, and sometimes impossible. But he also shows us that they are necessary, that they are the only way forward.

In the end, *Orbiting Jupiter* is a novel that will stay with you long after you’ve turned the last page. It is a story of found family, of the bonds that can be formed in the most unlikely of places, and of the quiet, unshakable love that can emerge from the deepest wounds. Schmidt’s writing is both a balm and a blade, a reminder of the beauty and brutality of human connection. It is a book that demands to be read, not just for its emotional power, but for its unflinching honesty and its profound understanding of what it means to love—and to be loved—in a world that often seems indifferent.
