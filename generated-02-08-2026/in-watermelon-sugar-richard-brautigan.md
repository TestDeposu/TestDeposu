# In Watermelon Sugar

**Author:** Richard Brautigan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1968

Richard Brautigan’s 1968 masterpiece, In Watermelon Sugar, is not merely a novel; it is a delicate, fragile artifact of the counterculture generation, salvaged from the ruins of modern anxiety and rebuilt out of pure, pastel-hued imagination. Set in a utopian commune where the sun shines a different color every day and reality is quite literally constructed from watermelon sugar, Brautigan crafts a post-apocalyptic pastoral that feels both impossibly light and devastatingly melancholic. 

At the heart of the novel lies the commune of iDEATH, a place where people live gentle, measured lives, rejecting the violence, noise, and industrialized fury of the past. That past is embodied by the Forgotten Works, a decaying monument to the old world where tigers once spoke to humans before being murdered and turned into lampshades. Brautigan’s central theme explores the human desire to completely wipe the slate clean—to invent a new language, a new architecture, and a new emotional baseline removed from historical trauma. Yet, iDEATH is not a flawless paradise. Beneath the surface of watermelon sugar lies an inescapable undercurrent of sadness, suicide, and detachment. The residents of iDEATH have traded the violent passions of the old gang for a serene, almost numb emotional equilibrium, raising the profound question: how much of our humanity do we sacrifice when we finally achieve peace?

Brautigan’s literary style is the true magic of the book. Written with deceptive simplicity, his prose reads like fairy tales for adults who have survived the 1960s. His sentences are short, declarative, and rhythmically hypnotic, stripping away literary artifice to reveal a stark, poetic innocence. There is an effortless surrealism to his world-building, where things made of watermelon sugar coexist with everyday melancholy, creating a dreamscape that feels entirely tangible. Brautigan captures the exact mood of the late-sixties counterculture—its yearning for communal harmony, its rejection of corporate and political machinery, and its tragic awareness that utopias are inherently fragile.

In Watermelon Sugar remains a striking, haunting critique of escapism and memory. It challenges readers to examine what we build our lives upon when the old world fails us. Brautigan does not offer easy answers; instead, he invites us into a world where everything is sweet, soft, and heartbreakingly temporary. It is a brilliant, timeless trip into the avant-garde, proving that sometimes the quietest voices leave the most enduring echoes.
