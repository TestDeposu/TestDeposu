# Age of Myth (The Legends of the First Empire, #1)

**Author:** Michael J. Sullivan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

**Age of Myth: A Mythic Reckoning That Redefines Fantasy’s Future**

Michael J. Sullivan’s *Age of Myth* isn’t just another fantasy epic—it’s a bold, mythic reinvention of the genre, where gods are not distant deities but flawed, mortal-like figures whose fall reshapes the world in ways both brutal and breathtaking. This first installment of *The Legends of the First Empire* doesn’t just tell a story; it *rewrites the rules* of divine power, destiny, and human defiance, blending the grandeur of *The Wheel of Time* with the gritty, character-driven depth of *The Lies of Locke Lamora*. Sullivan, a master of immersive worldbuilding and morally complex protagonists, delivers a tale where the gods themselves are the villains—and the humans are the only ones left to burn them down.

At its core, *Age of Myth* is a story about the illusion of divinity. For centuries, humans have worshipped the Fhrey, a pantheon of near-immortal beings who command magic, war, and fate with godlike authority. But when one of them is slain by a human hand, the cosmic order fractures. The gods are not invincible. They are not benevolent. And their downfall is just the beginning of a war that will decide whether humanity survives—or becomes the next extinct species. Sullivan’s genius lies in his ability to make the divine feel *human*. The Fhrey are arrogant, vengeful, and tragically flawed, their powers a double-edged sword that ensures their own undoing. This isn’t just a fantasy about gods falling; it’s a story about the hubris of power, the cost of belief, and the quiet rebellion of those who refuse to kneel.

The book’s three central characters—Raithe, Suri, and Persephone—each embody a different facet of this rebellion. Raithe, the reluctant God Killer, is a man haunted by his own destiny, his blade forged in the blood of the divine. His journey is one of reluctant heroism, where every strike against the gods is both a victory and a moral compromise. Suri, the seer burdened by visions of doom, represents the weight of prophecy and the terror of knowing the future before it happens. Her struggle is internal, a battle against fate itself, and Sullivan handles her perspective with a delicate balance of dread and hope. Then there’s Persephone, whose personal tragedy fuels her determination to lead her people—not as a queen, but as a survivor. Together, they form a trio that feels earned, flawed, and deeply human, their arcs intertwined with the larger mythos in a way that avoids the pitfalls of forced destiny tropes.

Sullivan’s prose is a masterclass in atmospheric storytelling. His descriptions are vivid yet uncluttered, painting a world where magic isn’t just spells and incantations but a tangible force—one that can heal, destroy, or corrupt with equal ease. The battles are visceral, the politics sharp, and the world itself feels alive, with cultures, histories, and conflicts that extend far beyond the immediate plot. The Fhrey’s domains are as diverse as their personalities: some rule over storms, others over time, and a few over the very fabric of reality. Sullivan doesn’t just introduce gods; he *humanizes* them, making their downfall feel inevitable yet devastating. When a god falls, it’s not just a loss of power—it’s a loss of *meaning*, a crack in the foundation of civilization itself.

What sets *Age of Myth* apart from so many modern fantasy epics is its refusal to romanticize the divine. The Fhrey are not wise, benevolent beings; they are tyrants who maintain their rule through fear and manipulation. Their magic is not a gift but a curse, a tool that ensures their own eventual destruction. Sullivan’s world is one where faith is both a shield and a chainsaw, where the gods’ greatest strength—their perceived immortality—is also their Achilles’ heel. This subversion of traditional fantasy tropes makes the story feel fresh, even within a genre saturated with divine intrigue.

The pacing is tight, the stakes high, and the worldbuilding so rich that even side characters feel like they belong in a grand, living tapestry. Sullivan excels at balancing large-scale mythic themes with intimate character moments, ensuring that the fall of a god isn’t just a plot point but a personal tragedy for those who loved—or feared—them. The book’s ending leaves room for the next installments to build on this foundation, promising even greater conflicts, deeper character arcs, and a world on the brink of upheaval.

For fans of fantasy that is both epic in scale and deeply personal, *Age of Myth* is a must-read. It’s a story about the end of an era, the birth of a rebellion, and the cost of defying the divine. Sullivan doesn’t just write a fantasy; he crafts a myth, one that feels as ancient and inevitable as the gods he dismantles. If you’ve ever wanted a fantasy where the gods are the real villains—and the humans are the only ones who can save themselves—this is your book.
