# My Enemy's Cradle

**Author:** Sara Young  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Literary history is rarely forged in moments of tranquility; more often, it is hammered out on the anvil of crisis. In Anne’s Cradle, Eri Muraoka presents a breathtaking and deeply moving biography of her grandmother, Hanako Muraoka—the visionary woman who introduced L. M. Montgomery’s *Anne of Green Gables* to Japan. Translated with exquisite sensitivity by Cathy Hirano, this English-language debut reveals how a timeless Canadian classic became a clandestine beacon of hope during the darkest hours of World War II. 

The narrative’s central theme explores the transcendent power of literature as an act of quiet rebellion and cultural preservation. Born into rural poverty, Hanako’s trajectory shifts when she enters an elite Tokyo missionary school. There, nurtured by Canadian educators, she discovers the boundless realms of English literature. Yet, the heart of the biography pulses with tension in 1941. As geopolitical hostilities peak following the attack on Pearl Harbor, Western missionaries are forced to flee, and Hanako—beloved across Japan as “Radio Auntie”—is branded a traitor to her own people simply for her deep ties to the West. 

Amidst the screaming wails of air-raid sirens and the constant, suffocating threat of imprisonment or execution, Hanako finds solace in a parting gift from a Canadian friend: a contraband copy of *Anne of Green Gables*. Refusing to let hatred eclipse her lifelong devotion to storytelling, she begins translating the adventures of the spirited red-headed orphan in 1943. Muraoka’s biographical style is both journalistic and deeply intimate, painting a vivid portrait of a woman who risked everything to smuggle empathy and imagination across enemy lines. 

Eri Muraoka writes with a profound reverence that never tips into hagiography; instead, she grounds her grandmother’s monumental achievements in raw humanity, personal tragedy, and resilience. The prose flows seamlessly, bridging the cultural gap between early twentieth-century rural Japan and the verdant hills of Prince Edward Island. Ultimately, this biography is not merely a tribute to a legendary translator; it is a stunning testament to the fact that art knows no borders, and that the simple, stubborn act of sharing a story can outlast the fires of war.
