# The Broken Wings

**Author:** Kahlil Gibran  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

A masterclass in lyrical melancholy, Kahlil Gibran’s The Broken Wings remains a timeless monument to the agonizing beauty of forbidden love. Long before he penned The Prophet, a young Gibran offered the world this deeply personal, semi-autobiographical novella set against the intoxicating, jasmine-scented backdrop of turn-of-the-twentieth-century Beirut. Now newly framed by Nadim Naaman’s contemporary adaptation—drawing from the acclaimed West End stage production—this enduring classic proves that true poetry never truly ages.

At its core, the narrative charts the breathtaking ascent and subsequent crushing descent of an eighteen-year-old Gibran’s first love. Enter Selma Karamy, a vision of ethereal grace and intellect, who is destined to become the author's spiritual twin. Their clandestine meetings in a quiet, secluded garden become sanctuaries of pure emotion, moments where two souls recognize each other across the cosmic divide. But beauty in Gibran’s universe is rarely permitted to exist without shadow. Selma is soon bartered away in an arranged marriage to Mansour Bey Galib, the arrogant nephew of a powerful bishop whose greed is matched only by his cruelty. Trapped by the gilded cage of patriarchal wealth and societal expectation, Selma becomes a tragic symbol of a caged bird, while Gibran watches helplessly as the wings of their shared future are systematically clipped.

Gibran’s signature literary style is the true beating heart of the novel. Eschewing dry prose, he writes with the breathless, visionary fervor of a mystic. Every sentence is drenched in sensory detail, reading less like a traditional novel and more like a prolonged, sorrowful elegy. Nature does not merely serve as a backdrop; it is an active participant, echoing the internal tumult of the lovers through the weeping willows, the restless sea, and the unyielding mountain peaks of Lebanon. It is a prose style that invites the reader to slow down, inhale deeply, and surrender entirely to the emotional gravity of the page.

Yet, beyond the romance, the true brilliance of The Broken Wings lies in its fearless confrontation of social injustice. Gibran uses the tragic plight of Selma to launch a fierce critique of the institutions of his time—institutions that continue to cast long shadows today. He dissects the brutal subjugation of women in marriage, the corrosive influence of wealth over spiritual contentment, and the agonizing friction between rigid religious tradition and the modern human need for autonomy. Selma is not merely a passive victim of a broken heart; she becomes an early voice for gender equality, courageously questioning a society that demands the total erasure of female agency. 

More than a century after its original publication, Gibran’s meditation on the freedom to love who we love resonates with startling urgency. It asks a universal question that still haunts the modern conscience: what is the cost of living a life dictated by the expectations of others rather than the truth of one's own soul?

This new adaptation breathes fresh life into Gibran’s poignant lament, making it accessible to a new generation of readers without losing an ounce of its original, aching romanticism. The Broken Wings is not a book to be passively consumed; it is an experience to be felt in the marrow of your bones. It reminds us that while love may be thwarted by the cruel machinery of society, the memory of true passion remains immortal. A breathtaking, heartbreaking triumph that demands to be read, wept over, and remembered.
