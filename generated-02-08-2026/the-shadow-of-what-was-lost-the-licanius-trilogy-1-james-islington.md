# The Shadow of What Was Lost (The Licanius Trilogy, #1)

**Author:** James Islington  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2016

Epic fantasy is a genre defined by its ambition, often demanding that readers surrender to sprawling worlds, complex magic systems, and characters burdened by the weight of destiny. Enter James Islington and his 2016 debut, The Shadow of What Was Lost, the explosive first installment of The Licanius Trilogy. From its very opening pages, Islington announces himself not merely as a participant in the modern fantasy renaissance, but as a master architect of intricate plotting and high-stakes world-building. 

At its core, the novel grapples with a profoundly compelling theme: the inescapable gravity of the past and the dangerous pursuit of absolute truth in a world built on lies. Set two decades after a devastating war that saw the godlike Augurs overthrown, the novel explores a society desperately trying to police its own magic. The surviving Gifted are forced to live under the shadow of the Four Tenets, heavily restricted rules designed to prevent a recurrence of past horrors. Into this uneasy peace steps Davian, a young man burdened not only by his status as a despised Gifted, but by the terrifying discovery that he possesses the forbidden, reality-bending power of the Augurs. 

Islington uses this central conflict to examine how societies heal—or fail to heal—from trauma. The war that birthed Davian’s world was won by rebels who promised peace through limitation, yet peace has merely bred paranoia and oppression. Davian, alongside his friends Wirr and Asha, is thrust into a perilous journey that forces them to question the very foundations of their authorities. Parallel to Davian’s struggle is an equally compelling narrative thread: a mysterious young man waking up in a forest, violently stripped of his memories yet inextricably bound to Davian’s fate. Meanwhile, a primordial threat stirs in the frozen north, whispering of an ancient enemy that time was supposed to have buried. 

What elevates The Shadow of What Was Lost above the standard tropes of coming-of-age epic fantasy is Islington’s striking stylistic confidence. Drawing favorable comparisons to genre titans like Robert Jordan and Brandon Sanderson, Islington weaves a narrative of breathtaking complexity. His prose is clean, purposeful, and propulsive, driving the reader relentlessly forward through shifting perspectives and mounting tension. 

Where Islington truly shines, however, is in his plotting. Modern epic fantasy often falls into the trap of sprawling aimlessness, but The Shadow of What Was Lost reads like a meticulously constructed clock. Every revelation feels earned, and every twist recontextualizes what came before. The magic system is rigorous and rule-bound—reminiscent of Sanderson’s hard magic—yet it carries the mythic, awe-inspiring weight of classic high fantasy. The sense of mystery is palpable throughout; the amnesiac stranger, the true nature of the Augurs, and the identity of the northern threat create an intoxicating puzzle box that refuses to let the reader rest.

Admittedly, the novel does not shy away from the dense exposition inherent to launching a massive secondary world, and the sheer volume of names, titles, and lore can occasionally dizzy the uninitiated. Yet, this density is precisely what makes the experience so rewarding. Islington is playing a long game, planting narrative seeds that hint at a staggering scope across the trilogy. 

The Shadow of What Was Lost is a triumph of modern debut fantasy. It honors the grand traditions of the genre while injecting them with fresh, breakneck momentum. For readers hungry for a sprawling epic defined by intricate magic, deep philosophical questions about free will versus destiny, and a labyrinthine plot that rewards careful attention, James Islington’s debut is an absolute must-read. It is a stunning opening salvo from an author destined to shape the future of fantasy.
