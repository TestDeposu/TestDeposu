# The Five Red Herrings (Lord Peter Wimsey, #7)

**Author:** Dorothy L. Sayers  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2023

Dorothy L. Sayers was never merely a writer of whodunits; she was an architect of puzzles, a sociologist of the British upper and middle classes, and the creator of one of literature’s most enduringly complex amateur sleuths, Lord Peter Wimsey. Originally published in 1931 and reissued to captivate modern audiences, The Five Red Herrings stands not only as a pinnacle of the Golden Age of Detective Fiction but as a masterclass in fair-play plotting. Set against the windswept, rugged backdrop of a Scottish artist colony in Kirkcudbright, the novel takes a delightfully cynical premise—a universally despised man is murdered, and nearly everyone has a motive—and elevates it into an intellectual marathon of time, space, and railway timetables.

The central theme of The Five Red Herrings is the friction between human nature and empirical logic, viewed through the lens of creative temperament. Kirkcudbright is a community defined by its dual passions: fishing and painting. Yet, beneath the picturesque veneer of easels and tide charts lies a hotbed of petty jealousies, financial desperation, professional rivalries, and personal betrayals. When the abrasive, obnoxious landscape painter Sandy Campbell is found dead at the foot of a cliff, ostensibly having tumbled while chasing inspiration, the local authorities are ready to write it off as a tragic accident. Lord Peter Wimsey, however, is holidaying nearby and senses the discordant notes in the tragedy. 

Sayers brilliantly uses the artistic community to explore the theme of obsession. The six suspects—all fellow painters—are brilliantly distinct, each harboring deep-seated grudges against Campbell, who had a vile habit of blackmail, plagiarism, and general unpleasantness. The title refers to the half-dozen suspects, five of whom are innocent diversions designed to pull the investigator off the scent, while the sixth is a cold-blooded mastermind. By populating the canvas with plausible killers, Sayers creates a rich tapestry of suspicion where artistic pride and personal survival clash violently.

What truly distinguishes The Five Red Herrings within Sayers’ bibliography, and within the genre as a whole, is her uncompromising commitment to technical precision. If Agatha Christie was the master of psychological misdirection, Sayers was the queen of logistical architecture. This novel is famously dense with timetables, bicycle speeds, tidal shifts, and artistic minutiae regarding tube paints and sketching techniques. Some contemporary readers might find the microscopic focus on train schedules and distances daunting, but for the true aficionado of detective fiction, it is intoxicating. Sayers does not cheat; she lays out every single piece of evidence on the table, challenging the reader to match wits with Wimsey. 

Lord Peter himself is in top form here. Moving away from the shell-shocked aristocrat of earlier novels or the romantic pursuer of later ones, this iteration of Wimsey is an intellectual hound on a scent, combining aristocratic charm with an almost machine-like deductive rigor. His collaboration with local Scottish law enforcement is handled with witty cultural observations that highlight Sayers’ sharp ear for dialogue and regional nuance. 

In the end, The Five Red Herrings remains a towering achievement in detective fiction. It requires patience and an active intellect, rewarding the attentive reader with one of the most satisfying and airtight resolutions in the genre. Sayers paints a vivid portrait of human pettiness and brilliant criminality, proving once again that murder is the ultimate dark art.
