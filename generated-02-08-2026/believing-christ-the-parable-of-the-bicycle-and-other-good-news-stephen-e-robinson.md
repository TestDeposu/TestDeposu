# Believing Christ: The Parable of the Bicycle and Other Good News

**Author:** Stephen E. Robinson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

**Believing Christ: The Parable of the Bicycle and Other Good News by Stephen E. Robinson – A Fresh Perspective on the Church’s True Identity**

In an era where the term "church" is often reduced to a building, a brand, or a social club, Stephen E. Robinson’s *Believing Christ* stands as a bold and necessary corrective. This book isn’t just another theological treatise on ecclesiology—it’s a passionate, scripturally grounded redefinition of what the church *really* is, rooted in the New Testament’s vision of a living, dynamic body of believers. Robinson, a scholar with a gift for clarity, doesn’t just dissect the Bible’s teachings on the church; he invites readers to *see* it afresh, as Jesus and the apostles intended.

At its core, *Believing Christ* is a book about *belonging*—not to an institution, but to a covenant. Robinson begins by dismantling modern misconceptions, tracing the word "church" (Greek *ekklēsia*) from its Hebrew origins to its New Testament fulfillment. He doesn’t shy away from the radical implications: the church isn’t a place you visit; it’s a community you’re called into, with clear biblical standards for membership, purpose, and mutual accountability. The book’s early chapters, particularly those on "Entrance into the Assembly" and "Official Acceptance into the Body of Christ," challenge readers to confront the question: *What does it truly mean to be part of Christ’s church?* Robinson’s answer is unflinching—it’s not about signing a membership form but about repentance, faith, and submission to the Lordship of Jesus.

One of the book’s most compelling strengths is Robinson’s ability to make abstract theological concepts tangible. The famous "Parable of the Minas" (Chapter Six) isn’t just a story—it’s a lens through which he examines the church’s role in equipping believers for ministry. He argues that the church isn’t obligated to meet every individual’s emotional or material needs; instead, its purpose is to cultivate faithfulness, stewardship, and the multiplication of disciples. This is where Robinson’s title, *Believing Christ*, takes on its full meaning. The church exists to *believe* in Christ’s promises, to *live* out His mission, and to *build* His kingdom—not as a service provider, but as a community of witnesses.

The book’s most striking contribution, however, is its emphasis on the *relational* nature of the church. Robinson devotes significant attention to how local congregations should function—from the obligations of members to one another to the bonds that unite churches across geographical and cultural divides. His discussion of *fellowship* (Chapter Seven) is particularly insightful, drawing from the Epistle to the Philippians to illustrate how true unity isn’t about uniformity but about shared suffering, humility, and mutual edification. He challenges readers to ask: *Are we building a church that reflects Christ’s heart, or one that reflects our own preferences?*

Robinson’s writing style is both rigorous and accessible. He engages with Scripture with the precision of a scholar but writes with the warmth of a pastor. The "Parable of the Bicycle" (a metaphor for the church’s mission) appears throughout the book, serving as a recurring reminder that the church isn’t a static institution but a living, moving entity—like a bicycle, it requires constant balance, direction, and effort to stay upright. This metaphor alone makes the book engaging for readers who might otherwise find ecclesiology dry.

That said, *Believing Christ* isn’t without its challenges. Some may find Robinson’s emphasis on biblical standards for membership restrictive, especially in a culture that often prioritizes inclusivity over orthodoxy. Others might wish for more practical application—how, exactly, does a congregation implement these principles today? But these are minor quibbles. The book’s greatest value lies in its ability to reignite a biblical vision of the church, one that is both challenging and hopeful.

For anyone seeking to understand the church as Jesus and the apostles did, *Believing Christ* is essential reading. It’s a book that will make you rethink what it means to be part of the body of Christ—and dare you to live accordingly. In a world where the church is often reduced to a brand or a program, Robinson’s work is a refreshing call to return to the original, transformative power of the gospel.
