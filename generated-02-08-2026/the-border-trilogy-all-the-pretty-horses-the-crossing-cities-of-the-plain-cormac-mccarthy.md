# The Border Trilogy: All the Pretty Horses, The Crossing, Cities of the Plain

**Author:** Cormac McCarthy  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1998

Cormac McCarthy’s *Cities of the Plain*—the final installment of *The Border Trilogy*—is a masterclass in the art of elegy, a work that lingers like the dust of a long-vanished frontier, both haunting and necessary. Here, McCarthy doesn’t just close a door; he dissolves the very frame of the story he’s been building for nearly a thousand pages, leaving only the weight of what was and the hollow promise of what might have been. The novel is a meditation on loss, on the erosion of a way of life, and on the fragile, almost mythic bonds that tie men together across time and distance. It is, in the best sense, a book that feels like a farewell—one that refuses to be sentimental, yet cannot help but ache.

At its core, *Cities of the Plain* is a story about the end of something, not just for John Grady Cole and Billy Parham, but for the entire world they inhabit. Nine years have passed since the events of *The Crossing*, and though the two men remain bound by an unspoken, unbreakable friendship, the landscape they move through has shifted irrevocably. The military’s encroachment on the New Mexico ranch, the creeping modernity of El Paso and Juárez, the slow death of the open range—all of these forces conspire to strip away the old order. McCarthy doesn’t romanticize this world; he renders it with a stark, almost clinical precision, as if the very act of describing it is an act of preservation. The result is a prose so vivid it feels like a physical presence, a language that carves into the reader’s mind with the same relentless force as the wind across the plains.

The novel’s title is telling. Cities are places of order, of progress, of the inevitable march toward something new. The plain, by contrast, is wild, untamed, a space where men like John Grady and Billy still have room to breathe. But the plain is also a place of isolation, of silence, of the kind of loneliness that gnaws at the edges of the soul. McCarthy doesn’t shy away from the brutality of this world—whether it’s the marauding dogs that stalk the ranch, the blind musician whose fate is sealed by circumstance, or the precocious shoe-shine boy who embodies the next generation’s inevitable disconnection from the land. These are not characters in a traditional sense; they are fragments of a larger, fading world, each with their own quiet tragedies.

And then there is the girl—seized by ill fortune, a love as dangerous as it is inevitable. She is the one bright spot in this otherwise desolate landscape, a reminder that even in the face of inevitable decay, there is still room for passion, for the kind of fierce, almost reckless devotion that defines the best of McCarthy’s male friendships. But her presence is also a catalyst for the novel’s central tension: the pull between the old world and the new. John Grady and Billy are men who have spent their lives moving between two borders—geographical, cultural, emotional. The girl represents the future, the possibility of something beyond the ranch, beyond the plain. But she also represents the very forces that are eroding the way of life they’ve known. McCarthy doesn’t offer easy answers. He doesn’t suggest that the old world can be preserved, or that the new is necessarily better. He simply shows us the moment of choice, the moment of reckoning, and leaves us to grapple with the consequences.

McCarthy’s prose is, as always, his greatest weapon. It is a language of restraint and power, of understatement and explosive violence. He doesn’t need to spell things out; he lets the reader feel the weight of the words, the way they settle into the bones. There is a musicality to his writing, a rhythm that mirrors the ebb and flow of the land itself. And yet, for all its beauty, there is a coldness to it too—a recognition that this world is doomed, that the men who inhabit it are doomed, and that the only thing left is to live with that knowledge. It is a prose that feels both timeless and utterly of its moment, a testament to McCarthy’s ability to capture the essence of a place and a time without ever resorting to cliché.

The Border Trilogy as a whole is often described as a Western, but *Cities of the Plain* is something more—a modern epic, a tragedy in the classical sense, a story about the end of an era. It is a book that demands to be read slowly, savored, because it is not just about the characters or the plot, but about the way McCarthy makes us feel the weight of history, the inevitability of change, the quiet despair of a world that is slipping away. It is a book that lingers in the mind long after the last page is turned, a book that refuses to be forgotten.

In the end, *Cities of the Plain* is not just the conclusion of a trilogy; it is a standalone masterpiece, a work that stands alongside the greatest of American literature. It is a book about the end of something, but also about the beginning of something else—about the way one generation passes the torch to the next, even as they know that the torch may burn out before it reaches its destination. It is a book that is as much about the land as it is about the men who love it, about the way the two are inextricably linked, about the way the land shapes them and they shape it in turn. And it is a book that, above all else, is about the way we remember—about the stories we tell ourselves to make sense of the world, and the stories that are told to us by the world itself.
