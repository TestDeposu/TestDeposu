# Sent (The Missing, #2)

**Author:** Margaret Peterson Haddix  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Sent (The Missing, #2) thrusts Jonah, Chip, Katherine, and Alex into the treacherous corridors of 1483 England, where the line between destiny and disaster is as thin as a blade’s edge. Margaret Peterson Haddix builds on the premise introduced in Found—that ordinary teenagers are actually displaced historical figures—and escalates the stakes by forcing her protagonists to confront a paradox: to restore the timeline they must allow the very princes they embody to meet their fated murders. This moral quandary fuels the novel’s central theme: the tension between agency and inevitability. Haddix asks whether fixing history is an act of heroism or a surrender to a prewritten script, and she lets her characters wrestle with that question in real time, making the philosophical dilemma feel immediate and personal.

The author’s style remains crisp and propulsive, a hallmark of her work across the Shadow Children and other series. Short, punchy chapters keep the narrative momentum relentless, while vivid sensory details— the chill of stone corridors, the murmur of court intrigue, the metallic tang of fear—ground the fantastical time‑travel premise in a tangible historical setting. Haddix skillfully balances exposition with action, dropping clues about the princes’ fate through documents, whispered rumors, and the protagonists’ own growing dread, rather than relying on heavy-handed info‑dumps. This technique invites readers to piece together the mystery alongside Jonah and Chip, enhancing the sense of immersion.

Characterization continues to shine. Chip’s internal struggle—balancing his newfound identity as the future king with the terror of his own death—provides an emotional core that prevents the plot from becoming a mere puzzle‑box exercise. Jonah’s steadfast loyalty and Katherine’s pragmatic bravery offer contrasting yet complementary perspectives, while Alex’s youthful idealism adds a touch of hope amid the gloom. Haddix’s dialogue feels authentic to each voice, shifting subtly as the teens grapple with the weight of their roles.

One of the novel’s strengths is its ability to make history feel alive without sacrificing the urgency of a thriller. The Tudor court is rendered with enough period detail to satisfy history buffs, yet the narrative never lingers so long that the pacing drags. Instead, each revelation about the princes’ fate propels the characters—and the reader—forward, turning the act of “fixing” history into a high‑stakes race against time.

If there is a minor critique, it lies in the occasional reliance on familiar tropes of time‑travel narratives: the mysterious overseer (JB) who dangles the promise of return, the looming threat of paradox, and the inevitable sacrifice required to set things right. However, Haddix transcends these conventions by grounding them in genuine emotional conflict and by refusing to offer easy answers. The resolution respects the complexity of the moral dilemma, leaving readers to ponder whether the characters truly “fixed” history or merely accepted a different version of it.

Sent succeeds as both a gripping adventure and a thoughtful meditation on fate, identity, and the ethics of intervention. Haddix’s trademark blend of brisk pacing, rich historical texture, and deep character work makes this sequel a worthy continuation of the Missing series, appealing to fans of speculative fiction, historical intrigue, and young‑adult drama alike.
