# Perfection (Neighbor from Hell, #2)

**Author:** R.L. Mathewson  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Perfection, the second installment in R.L. Mathewson’s Neighbor from Hell series, reads like a warm, slightly mischievous conversation you’d have over coffee with a friend who’s both brutally honest and hopelessly romantic. The story centers on Trevor Bradford, a self‑assured, perfection‑obsessed landlord whose carefully curated world is upended by Zoe, his plucky, perpetually‑unlucky tenant. What begins as a grudging agreement to help her after a thoughtless slip of the tongue quickly spirals into a dance of hesitant touches, reluctant admissions, and the slow, stubborn thawing of two guarded hearts.

At its core, Perfection explores the illusion of flawlessness—both in ourselves and in the relationships we chase. Trevor’s initial mantra, “I know exactly what I want,” crumbles under the weight of Zoe’s imperfect, endearing reality. Mathewson uses this tension to interrogate the ways we armor ourselves against vulnerability, suggesting that true connection demands we relinquish the script we’ve written for our lives and allow room for messiness, mistake, and growth. Zoe, meanwhile, embodies the hope that even when life keeps serving you burnt toast, a single act of kindness can reroute the day’s trajectory. Their push‑pull dynamic is less about opposition and more about mutual discovery: each character learns that the other's perceived shortcomings are, in fact, the very qualities that make them lovable.

Mathewson’s style is unmistakably her trademark: snappy dialogue laced with wit, a breezy pacing that never lets the tension sag, and an ear for the humor hidden in everyday awkwardness. She balances laugh‑out‑loud moments—Trevor’s internal monologue about Zoe’s “dangerously cute” baking disasters, Zoe’s sarcastic retorts to his over‑the‑top gestures—with genuinely tender scenes that feel earned rather than saccharine. The romance unfolds in a series of small, significant gestures rather than grandiose proclamations, making the eventual emotional payoff feel both satisfying and credible. Steam is present but never overwhelms the narrative; it serves as a natural extension of the characters’ growing trust, reinforcing the theme that intimacy blossoms when we stop performing perfection and start being present.

Where the novel stumbles is in its occasional reliance on familiar rom‑com tropes—the gruff hero with a hidden soft spot, the plucky heroine who “just needs someone to believe in her.” While Mathewson infuses these conventions with her distinctive voice, seasoned readers may anticipate certain beats before they arrive. Additionally, Zoe’s backstory, though hinted at, could have been explored with a bit more depth to heighten the stakes of her emotional journey. Nevertheless, these minor critiques do little to derail the overall charm of the story; they merely remind us that even a well‑crafted romantic comedy benefits from a little more nuance in its supporting layers.

In the end, Perfection succeeds because it captures the alchemy of two imperfect people finding a rhythm that feels uniquely theirs. Mathewson’s humor keeps the tone light, her dialogue crackles with chemistry, and her underlying message—that love isn’t about finding a perfect match but about embracing perfectly imperfect moments—resonates long after the final page. It’s a delightful, quick‑read escape for anyone who believes that sometimes the best things in life are the ones we never planned to love.
