# Kruistocht in spijkerbroek

**Author:** Thea Beckman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

**"Kruistocht in Spijkerbroek": A Time-Traveling Masterpiece That Turns History into Heartbreak—and Hope**

Thea Beckman’s *Kruistocht in Spijkerbroek* (translated as *Crusade in Jeans*) is more than a historical adventure—it’s a searing, tender exploration of faith, innocence, and the brutal cost of misplaced belief. Through the eyes of sixteen-year-old Dolf, a modern-day Amstelveen teen, Beckman crafts a story that feels both wildly imaginative and painfully real, blending time travel with the harrowing reality of the Children’s Crusade of 1212. The result is a book that lingers long after the last page, challenging readers to question what they’d do—and what they’d believe—in the face of the impossible.

At its core, *Kruistocht in Spijkerbroek* is a story about the collision of two worlds: Dolf’s cynical, comfort-driven present and the fervent, desperate idealism of medieval children. Beckman’s genius lies in her ability to make the past feel visceral without glorifying it. The Children’s Crusade—a tragic episode where thousands of young Europeans, led by charismatic preachers, marched toward the Holy Land with nothing but their faith and naive conviction—is rarely depicted with such raw immediacy. Beckman doesn’t shy away from the horror: the children’s hunger, their vulnerability, the way their leaders exploit their trust. Yet, she also captures their resilience, their songs, and their stubborn hope, making them more than just historical footnotes. They become real, flawed, and deeply human.

Dolf, the protagonist, is the perfect lens through which to experience this. A sarcastic, rule-following teen who signs up for a time-travel experiment to avoid a boring school event, he’s the last person you’d expect to be thrust into a medieval catastrophe. His initial detachment—his "I’m just here for the ride" attitude—sharpens the contrast between his world and theirs. But as he watches the children march, their banners fluttering in the wind, their voices rising in hymns, Dolf’s own cynicism begins to crack. He starts to see the world through their eyes: not as a problem to solve, but as a promise to fulfill. Beckman’s writing is deft in this psychological unraveling. Dolf isn’t a hero; he’s a boy forced to confront the gaping chasm between his life and theirs, and the book refuses to let him look away.

Beckman’s prose is sharp, unpretentious, and deeply empathetic. She avoids the pitfall of making the medieval world feel like a museum exhibit, instead grounding it in sensory detail—the smell of unwashed bodies, the taste of stale bread, the weight of a child’s pack on their shoulders. Her dialogue crackles with authenticity, whether it’s the preachers’ soaring rhetoric or the quiet desperation of a mother who’s lost her son to the march. The time-travel mechanism, while a fun premise, never overshadows the story’s emotional core. Dolf’s journey isn’t just about returning home; it’s about understanding what it means to believe in something greater than yourself, even when that belief is misguided.

The book’s themes resonate with unsettling relevance. In an era where conspiracy theories, blind devotion to ideologies, and the manipulation of youth are all too common, *Kruistocht in Spijkerbroek* serves as a cautionary tale wrapped in a gripping narrative. Beckman doesn’t preach; she invites readers to sit with the discomfort. Why do people follow leaders who promise miracles? How much of our own faith—or skepticism—is shaped by fear? These questions hang in the air like the dust of a medieval road, unanswered but impossible to ignore.

Visually, the book is a feast. The cover—if it follows the Dutch edition’s design—likely captures the stark contrast between Dolf’s modern jeans and the medieval garb of the crusaders, reinforcing the theme of clashing worlds. Inside, Beckman’s narrative is rich with atmosphere, from the eerie silence of the forest where the children sleep to the deafening roar of the crowd when they finally reach the Rhine. The pacing is tight, never bogged down by excessive exposition. The stakes are high: Dolf has five hours to find a way back before he’s lost forever, but the real tension lies in whether he’ll let the experience change him—or if he’ll walk away unchanged.

Awards like the Gouden Griffel and the European Prize for Historical Fiction aren’t given lightly, and *Kruistocht in Spijkerbroek* earns them. It’s a book that works on multiple levels: as a thrilling adventure, a historical deep dive, and a profound meditation on what it means to grow up. Beckman doesn’t offer easy answers, but she does something far more powerful—she makes the reader feel the weight of history in their own bones.

For fans of time-travel stories with heart (*The Giver*, *A Wrinkle in Time*), historical fiction that doesn’t shy from darkness (*The Boy in the Striped Pajamas*), or coming-of-age tales that challenge the reader (*The Book Thief*), this is a must-read. It’s a book that will make you laugh, cry, and think—all in the span of a single, unforgettable journey through time.
