# To Light a Candle (Obsidian Mountain, #2)

**Author:** Mercedes Lackey  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2005

**A Flame in the Dark: The Gritty Brilliance of *To Light a Candle***

Mercedes Lackey’s *To Light a Candle*—the second installment in *The Obsidian Trilogy*—is a masterclass in weaving political intrigue, personal sacrifice, and mythic stakes into a tapestry of light and shadow. Where the first book, *The Outstretched Shadow*, introduced readers to a world teetering on the edge of war between High Magic’s rigid order and Wild Magic’s untamed fury, this sequel dives deeper into the cost of defiance, the weight of legacy, and the fragile balance between hope and annihilation. Lackey, a titan of fantasy who once redefined heroines with *The Forgotten Beasts of Eld*, here crafts a narrative that is as intellectually layered as it is emotionally devastating—a story where every candle flickers against the encroaching darkness of the Demon Queen’s reign.

At its core, *To Light a Candle* is a meditation on the price of power. The Golden City of the Bells, ruled by the all-male Mage Council, embodies the suffocating weight of tradition: High Magic is precise, controlled, and exclusionary, its practitioners bound by rules that stifle both creativity and humanity. Outside its walls, Wild Magic thrives in the chaos of desire and necessity, practiced by all, but its unpredictability makes it dangerous—both to its wielders and to those who would suppress it. Kellen, the protagonist, is the son of the Mage Council’s leader, a man who once dismissed him as a disappointment. Yet Kellen has become something greater: a Knight-Mage, a warrior who bridges the gap between sword and spell, between the rigid order of the City and the untamed wilds where elves, centaurs, and unicorns roam. His journey is one of redemption, not just for himself, but for the City he loves—one that has blindly trusted in its own invincibility. Lackey’s genius lies in making Kellen’s struggle feel personal, not just a grand political chess match, but a battle for the soul of a man who has spent his life being told he was never enough.

But if Kellen’s arc is about the burden of legacy, then Idalia’s is about the cost of love. A Wild Mage with healing powers, she has found joy in her relationship with Jermayan, an elven warrior whose own path is transformed when he bonds with a dragon—a rare and dangerous act that makes him the first elven Mage in a thousand years. Their love story is tender, aching, and ultimately tragic, for Idalia’s Wild Magic will demand a terrible price for the world-saving spell she must cast. Lackey doesn’t shy away from the bittersweet; she understands that fantasy is not just about epic battles, but about the quiet moments of connection that make the fall harder when they’re torn apart. Jermayan’s evolution—from a proud warrior to a man who must embrace magic against his people’s traditions—mirrors Kellen’s, but where Kellen fights for his City’s survival, Jermayan fights for his own identity, torn between duty and desire.

The Demon Queen’s attack is the book’s crescendo, a force of pure malevolence that seeks to unravel everything: the fragile alliance between humans and elves, the fragile peace between High and Wild Magic, and the fragile hearts of those who dare to hope. Lackey’s prose is rich with atmospheric detail, painting a world where magic is not just a tool but a living, breathing force—sometimes benevolent, sometimes vengeful. The tension between light and dark is palpable, not just in the literal battles but in the moral gray areas of the characters’ choices. The Mage Council’s arrogance is their downfall, but so is their refusal to see the world beyond their walls. The elves’ pride is their strength, but it also blinds them to the changing times. And the Wild Mages, though free, are often the most vulnerable, their magic as much a curse as a gift.

What makes *To Light a Candle* stand out in Lackey’s body of work—and in the broader fantasy genre—is its refusal to simplify its themes. This is not a story where good and evil are neatly divided; it’s a story where the line between them is blurred by ambition, fear, and the desperate need to survive. The Demon Queen is not just a villain; she is a catalyst, forcing characters to confront their deepest flaws. Kellen must choose between loyalty to his City and the truth of its peril. Idalia must accept that her love will cost her everything. Jermayan must decide whether to embrace his newfound power or betray the elven traditions that shaped him. Lackey’s characters are flawed, complex, and deeply human—qualities that make their struggles feel urgent and real.

The book’s title itself is a metaphor for the entire narrative: a candle in the dark, flickering against the encroaching shadow of Obsidian Mountain. It’s a reminder that even in the face of overwhelming darkness, there is still light to be found—if only one knows how to light the flame. Lackey’s writing is lyrical yet grounded, her world-building immersive without being overwhelming. She balances grand political maneuvering with intimate character moments, ensuring that the reader doesn’t just *experience* the story but *feels* it.

For fans of *The Forgotten Beasts of Eld*, this book will resonate with the same themes of defiance and transformation, but with a darker edge. For those new to Lackey’s work, *To Light a Candle* is a perfect introduction to her ability to craft stories that are both epic in scope and deeply personal in execution. It’s a fantasy novel that doesn’t just entertain—it challenges, it moves, and it lingers long after the final page.

In a genre often dominated by binary good vs. evil narratives, *To Light a Candle* is a breath of fresh air, a story where the real battles are fought not just with swords and spells, but with courage, sacrifice, and the willingness to see the world as it truly is—flawed, beautiful, and on the brink of ruin.
