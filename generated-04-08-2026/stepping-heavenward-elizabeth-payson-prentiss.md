# Stepping Heavenward

**Author:** Elizabeth Payson Prentiss  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2021

Stepping Heavenward remains a quiet yet powerful testament to the transformative power of honest self‑examination, a novel that reads less like a story and more like a soul laid bare on the page. Elizabeth Payson Prentiss, whose own hymn “More Love to Thee O Christ” echoes throughout the work, invites us into the intimate diary of Katherine Mortimer—a sixteen‑year‑old whose fiery temperament gradually yields to a deepening, reflective faith. The novel’s central theme is unmistakable: spiritual growth is forged not in grand gestures but in the daily, often painful, acts of choosing humility, gratitude, and surrender amid loss, marriage, and motherhood.

Prentiss’s style is deceptively simple, yet it carries a lyrical gravity that feels both period‑appropriate and timeless. She writes Katherine’s entries with the straightforwardness of a girl learning to trust her own voice, allowing the reader to hear the raw tremor of teenage angst, the sting of bereavement, and the tender awe of newborn life without the filter of an omniscient narrator. This first‑person, journal‑like approach creates an immediacy that pulls us into Katherine’s inner world as if we were sharing her quiet moments by the fireplace. The language is modest—no ornate Victorian flourishes for show—but each sentence is carefully weighted, often pausing on a single word (“grace,” “mercy,” “trust”) that resonates like a hymn refrain. Prentiss’s own spiritual earnestness seeps into the prose, turning what could have been a sentimental moral tale into a genuine exploration of doubt and conviction.

What makes Stepping Heavenward endure is its refusal to offer easy answers. Katherine’s journey is marked by setbacks: she lashes out, she questions God’s goodness, she wrestles with the expectations placed on women of her era. Yet each stumble is met with a quiet, persistent return to the journal—a practice that becomes her spiritual discipline. Prentiss shows that faith is not a static destination but a habit of returning, again and again, to the page (and to the prayer) when the heart feels heavy. In this way, the novel anticipates modern contemplative practices while remaining firmly rooted in its 19th‑century context.

The novel’s relevance today lies in its candid portrayal of the interior life. Readers accustomed to rapid‑fire social media updates will find a refreshing counterpoint in Katherine’s slow, deliberate reflections. Her struggles with anger, grief, and the desire for approval mirror contemporary anxieties, and her gradual embrace of gentleness offers a hopeful model for anyone seeking depth amidst distraction. Prentiss’s insistence that holiness is discovered in the mundane—diaper changes, marital disagreements, the quiet ache of a missing parent—speaks directly to a culture that often equates spirituality with extraordinary experiences.

Critics may note that the narrative occasionally leans didactic, with Katherine’s reflections veering into explicit moral lessons. Yet this tendency feels less like preaching and more like the natural outflow of a young woman earnestly trying to make sense of her world through the lens of her faith. The book’s strength is precisely its willingness to let the reader sit with Katherine’s uncertainties, to watch her stumble, and to witness the slow, steady rise of a heart learning to trust.

In sum, Stepping Heavenward is a beautifully restrained novel that captures the alchemy of turning personal turmoil into spiritual growth. Prentiss’s journalistic intimacy and sincere voice make Katherine’s journey feel both historically grounded and universally resonant. For anyone who cherishes a story where faith is lived rather than proclaimed, this reissued classic offers a rewarding, contemplative read.
