# Notes of a Crocodile

**Author:** Qiu Miaojin  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

**Notes of a Crocodile: A Queer, Chaotic, and Radiant Coming-of-Age Masterpiece**

Qiu Miaojin’s *Notes of a Crocodile* is not just a novel—it is a rebellious manifesto, a fragmented love letter to youth, and a razor-sharp satire of Taiwan’s post-martial-law society, all wrapped in the unmistakable voice of a writer who refused to be contained. Published in 1995 but only now fully translated into English (thanks to the 2018 Lucien Stryk Asian Translation Prize), this cult classic is a postmodern fever dream, a coming-of-age story that dismantles the very idea of what a novel should be. It is equal parts diary, collage, and confessional rant, told through the fractured perspective of Lazi, a lesbian narrator whose fatalistic devotion to the enigmatic Shui Ling—an older woman who may or may not be her lover—drives the narrative’s restless energy.

At its core, *Notes of a Crocodile* is a story about the search for belonging in a world that insists on conformity. Set in the late 1980s Taipei, a city still grappling with the remnants of authoritarian rule, the novel follows a group of misfit students at Taiwan’s most prestigious university—intellectuals, criminals, artists, and dreamers who reject the rigid expectations of gender, sexuality, and success. Lazi, our anonymous narrator, is a self-described "crocodile" (a nickname that hints at her predatory allure and emotional depth), trapped in a cycle of longing for Shui Ling while navigating a world that either ignores or mocks her desires. Her friends—a wealthy delinquent and his self-destructive gay lover, a hyper-competitive overachiever, and her effortlessly cool artist girlfriend—form a loose, chaotic family of outcasts, each grappling with their own forms of rebellion.

What makes *Notes of a Crocodile* so compelling is its refusal to be a traditional narrative. Qiu Miaojin’s prose is a whirlwind of styles: aphoristic musings, fragmented vignettes, satirical asides, and even what appears to be random scribbles—all of which mirror the disorientation of adolescence and the fluidity of queer identity. The novel’s structure is deliberately disjointed, jumping between Lazi’s introspections, her friends’ antics, and sharp observations about Taiwanese society. There are moments of poetic beauty, like when Lazi reflects on the "notes" she leaves for Shui Ling, each one a desperate attempt to bridge the gap between desire and unrequited love. But there are also moments of dark humor, as when the novel mocks the pretensions of academia or the performative masculinity of the university’s elite.

The novel’s most radical achievement, however, is its exploration of gender and sexuality as acts of defiance. Lazi’s relationship with Shui Ling is never fully explained—is it love? obsession? a power dynamic?—but the ambiguity is intentional. Qiu Miaojin forces the reader to confront the messy, often painful reality of queer desire, where labels are irrelevant and the pursuit of happiness is a constant negotiation with societal norms. The novel’s queer characters are not tragic figures but complex, flawed individuals who carve out their own spaces in a world that would rather erase them. The overachiever’s girlfriend, for instance, is both alluring and self-destructive, embodying the tension between ambition and rebellion. Meanwhile, the wealthy delinquent’s gay lover is a tragic figure, his self-destruction a commentary on the violence of heteronormative expectations.

Beyond its queer themes, *Notes of a Crocodile* is a scathing critique of Taiwanese society in transition. The novel’s setting—post-martial-law Taipei—is alive with the tension of a nation emerging from dictatorship but still bound by outdated hierarchies. Qiu Miaojin’s satire is razor-sharp, whether she’s poking fun at the pretentiousness of intellectuals, the performative wealth of the elite, or the stifling expectations placed on young women. Yet, for all its cynicism, the novel is also deeply tender. There is a quiet hope in Lazi’s friendships, in the way these misfits find solace in each other’s chaos. The novel’s title itself—*Notes of a Crocodile*—is a metaphor for the way Lazi (and by extension, the reader) is both predator and prey, both observer and participant in the messy, beautiful, and often painful process of self-discovery.

Qiu Miaojin’s style is as much a part of the novel’s genius as its themes. Her prose is dense, allusive, and deliberately fragmented, reflecting the disorientation of youth and the fluidity of memory. She blends high culture with street slang, philosophical musings with crude humor, creating a voice that feels both intimate and alienating. The novel’s structure—part diary, part collage, part performance—mirrors the way queer identity itself is often fragmented, pieced together from scraps of experience, half-remembered moments, and fleeting connections.

*Notes of a Crocodile* is not an easy read. It demands patience, engagement, and a willingness to be unsettled. But for those who embrace its chaos, it is a revelatory experience—a novel that feels like a conversation with a brilliant, troubled friend, one who refuses to be silenced. It is a testament to the power of queer literature to challenge norms, to celebrate the margins, and to find beauty in the cracks. In an era where coming-of-age stories often prioritize neat resolutions, Qiu Miaojin’s novel is a radical act of defiance, a reminder that growth is not linear, and that love—queer, messy, and unrequited—is still worth the risk.
