# The Amazing Maurice & His Educated Rodents (Discworld, #28)

**Author:** Terry Pratchett  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

In the vast, sprawling cosmos of Terry Pratchett’s Discworld, where the surreal meets the profound, The Amazing Maurice and His Educated Rodents occupies a distinct, darker niche. While many of the series’ installments are characterized by their whimsical satire of modern institutions, this particular tale—a twisted, witty deconstruction of the Pied Piper of Hamelin—is something altogether more elemental. It is a story about the stories we tell ourselves, and the terrifying chasm between a comfortable lie and a harsh reality.

The narrative follows Maurice, a streetwise alley cat with a penchant for profit and a surprisingly sophisticated vocabulary, and his troupe of "educated" rats. They are the quintessential con artists of the Disc: Maurice orchestrates the scheme, a boy named Keith provides the musical distraction, and the rats perform a staged infestation. It is a lucrative, well-oiled machine until they stumble upon the town of Bad Blinitz. Here, the scam goes sideways, and the tone shifts from lighthearted caper to genuine psychological horror.

Pratchett’s genius has always been his ability to smuggle philosophy into fantasy, and Maurice is a masterclass in this regard. At the heart of the book is the struggle for identity. Having gained the ability to think—and speak—the rats are thrust into an existential crisis. If you are no longer a scavenger driven by instinct, what are you? If you are a "thinking" being, does that make you a person? Pratchett uses the rats to hold a mirror up to human nature, exploring the thin veneer of civilization that separates a society from a pack of feral beasts.

The author’s prose is, as expected, razor-sharp. He possesses a rare gift for the "funny-sad" observation, where a joke delivered by a cynical cat reveals a stinging truth about human greed or theological desperation. He treats his younger audience with the same intellectual rigor he applies to his adult novels, refusing to sugarcoat the realities of fear, hunger, and the violence that occurs when a community turns against those it deems "other."

What makes this book resonate so deeply is that it refuses to stay in the lane of a traditional fairy tale. It is an exploration of the darkness that hides in the cellar—both literally, in the form of a malevolent presence under the town, and figuratively, in the darkness of the human heart. Pratchett forces the reader to confront the concept of the "Big Story," the cultural myths we lean on to justify our cruelty and our triumphs. He implies that when a story becomes more important than the truth, catastrophe is never far behind.

Maurice himself is one of Pratchett’s most captivating protagonists. He is selfish, manipulative, and deeply pragmatic, yet he is forced into a reluctant heroism that he would likely deny if asked. Watching him navigate the moral complexities of his own creation provides the narrative with its biting irony and its beating heart.

The Amazing Maurice and His Educated Rodents is not merely a "children’s book"; it is a sophisticated piece of literature that happens to feature talking rodents. It is a brisk, unsettling, and profoundly wise look at what happens when the vermin stop listening to the piper and start listening to their own consciences. For anyone who has ever suspected that the world is run by people (or cats) who are just making it up as they go along, this book will feel both like a homecoming and a warning. It remains one of the finest examples of why Pratchett is not just a fantasy author, but a foundational voice in contemporary literature.
