# Amusing Ourselves to Death: Public Discourse in the Age of Show Business

**Author:** Neil Postman  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1985

In "Amusing Ourselves to Death: Public Discourse in the Age of Show Business," Neil Postman delivers a searing critique of the impact of television on modern society. Written in 1985, this prophetic book is more relevant today than ever, as the lines between entertainment and information continue to blur. Postman's central argument is that television has transformed the way we consume information, reducing complex ideas to sound bites and prioritizing style over substance.

With characteristic wit and erudition, Postman dissects the ways in which television has reshaped our public discourse, turning politics, education, and even journalism into forms of entertainment. He argues that this transformation has led to a decline in critical thinking and a rise in superficiality, as we become increasingly accustomed to consuming information in bite-sized, visually engaging chunks. Postman's critique is not limited to television alone; he also examines the broader cultural implications of a society that values entertainment above all else.

One of the strengths of Postman's book is its accessibility. He writes in a clear, engaging style that makes complex ideas feel approachable and understandable. His use of historical and cultural references adds depth and context to his arguments, making the book feel both timely and timeless. Whether he's discussing the rise of television news or the impact of advertising on our perceptions of reality, Postman's writing is always lucid and compelling.

Throughout the book, Postman draws on a wide range of sources, from the works of Aldous Huxley and George Orwell to the writings of media theorists like Marshall McLuhan. He also engages with the ideas of philosophers like Plato and Aristotle, using their concepts to illuminate the ways in which television has transformed our understanding of reality. This eclectic approach adds richness and texture to the book, making it feel like a true intellectual tour-de-force.

Despite being written over three decades ago, "Amusing Ourselves to Death" remains a remarkably prescient book. Postman's warnings about the dangers of a society that prioritizes entertainment above all else feel more urgent today than ever, as we grapple with the implications of social media, reality TV, and the 24-hour news cycle. His critique of the ways in which television can be used to manipulate public opinion feels particularly relevant in the age of "fake news" and propaganda.

Ultimately, "Amusing Ourselves to Death" is a call to action, urging readers to think more critically about the media they consume and the ways in which it shapes their perceptions of the world. Postman's book is not a pessimistic one; instead, it offers a vision of a more informed, more engaged citizenry, one that is capable of withstanding the media onslaught and forging a more rational, more compassionate public discourse.

In short, "Amusing Ourselves to Death" is a masterpiece of media criticism, a book that should be read by anyone interested in the ways in which television and other forms of media shape our lives. Its themes and arguments are more relevant today than ever, making it a truly essential read.
