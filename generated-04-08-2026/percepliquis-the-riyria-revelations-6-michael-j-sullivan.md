# Percepliquis (The Riyria Revelations, #6)

**Author:** Michael J. Sullivan  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Percepliquis, the sixth entry in Michael J. Sullivan’s Riyria Revelations series, pulls the reader back to the uneasy beginnings of its most famous duo—Royce Melborn, the world‑weary thief, and Hadrian Blackwater, the earnest swordsman. Though marketed as a standalone first installment of The Riyria Chronicles, this 5,400‑word tale functions less as a prologue to the larger saga and more as a character‑study vignette that illuminates the fragile trust that will later become the series’ beating heart.

The story’s central theme is the transformation of self‑interest into reluctant partnership. Royce’s initial motivation—to teach Hadrian a harsh lesson about the futility of good deeds—quickly unravels as the pair stumble into a dangerous conspiracy that forces them to rely on each other’s complementary skills. Sullivan deftly uses this early mission to explore how cynicism and idealism can coexist, not as opposing forces but as complementary lenses through which the world is perceived. Royce’s hardened pragmatism masks a nascent sense of responsibility, while Hadrian’s naïve optimism is tempered by the harsh realities he encounters. Their evolving dynamic underscores a timeless fantasy truth: true strength often lies not in solitary brilliance but in the willingness to let another’s weaknesses shore up your own.

Sullivan’s style in Percepliquis mirrors the crisp, dialogue‑driven prose that has become his trademark. Sentences are lean, action‑laden, and peppered with witty banter that feels both authentic and effortlessly engaging. The thief’s sardonic internal monologue contrasts sharply with the swordsman’s earnest reflections, creating a rhythmic interplay that keeps the narrative brisk without sacrificing depth. Despite the story’s brevity, Sullivan manages to embed vivid world‑building hints—glimpses of the kingdom’s political intrigue, the lurking threat of ancient magic, and the moral ambiguity that colors every decision. These touches serve as tantalizing breadcrumbs for readers familiar with the full Revelations arc, while remaining accessible to newcomers who may encounter Royce and Hadrian for the first time here.

What stands out most is Sullivan’s ability to convey emotional growth within a tight word count. The climactic confrontation is not merely a test of blade skill but a moment where Royce chooses to protect Hadrian rather than exploit his innocence, and Hadrian learns that trust can be a weapon as potent as any sword. This subtle shift lays the groundwork for the loyalty and camaraderie that will define the duo’s later exploits, making Percepliquis a vital, if modest, piece of the larger mosaic.

Critically, the story’s brevity is both its strength and its limitation. Readers craving the expansive political machinations and sprawling battles of the full‑length novels may find the episode feels like a tantalizing amuse‑bouche rather than a full meal. Yet for those interested in the origins of a beloved partnership, the tale delivers a satisfying, tightly crafted character study that reinforces why Sullivan’s work resonates: his characters feel real, his dialogue sparkles, and his themes—loyalty, redemption, and the blurred line between self‑service and altruism—remain refreshingly relevant in a genre often dominated by epic scale over intimate nuance.

In sum, Percepliquis offers a sharp, entertaining glimpse into the formative moments of Royce and Hadrian’s alliance. It showcases Sullivan’s knack for blending swift, witty storytelling with meaningful emotional beats, making it a worthwhile read for both longtime fans of the Riyria Revelations and newcomers eager to see how two unlikely allies first learned to watch each other’s backs.
