# Bones of the Hills (Conqueror, #3)

**Author:** Conn Iggulden  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

**Bones of the Hills: Conn Iggulden’s Masterstroke in the Conqueror Saga**

Conn Iggulden’s *Bones of the Hills*—the third installment in his *Conqueror* series—is a tour de force of historical fiction, where the myth of Genghis Khan is reimagined not as a one-dimensional tyrant, but as a man of ruthless ambition, deep familial strife, and an almost tragic sense of inevitability. Iggulden, a master of blending meticulous research with gripping narrative, crafts a tale that is as much about power and legacy as it is about the brutal mechanics of conquest. This book doesn’t just retell history; it *revivifies* it, making the past feel visceral, alive, and undeniably human.

At its core, *Bones of the Hills* is a story of succession—a battle not just fought on battlefields, but in the shadows of Genghis’s own mind. The novel opens with the Mongol warlord poised on the cusp of a new campaign, his empire stretching from the frozen steppes to the edges of the known world. But beneath the surface of his invincibility simmers a crisis: his sons, Jochi and Chagatai, are locked in a bitter rivalry that threatens to fracture the very foundation of his legacy. Iggulden’s genius lies in his ability to weave this personal drama with the grand sweep of history. The political intrigue among the Mongols is as sharp as any Shakespearean tragedy, while the external conflicts—against the Persian Shah Ala-ud-Din and the formidable Arab armies—serve as a backdrop to Genghis’s internal torment. The author doesn’t shy away from the brutality of war, but he also humanizes the chaos, showing how even the most feared conqueror is vulnerable to betrayal, loss, and the weight of his own decisions.

Iggulden’s prose is a perfect match for the subject matter—lean, evocative, and relentless. His descriptions of the Mongol horde in motion are nothing short of cinematic, capturing the raw power of cavalry charges and the eerie stillness of the steppes. Yet, he balances this with intimate, almost poetic moments, such as the quiet desperation of Genghis’s wife, Börte, or the quiet defiance of the Persian nobles who refuse to kneel. The author’s dialogue crackles with authenticity, whether it’s the sharp exchanges between Genghis and his generals or the whispered threats of assassins lurking in the night. There’s a rhythm to Iggulden’s writing that keeps the pages turning, even as he delves into the darker corners of human nature—greed, fear, and the lengths to which men will go to secure their place in history.

What makes *Bones of the Hills* particularly compelling is its exploration of the cost of empire. Genghis Khan is not portrayed as a villain, but as a man who understands that power is fleeting and that the only way to ensure his legacy is to crush his enemies before they can rise again. Yet, the novel doesn’t glorify his methods. Iggulden shows the human cost—civilians caught in the crossfire, the fear in the eyes of those who once ruled, the betrayals that tear families apart. The title itself, *Bones of the Hills*, is a haunting metaphor for the destruction left in Genghis’s wake, a reminder that every empire is built on the ruins of others.

The novel’s pacing is masterful, juggling multiple threads—military campaigns, political maneuvering, and personal vendettas—without ever losing focus. The shift from the Mongol heartlands to the arid deserts of Persia and the towering mountains of Afghanistan is seamless, each setting lending its own flavor to the story. Iggulden’s research is evident in the attention to detail, from the tactics of Mongol warfare to the cultural nuances of the regions Genghis invades. Yet, he never lets the history overshadow the storytelling. Instead, he uses it as a foundation to build a narrative that feels both grounded and epic.

One of the most striking aspects of *Bones of the Hills* is its portrayal of Genghis Khan as a flawed but deeply strategic leader. He is not a man of blind fury, but one who calculates every move, who knows that mercy is a luxury he cannot afford. His relationship with his sons is particularly compelling—Jochi, the eldest, is a wild card, his temper and impulsiveness making him both a threat and a potential ally. Chagatai, meanwhile, is more calculating, his ambition tempered by a desire to secure his father’s legacy. The tension between them is the emotional heart of the novel, a microcosm of the larger struggle for power that will define the Mongol Empire’s future.

Iggulden also excels in creating a cast of memorable characters, from the cunning Persian generals to the loyal but weary Mongol warriors. Each figure is given depth, their motivations clear, even when their actions are morally ambiguous. This is not a story of clear heroes and villains, but of men and women caught in the gears of history, doing what they must to survive.

In the end, *Bones of the Hills* is more than just another historical epic—it’s a meditation on power, legacy, and the price of greatness. Conn Iggulden doesn’t just retell the story of Genghis Khan; he reimagines it as a tragedy, a tale of a man who built an empire on blood and steel, only to face the inevitable question: who will inherit his storm? The answer, as the novel unfolds, will shape the course of history itself.

For fans of historical fiction, *Bones of the Hills* is a must-read. It’s a book that immerses you in a world of dust and steel, of betrayal and triumph, where every decision has consequences that echo through the ages. Iggulden’s writing is as sharp as the swords of the Mongols, as vast as the steppes they rode across, and as enduring as the bones left in their wake.
