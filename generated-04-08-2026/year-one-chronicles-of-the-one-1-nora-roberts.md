# Year One (Chronicles of The One, #1)

**Author:** Nora Roberts  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

**A World on the Brink: Nora Roberts’ *Year One* Rewrites Survival with Magic and Desperation**

Nora Roberts has always been a master of blending romance with high-stakes drama, but *Year One*—the first installment in her *Chronicles of the One* series—is something far more ambitious. Here, she doesn’t just write about love in the face of chaos; she crafts an epic of collapse, where the end of civilization isn’t just a backdrop but the very engine of the story. Roberts, who has spent decades weaving emotional depth into her narratives, takes a bold leap into post-apocalyptic fiction, infusing it with the same lyrical prose and moral complexity that define her work. The result is a gripping, if uneven, meditation on hope, betrayal, and the fragile bonds that hold people together when the world unravels.

At its core, *Year One* is a story about the fragility of human connection in a world where trust is a luxury. The novel opens on New Year’s Eve, a night that should have been celebratory, but instead becomes the catalyst for catastrophe. Within weeks, a mysterious sickness wipes out half the global population, and the infrastructure that once sustained civilization—electricity, law, medicine—crumbles. Roberts doesn’t shy away from the brutality of the collapse; she depicts it with stark realism, showing how quickly order dissolves into savagery. But what sets this apart from typical post-apocalyptic fare is her insistence on the human cost. The characters aren’t just survivors; they’re people grappling with grief, fear, and the ethical dilemmas of a world where every choice could mean life or death.

The protagonist, Lana Bingham, is a witch—a detail that immediately signals Roberts’ willingness to embrace the fantastical. Lana’s magic isn’t just a plot device; it’s a reflection of the novel’s central tension: the rise of the supernatural as the old world’s power fades. Some magic is benevolent, like Lana’s, while other forces lurk in the shadows, threatening to corrupt even those who mean well. This duality mirrors the moral ambiguity of the characters themselves. Max, her lover, is a soldier with a dark past; Chuck, the tech genius, is brilliant but morally flexible; and Rachel, the doctor, must make impossible choices to keep her charges alive. Roberts excels at making these characters feel real, flawed, and deeply human, even as they navigate a world where the rules of society no longer apply.

The novel’s strength lies in its ensemble cast, each representing a different facet of survival. There’s Arlys, the journalist clinging to truth in a world of lies; Fred, the young man discovering his own latent powers; and Jonah, the paramedic whose faith in humanity is constantly tested. Roberts weaves their stories together with a deft hand, ensuring that no single character dominates the narrative. Instead, *Year One* feels like a collective journey, one where the bonds between these strangers become as vital as the magic that now shapes their world.

However, the book isn’t without its flaws. Roberts’ prose, while evocative, can sometimes feel overly descriptive, slowing the pacing at moments when the tension demands urgency. The magic system, while intriguing, is introduced with a certain vagueness that might leave some readers wanting more clarity. And while the characters are compelling, their arcs occasionally feel underdeveloped, particularly in the novel’s first half. These are minor quibbles, though, in a story that ultimately succeeds in delivering what it promises: a fresh, thought-provoking take on the apocalypse.

What makes *Year One* stand out is its refusal to romanticize survival. Roberts doesn’t offer easy answers or neat resolutions; instead, she presents a world where hope is hard-won and trust is earned. The novel’s title isn’t just a reference to the year after the collapse—it’s a promise of what comes next, a new beginning forged in the ashes of the old. In that sense, *Year One* is more than just a post-apocalyptic tale; it’s a story about the resilience of the human spirit, even in the darkest of times.

For fans of Roberts’ emotional depth and romantic undertones, this book will feel like a natural evolution of her work. For those who prefer their apocalypses grounded in grit and realism, *Year One* may not deliver the same level of harshness as *The Road* or *Station Eleven*. But for readers who want a story that balances magic, morality, and the raw, unfiltered struggle to survive, it’s a compelling choice. Roberts has always been a storyteller who understands the power of connection, and in *Year One*, she uses that understanding to craft a tale that’s as much about the people who endure as it is about the world they must rebuild.
