# Dragon Wing (The Death Gate Cycle, #1)

**Author:** Margaret Weis  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

**Dragon Wing: A Skybound Epic of Rebellion, Magic, and the Fragility of Worlds**

Margaret Weis, co-creator of the legendary *Dragonlance* saga, returns with *Dragon Wing*, the first installment of *The Death Gate Cycle*, a sprawling fantasy that reimagines the fractured remnants of a world sundered by ancient sorcerers. Here, the realms of sky, stone, fire, and water exist in uneasy separation, their boundaries guarded by the Death Gate—a threshold only the most daring (or desperate) dare cross. *Dragon Wing* unfurls in the Realm of Sky, a world of floating islands, mammoth dragons, and a desperate struggle for survival, where magic is both a blessing and a curse, and the cost of power is measured in blood.

At its core, *Dragon Wing* is a story of rebellion, betrayal, and the relentless march of fate. The novel introduces a trio of protagonists whose paths collide in the sky: **Prince Kael**, a royal heir whose assassination is secretly ordered by his own father; **Gorim**, a dwarf who rejects the rigid traditions of his people and becomes a catalyst for revolution; and **Vaelis**, a sinister sorcerer whose ambitions threaten to unravel the very fabric of Arianus. Weis weaves their narratives together with the precision of a master weaver, ensuring that each thread—political intrigue, personal vendettas, and cosmic mysteries—feeds into a grander tapestry of conflict.

The worldbuilding is rich and immersive, with the Realm of Sky rendered in vivid detail. Floating islands drift on currents of elven magic, held aloft by ancient spells that are slowly fading. The dwarves, once masters of stone, now cling to their mountain strongholds, their society rigid with dogma until Gorim’s defiance shakes the foundations. The dragons, majestic and ancient, serve as both allies and harbingers of doom, their presence a constant reminder of the world’s fragility. Weis’ prose is lyrical yet grounded, blending poetic descriptions of the sky’s endless expanse with the gritty realities of war and betrayal. Her dialogue crackles with wit and tension, whether between the cunning prince and his assassin or the fiery dwarf and his reluctant followers.

Yet *Dragon Wing* is more than just a tale of political maneuvering—it is a meditation on the cost of knowledge and the dangers of forgotten power. The novel hints at the existence of the Death Gate, a gateway to realms beyond Arianus, and the ancient sorcerers who once wielded magic across all four domains. The question lingers: *What happened to them?* And more importantly, *what will happen if the realms are reunited?* Weis teases this mystery with careful restraint, ensuring that the reader is left hungry for answers rather than satiated with exposition. The pacing is deliberate, allowing the world to breathe while driving the plot forward with a sense of inevitability.

Themes of rebellion and the price of defiance run deep. Gorim’s journey from a loyal dwarf to a revolutionary is one of the most compelling arcs, as he grapples with the weight of tradition versus the need for change. His challenge to his people’s beliefs resonates with the broader struggle for autonomy in a world where magic and power are hoarded by the few. Meanwhile, Prince Kael’s story is a tragic one, a young man caught between duty and desire, his fate sealed by the machinations of a king who sees him as a pawn rather than a son. Vaelis, the sorcerer, embodies the darker side of ambition, his quest for dominion over Arianus a chilling reminder that power corrupts even the most brilliant minds.

Weis’ signature strength lies in her ability to craft complex, morally ambiguous characters. None of the protagonists are purely heroic, and their flaws drive the narrative forward. Kael is noble but naive; Gorim is passionate but reckless; Vaelis is brilliant but ruthless. This nuance elevates *Dragon Wing* beyond a simple adventure tale, making it a story about the choices we make and the consequences that follow.

The only minor quibble is the occasional pacing issue, where subplots—particularly those involving the elves and their role in the magic that sustains the sky—feel slightly underdeveloped. However, this is a minor oversight in an otherwise sweeping and ambitious work. *Dragon Wing* is a book that rewards patience, its depth and intrigue making it a standout in modern fantasy.

For fans of *Dragonlance*, *The Wheel of Time*, or any epic fantasy that blends political intrigue with high-stakes magic, *Dragon Wing* is a must-read. It is a story of a world on the brink, where the sky itself is a battleground, and the fate of the realms hangs in the balance. Weis has crafted a tale that is both grand and intimate, a reminder that even in a world of floating islands and dragon riders, the human (and elven, and dwarf) condition remains the same: we are all shaped by the choices we make, and the consequences we cannot escape.

As the Death Gate looms in the distance, *Dragon Wing* invites readers to step into a world where magic is fading, rebellion is brewing, and the past is never truly dead. It is a story of wings—both literal and metaphorical—and the courage it takes to fly.
