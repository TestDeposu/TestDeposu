# Relentless

**Author:** Dean R. Koontz  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

Red Angel grips the reader from the first page and never loosens its hold. Douglas Clegg (writing here as Andrew Harper) thrusts us into a sun‑scorched Southern California nightmare where a killer dubbed the Red Angel leaves his victims crowned with bird‑wing talismans—a grotesque signature that feels both ritualistic and personal. Rookie officer Jane Laymon and the enigmatic Trey Campbell, a specialist from Darden State Hospital for Criminal Justice, are forced into an uneasy partnership as they chase a murderer whose motives seem to shift with every new corpse. The novel’s core tension isn’t just the chase; it’s the psychic link that binds the killer to Scoleri, a confined patient whose mind appears to echo the murderer’s thoughts. This connection raises a haunting question: is the evil external, or does it spring from the fractured psyches of those we label insane?

Clegg’s style is relentless—aptly named for the very feeling he evokes. His prose moves with the tempo of a thriller, each sentence sharpened like a blade, yet he lingers long enough on sensory details to make the horror tactile. The Southern California setting burns with heat and haze, contrasting the cold, clinical corridors of Darden State, and the isolated, moon‑lit terror of Moon Lake in the San Bernardino Mountains becomes a character in its own right. Clegg blends police procedural grit with supernatural unease, allowing the reader to feel the weight of Laymon’s badge and Campbell’s clinical intuition while simultaneously sensing an otherworldly pull that defies rational explanation.

Thematically, Red Angel explores the thin line between sanity and monstrosity. By embedding a psychic connection within a criminal investigation, Clegg suggests that evil can be contagious—transmitted not just through action but through thought, perception, and the fragile boundaries of the mind. The novel asks whether our institutions, designed to contain the criminally insane, might instead be incubators for the very horrors they seek to suppress. Laymon’s inexperience and Campbell’s institutional familiarity create a dual perspective that lets the reader see both the fear of the unknown and the temptation to rationalize the irrational. As the killer descends from the mountains to the valley, the narrative’s tension escalates, mirroring the descent into the darker recesses of human psyche.

Clegg’s ability to deliver “hair‑raising chills” is evident in his vivid, often brutal imagery—bird‑woven necklaces, the oppressive silence of the asylum, the stark brilliance of a desert sunrise that promises no safety. Yet he never sacrifices character for spectacle; Laymon’s determination and Campbell’s haunted past are rendered with enough depth to make their stakes feel personal. The pacing is relentless, chapters ending on hooks that propel the reader forward, while the underlying psychological dread simmers just beneath the surface, ready to erupt.

Fans of Dean Koontz will recognize a familiar thrill in Clegg’s work; indeed, Koontz’s own blurb praises Clegg’s capacity to chill the spine so effectively that readers might want paramedics on standby. Red Angel lives up to that promise, delivering a tightly wound tale where every clue feels like a feather brushed against the skin—light, unsettling, and impossible to ignore.

In sum, Red Angel is a masterclass in blending procedural tension with psychic horror, driven by a style that is both brisk and unnervingly atmospheric. It forces readers to confront the possibility that the most dangerous monsters are not those lurking in the shadows, but the thoughts that whisper from within the walls of our own minds.
