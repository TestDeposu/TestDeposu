# Long Way Home (Jack McNeal #2)

**Author:** J.B. Turner  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2022

Title: Mapping the Legacy: A Critical Look at Knowing Him by Heart

Few historical figures evoke as complex a duality in the American consciousness as Abraham Lincoln. Yet, while mainstream history often reduces the sixteenth president to a singular savior, the Black American intellectual tradition has always engaged in a much deeper, more critical, and fiercely dynamic dialogue with his legacy. Edited by Frederick Hord and Matthew D. Norman, Knowing Him by Heart: African American Voices on Abraham Lincoln is a vital, eye-opening anthology that captures this multifaceted relationship over a span of one hundred and fifty years. 

It is crucial to note immediately that the publisher’s original synopsis suffers from a bizarre metadata mismatch—falsely attributing this profound historical work of non-fiction as Long Way Home, a Jack McNeal thriller by J.B. Turner. Rest assured, there are no fictional detectives or action tropes here. Instead, Hord and Norman have curated a rigorous, emotionally resonant collection of primary sources that map the changing contours of the bond between Black Americans and the Great Emancipator. 

The primary theme of the anthology is the reclamation and constant reshaping of historical memory. The selected writers—ranging from towering historical figures like Frederick Douglass, Ida B. Wells-Barnett, and Mary McLeod Bethune to modern voices like Thurgood Marshall, Malcolm X, Gwendolyn Brooks, Barbara Jeanne Fields, and Barack Obama—are neither blind to Lincoln’s profound racial shortcomings nor dismissive of his monumental impact. Through speeches, personal letters, essays, and searing book excerpts, the book illustrates how Black intellectuals have historically taken Lincoln's ideals and repurposed them as tools for their own liberation, social justice, and political survival. 

The editors’ style is commendable in its letting-the-text-speak approach. Rather than imposing an overwhelming academic narrative, Hord and Norman act as careful curators, allowing the historical trajectory to unfold organically. The progression from post-Civil War reckonings to Civil Rights agitation and into the 21st-century reflections of the Obama era creates a powerful, polyphonic chorus. Readers will witness how the perception of Lincoln shifted from a pragmatic politician to a flawed yet indispensable symbol of American promise, and finally to a contested figure subjected to rigorous deconstruction.

Knowing Him by Heart is essential reading for anyone seeking to understand the intricate architecture of American history, race, and myth-making. It challenges us to look beyond simplistic hagiography and engage with history in all its uncomfortable, evolving complexity.
