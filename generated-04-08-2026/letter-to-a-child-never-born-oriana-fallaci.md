# Letter to a Child Never Born

**Author:** Oriana Fallaci  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1975

**A Raw and Unflinching Dialogue with the Unborn: Oriana Fallaci’s *Letter to a Child Never Born***

Oriana Fallaci’s *Letter to a Child Never Born* (1975) is not a book—it is a confrontation. Written in the wake of her own abortion, this searing monologue forces the reader to sit in the uncomfortable silence of a woman questioning whether life is worth the weight of existence. Fallaci, a journalist known for her fearless interrogations of power and truth, turns her razor-sharp intellect inward, addressing the child she carries as if it were a living, breathing interlocutor. The result is a work that is equal parts philosophical inquiry, personal confession, and a defiant assertion of female autonomy—a book that refuses easy answers and demands moral reckoning.

At its core, *Letter to a Child Never Born* is a meditation on the ethics of creation and the right to self-determination. Fallaci’s protagonist—who, like the author, is pregnant but uncertain—speaks directly to the unborn child, posing questions that cut to the heart of human existence: *Is it just to impose suffering on a being that has no choice in its birth?* The letter oscillates between tenderness and despair, imagining a world where the child might prefer the "joyful limbo" of non-existence over the inevitable trials of life. Fallaci’s prose is unapologetically raw, stripping away the romanticized notions of motherhood to expose the raw, existential dilemma: *What does it mean to bring another soul into a world that may not be kind to it?*

The book’s power lies in its refusal to moralize. Fallaci does not preach; she interrogates. She considers the selfishness of life—how each of us, in our own way, imposes our existence on others—and yet, she does not condemn the act of creation outright. Instead, she lays bare the contradictions: the joy of love, the terror of responsibility, the fear of failure. The letter is a dialogue with the unknown, with the possibility that the child might have chosen differently. And in that uncertainty, Fallaci finds both the beauty and the horror of human freedom.

Her style is unmistakably hers—intense, lyrical, and relentless. Fallaci’s writing is a blend of poetic reflection and journalistic precision, as if she were conducting an interview with the void itself. She leaps from existential philosophy to personal anecdote, from the history of abortion to the intimate details of her own body. There is no sugarcoating; no false comfort. The result is a work that is as unsettling as it is profound, forcing the reader to confront the same questions she did: *What would you do if you could choose not to be born?*

The book also challenges the societal expectations placed on women. Fallaci’s protagonist is not a passive figure; she is a woman who must decide whether to surrender her autonomy to the demands of motherhood. The letter becomes a manifesto for female agency, arguing that the right to life should extend to the right to decline it. In an era when women’s bodies were (and still are) policed and controlled, Fallaci’s defiance is radical. She does not glorify abortion; she does not condemn it. She simply asks: *Who gets to decide?*

*Letter to a Child Never Born* is not an easy read. It is a book that lingers, that refuses to let go, because it forces us to stare into the abyss of our own mortality and choice. Fallaci’s genius lies in her ability to turn a personal tragedy into a universal question, one that resonates across time and culture. It is a book for anyone who has ever wondered about the meaning of existence—or the right to refuse it.

In the end, *Letter to a Child Never Born* is more than a book about abortion; it is a book about the fragility of life, the courage of choice, and the terrifying beauty of human freedom. Fallaci does not offer answers. She does not need to. She simply holds up a mirror and asks: *What would you do if you could choose not to be here at all?*
