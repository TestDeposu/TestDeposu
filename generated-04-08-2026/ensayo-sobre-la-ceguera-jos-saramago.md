# Ensayo sobre la ceguera

**Author:** José Saramago  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

Ensayo sobre la ceguera (Blindness) by José Saramago is a harrowing allegory that erupts from a single, inexplicable moment: a man waiting at a red traffic light suddenly loses his sight. What follows is not merely a medical mystery but a swift, merciless contagion that blankets an unnamed city in a “white blindness.” As the afflicted are herded into makeshift quarantines and the healthy scramble to avoid infection, Saramago strips away the veneer of civilization to expose the raw, primal instincts that lie beneath. The novel’s central theme is the fragility of social order when the sense that anchors us to shared reality—vision—fails, and with it, the moral compass that guides collective responsibility.

Saramago’s narrative style is as distinctive as the epidemic he depicts. Long, sinuous sentences flow without the conventional punctuation of dialogue marks, creating a breathless, almost oral rhythm that pulls the reader into the characters’ disorientation. He employs a sparse, almost detached third‑person voice that occasionally shifts into collective “we,” blurring the line between individual experience and communal fate. This technique mirrors the loss of personal boundaries that blindness imposes: identities merge, names disappear, and humanity is reduced to its most elemental drives—hunger, fear, cruelty, and, unexpectedly, tenderness. The author’s use of irony and dark humor does not soften the horror; rather, it sharpens the critique, allowing readers to laugh nervously at the absurdity of bureaucratic inefficiency while feeling the weight of genuine suffering.

The quarantine becomes a micro‑society where power structures invert. Those who retain sight—whether by feigning blindness or by genuine immunity—exploit their advantage, trading food, shelter, and even sexual favors for survival. Yet amid the degradation, Saramago offers glimpses of grace: a woman who nurses the sick without expectation of reward, a child who clings to a sense of wonder despite the darkness, and a doctor who refuses to abandon his oath. These moments are not sentimental consolations; they are quiet affirmations that empathy can persist even when the world is literally blind. The novel’s famous refrain—“Inside us there is something that has no name, that something is what we are”—encapsulates Saramago’s belief that an essential, indefinable core of humanity endures beyond sensory perception and social constructs.

Historically, readers have drawn parallels between the white blindness and various real‑world crises: the AIDS epidemic, refugee displacement, governmental neglect of the elderly, and the moral blindness that accompanies war and authoritarianism. Saramago himself resisted assigning a single allegorical target, insisting that the story’s power lies in its universality. The blindness is both literal and metaphorical—a failure to see the suffering of others, a refusal to acknowledge our shared vulnerability, and a willingness to let self‑preservation eclipse solidarity. In this way, the novel functions as a moral mirror, compelling readers to confront their own capacities for compassion and complicity.

Stylistically, Saramago’s avoidance of quotation marks and his tendency to embed dialogue within the narrative stream create a sense of immediacy that mirrors the characters’ disorienting experience. The prose moves like a tide—sometimes gentle, sometimes overwhelming—forcing the reader to adapt, just as the blind must learn to navigate a world without visual cues. The lack of clear chapter breaks and the occasional shift into present tense heighten the feeling that the events are unfolding in real time, resisting the comfort of retrospective analysis.

Ultimately, Ensayo sobre la ceguera is less a story about disease than a meditation on the ethics of seeing. It asks what it means to truly perceive another’s pain when our own senses are compromised, and whether sight—physical or moral—can be reclaimed through acts of love and solidarity. Saramago does not offer easy answers; instead, he leaves the reader with an unsettling, yet hopeful, reminder that even in a world plunged into white darkness, the capacity to “see” each other persists, waiting to be awakened.
