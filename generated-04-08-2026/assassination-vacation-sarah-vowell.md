# Assassination Vacation

**Author:** Sarah Vowell  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2005

If you have ever wondered what happens when a brilliant, history-obsessed public radio humorist packs her bags to stalk the ghosts of America’s slain presidents, Sarah Vowell’s Assassination Vacation is your answer. Part travelogue, part dark comedy, and entirely compulsive, Vowell’s 2005 bestselling book transforms the macabre history of American political assassination into a surprisingly delightful, cross-country road trip. 

At its core, the book explores a uniquely American paradox: our national obsession with the violent ends of our commanders-in-chief versus the sanitized, commercialized memory palaces we build in their honor. Vowell does not merely recount the grim details of the deaths of Abraham Lincoln, James A. Garfield, and William McKinley. Instead, she investigates how these national traumas have been packaged for popular consumption—exploited for politics, architecture, sculpture, and, most importantly, historical tourism. 

What elevates Assassination Vacation from a standard historical overview into a literary triumph is Vowell’s inimitable voice. Known for her deadpan NPR delivery, she narrates her journey with a signature blend of wisecracking humor, remarkable intellectual honesty, and sharp, thought-provoking criticism. She acts as a cynical yet deeply affectionate tour guide, dragging us from the Ford's Theatre in Washington, D.C., all the way to the remote reaches of Alaska and the baking bricks of the Dry Tortugas. Along the way, she manages to unearth the most eccentric historical footnotes, treating readers to bizarre historical detours involving 19th-century biblical sex cults, spiteful totem poles, traveling mummies, and Broadway show tunes. 

Vowell’s historical curiosity is infectious. She introduces us to figures like the ultimate historical jinx, Robert Todd Lincoln—a man who managed to be physically present at, or chillingly close to, the assassinations of three different presidents. She details the petty politics and bureaucratic squabbling that birthed the iconic Lincoln Memorial, stripping away the marble reverence to reveal the messy human ambition underneath. 

Yet, beneath the snark and the snappy observations, Vowell touches on something deeply vulnerable about the American psyche. She interrogates the themes of loss and political violence, mapping out how the fractures of the past continue to shape our modern political landscape—even taking time to trace the evolutionary roots of the Republican Party. She reminds us that the history of the United States is written not just in ink and constitutions, but frequently in spilled blood, and that our monuments are as much about how we want to be seen as they are about who we actually were.

Assassination Vacation is a rare piece of non-fiction that manages to be wildly entertaining while expanding your understanding of the nation’s cultural DNA. It is funny, slightly unhinged, deeply researched, and wholly unforgettable. Whether you are a history buff or a casual traveler looking for a literate escape, Vowell proves that exploring the dark corners of America's past has never been so much fun.
