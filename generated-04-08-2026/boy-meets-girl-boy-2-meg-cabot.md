# Boy Meets Girl (Boy, #2)

**Author:** Meg Cabot  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Boy Meets Girl, the second installment in Meg Cabot’s “Boy” series, thrusts readers into the chaotic world of Kate Mackenzie—a New York‑based HR assistant whose life is a comedy of errors wrapped in a love‑story cloak. The novel’s central tension hinges on the clash between professional duty and personal longing: Kate must fire a beloved senior employee, only to find herself on the receiving end of a wrongful‑termination lawsuit and forced into a deposition with Mitch Hertzog, a polished, wealthy lawyer whose smile is as disarming as his pedigree is intimidating. What begins as a courtroom showdown morphs into an unexpected romance, proving that love can surface in the most unlikely of arenas—especially when the parties involved are as mismatched as a corporate enforcer and a blue‑blooded litigator.

Cabot’s style shines here with her trademark blend of snappy dialogue, incisive wit, and a keen eye for the absurdities of modern office life. She balances the legal procedural details—depositions, arbitration, corporate hierarchies—with a light‑hearted, almost breezy tone that never lets the stakes feel oppressive. Kate’s first‑person narration is peppered with self‑deprecating humor and pop‑culture references, making her both relatable and endearing. The author’s pacing is brisk; scenes shift from the claustrophobic confines of the T.O.D.’s office to the opulent corridors of Mitch’s law firm with a kinetic energy that mirrors Kate’s own whirlwind emotional state. Cabot’s ability to weave humor into tension keeps the narrative lively, ensuring that even the most procedural moments feel charged with romantic possibility.

Thematically, Boy Meets Girl explores the idea that authenticity can flourish amid façade. Kate’s initial defensiveness—stemming from years of unfulfilled commitment and a cramped couch‑surfing existence—gradually gives way to vulnerability as she confronts both her career anxieties and her fear of intimacy. Mitch, meanwhile, represents the allure of stability and tradition, yet his genuine interest in Kate challenges his own preconceptions about love and success. Their evolving relationship interrogates the notion that professional success and personal happiness are mutually exclusive, suggesting instead that honesty—both in the workplace and in the heart—can bridge even the widest social divides.

Cabot’s second “Boy” novel is a delightful romp that satisfies fans of romantic comedy while offering a subtle commentary on workplace power dynamics. It’s a quick, satisfying read that leaves you rooting for the unlikely pair and smiling at the absurdities of corporate America—proving that, sometimes, the best depositions end with a kiss.
