# They Never Learn

**Author:** Layne Fargo  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

In the landscape of modern psychological thrillers, it is rare to find a novel that balances visceral, cathartic rage with the cold, surgical precision of a procedural. Layne Fargo’s 2020 novel, They Never Learn, does exactly that, offering a dark, unapologetic exploration of institutional toxicity and the violent lengths to which women are driven when justice is not merely delayed, but actively denied.

The narrative architecture of the book is its greatest strength. Fargo interweaves two distinct threads: the calculated, ritualistic serial killings of Professor Scarlett Clark, who treats the eradication of predatory men as an annual academic duty, and the desperate, reactionary descent of freshman Carly Schiller. While Scarlett represents the apex of the "femme fatale" archetype—meticulous, brilliant, and utterly detached—Carly serves as the grounded, messy emotional core. Watching the two trajectories eventually collide is less about the suspense of "whodunnit" and more about the transgressive thrill of "what will they do next?"

Fargo’s writing style is razor-sharp. She possesses a rare talent for making the reader complicit in her protagonists' actions. You aren't just reading about a serial killer; you are effectively cheering for one. The prose is lean and atmospheric, perfectly capturing the suffocating, performative nature of university life. Fargo understands the micro-aggressions and power dynamics that plague higher education, transforming a college campus—usually a setting of intellectual growth—into a hunting ground where the curriculum is rewritten in blood.

The central theme of the novel is the institutional protection of the "bad man." Scarlett Clark’s victims aren't just individuals; they are symptoms of a system that shields abusers behind tenure, status, and the fragility of their own reputations. Fargo isn't interested in redemption arcs or moral hand-wringing. Instead, she leans into the "ragestorm" aspect of the story. She invites the reader to sit with the discomfort of vigilante justice, questioning how we define morality when the traditional avenues of accountability have been systematically dismantled.

There is a distinct "Killing Eve" energy here, particularly in the way Fargo toys with the concept of the female predator. Scarlett is not a victim of circumstance; she is an agent of change, albeit a lethal one. Her interactions with the investigation into her own crimes add a layer of delicious irony that keeps the pacing tight and the tension high. Meanwhile, Carly’s arc provides the necessary contrast—the evolution from a traumatized student to a woman who realizes that she, too, has the capacity for violence when pushed to the brink.

Ultimately, They Never Learn is a provocation. It is a book that demands you pick a side, even when the lines between hero and villain are intentionally blurred into an ink-black smear. Fargo has crafted a story that is as intellectually stimulating as it is pulse-pounding, proving that sometimes, the only way to silence the noise of a broken system is to ensure the perpetrators never learn from their mistakes because they are no longer around to make them.

This is not a book for the faint of heart or those seeking a comfortable resolution. It is a bold, subversive, and unflinching examination of power, trauma, and the dark satisfaction of leveling the playing field by any means necessary.
