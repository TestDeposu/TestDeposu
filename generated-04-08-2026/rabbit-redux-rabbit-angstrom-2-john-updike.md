# Rabbit Redux (Rabbit Angstrom, #2)

**Author:** John Updike  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 1971

John Updike’s 1971 masterpiece, Rabbit Redux, does what few sequels manage to achieve: it takes a definitive character study and expands it into a monumental canvas of a shifting American psyche. Ten years after we first met the fleet-footed, anxious former high school basketball star in Rabbit, Run, Harry "Rabbit" Angstrom is no longer the agile youth fleeing the claustrophobia of domesticity. At thirty-six, he is a paunchy, working-class linotyper holding fast to a fading conservative worldview, entirely unequipped for the dizzying, radioactive fallout of 1969. 

If the first novel captured the quiet, suffocating stagnation of Eisenhower’s America, Rabbit Redux plunges headfirst into the lurid, hallucinogenic turmoil of the Vietnam era. The backdrop is a nation tearing at the seams, defined by the Apollo 11 moon landing, racial tension, the counterculture, casual drug use, and violent generational fracture. Rabbit’s suburban life implodes when his wife, Janice, abandons him for a car salesman, leaving his home to be effectively hijacked by an unlikely and combustible household: Jill, a wealthy runaway desperate for self-destruction, and Skeeter, a militant, highly articulate Black radical on the run. 

Updike’s central theme is the agonizing friction between personal nostalgia and historical inevitability. Rabbit is the ultimate American Everyman, desperately clinging to semblances of decency, patriotism, and order while the ground literally shifts beneath his feet. He yearns to belong, to believe in something larger than himself, yet he is hopelessly trapped in his own provincial prejudices and physical appetites. Through Rabbit’s uneasy accommodation with Jill and Skeeter, Updike explores the racial and cultural fault lines of the late sixties with unflinching honesty, refusing to offer easy moral binaries. Instead, he presents a country—and a soul—in violent, painful transition.

What elevates Rabbit Redux from a period piece into enduring literature is Updike’s inimitable prose style. His writing is nothing short of alchemy. Updike possesses a terrifyingly acute eye for the banal and the transcendent alike, describing the grease of a linotype machine, the neon glow of a parking lot, or the texture of human skin with the same lyrical, breathless intensity. His metaphors are startling, sensual, and relentlessly precise, turning the mundane tragedies of middle-class American life into matters of high spiritual drama. Updike does not merely narrate Rabbit’s life; he forces the reader to inhabit Rabbit’s senses, making his failures, hypocrisies, and blind yearnings agonizingly palpable.

Decades after its publication, Rabbit Redux feels startlingly prophetic. It anticipates the culture wars and ideological polarization that continue to define the American landscape. It is a brilliant, uncomfortable, and breathtakingly written portrait of a man—and a nation—forced to reckon with the death of their own mythologies. For anyone seeking to understand the turbulent soul of twentieth-century America through the lens of a deeply flawed, unforgettable protagonist, this novel remains an absolute imperative.
