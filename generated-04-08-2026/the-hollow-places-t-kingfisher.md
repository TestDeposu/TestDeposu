# The Hollow Places

**Author:** T. Kingfisher  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2020

**The Hollow Places by T. Kingfisher: A Descent into the Unseen**

T. Kingfisher’s *The Hollow Places* is a novel that burrows beneath the skin, twisting the familiar into something vast and unsettling. It’s a story about the spaces we don’t see—the cracks in the walls, the doors we never open, the places we’re told not to look. Kara, a recently divorced woman returning to her childhood home, stumbles upon one such place: a hidden bunker behind a rotting panel in her uncle’s house. Inside, she finds a single phrase, carved into the wall: *"Pray they are hungry."* What follows is a descent into a labyrinth of alternate realities, where the boundaries between fear and reality dissolve like sugar in water.

Kingfisher’s prose is sharp, precise, and dripping with a dark, poetic dread. She doesn’t just tell us that something is wrong—she makes us *feel* the weight of it, the way the air thickens when you’re standing too close to a thing you shouldn’t. The novel’s horror isn’t just in the monsters lurking beyond the portals but in the way fear itself becomes a weapon. The creatures that inhabit these hollow places don’t just attack; they *listen*. They feed on the thoughts that haunt you, the doubts that gnaw at your bones. The more you fear them, the stronger they grow. It’s a chilling inversion of the old adage that fear is the mind-killer—here, fear is the mind’s *nourishment*.

The novel’s strength lies in its ability to blend the mundane with the monstrous. Kara’s personal struggles—her divorce, her loneliness, the slow erosion of her self-worth—are woven into the fabric of the story. The portals she discovers aren’t just gateways to other worlds; they’re mirrors reflecting her own fractured psyche. Kingfisher doesn’t shy away from the messiness of human emotion, using it to heighten the terror. The horror isn’t just in the unknown; it’s in the recognition that the things we fear most often live inside us.

The Hollow Places also plays with the idea of the "hollow"—not just as a physical space but as an absence, a void. The bunker is a hollow place, a gap in the world that shouldn’t exist. The portals lead to other hollow places, other versions of reality where things are missing, where the rules don’t apply. It’s a theme that resonates deeply, especially in a world where we’re increasingly aware of the gaps in our understanding of reality itself. Kingfisher doesn’t offer easy answers, and that’s part of its power. The novel lingers in the mind long after the last page, like a half-remembered dream.

Kingfisher’s writing is rich with atmosphere, drawing comparisons to the surreal horror of Guillermo del Toro’s *Pan’s Labyrinth*. There’s a fairy-tale quality to the story, but it’s a fairy tale where the monsters are real, and the magic is a curse. The prose is lush and evocative, painting pictures of decay and wonder with equal skill. She has a knack for finding the grotesque in the beautiful and the beautiful in the grotesque, making the novel feel both intimate and vast.

At its core, *The Hollow Places* is a story about the things we bury and the things that bury us. It’s about the fear of the unknown, the terror of our own thoughts, and the way we all, at some point, stand at the edge of a hole and wonder what’s waiting on the other side. Kingfisher doesn’t just scare us; she makes us *look*, even when we don’t want to. And that’s what makes this novel so compelling—it doesn’t just tell a story; it forces you to see the world differently.

If you’re looking for a horror novel that’s as thought-provoking as it is terrifying, *The Hollow Places* is a must-read. It’s a book that will stay with you, long after the last portal has closed behind you.
