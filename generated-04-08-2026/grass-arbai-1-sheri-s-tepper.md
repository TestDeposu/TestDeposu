# Grass (Arbai, #1)

**Author:** Sheri S. Tepper  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** Unknown

Sheri S. Tepper was a master of ecological science fiction, a writer who used the tropes of the genre not merely for spectacle, but as a scalpel to dissect the human condition. In Grass, the first installment of the Arbai trilogy, Tepper constructs a universe that feels both chillingly bureaucratic and breathtakingly alien. It is a novel that operates on a grand, planetary scale while simultaneously plumbing the depths of religious dogma, patriarchal control, and humanity's fraught relationship with the natural world.

The narrative unfolds on Grass, a world almost entirely covered in the titular vegetation—a vast, undulating sea of grass that is home to native life forms both magnificent and terrifying. Humans have colonized this remote planet, living under a strict, archaic feudal society governed by the religious orthodoxy of the Sanctity. The ruling class, obsessed with tradition, hunting, and maintaining the status quo, strictly forbids interference with the planet's ecosystem, particularly the majestic, carnivorous hippoid creatures known as the Hounds, which are hunted in ritualistic fashion by the planetary elite.

Into this rigid, stagnant society steps Marjorie Westriding Yrarier, an investigator dispatched by the overarching galactic government to understand a mysterious plague sweeping humanity across the stars. Marjorie is an outsider, a woman of intellect and skepticism, and her arrival serves as the catalyst for the unraveling of centuries of deception. As she delves into the mysteries of Grass, she uncovers uncomfortable truths about the planet's history, the nature of the native life, and the long-extinct alien race known as the Arbai.

Tepper’s style is characterized by a slow-burn, immersive world-building that rewards patience. She does not rush to explain her universe; instead, she lets the culture, the stark landscapes, and the oppressive social structures breathe. Her prose is incisive, often laced with a sharp, satirical wit regarding human bureaucracy and religious hypocrisy. Yet, when she turns her attention to the natural world, her writing blooms into something lyrical and haunting. The plains of Grass are not just a setting; they are a living, breathing entity that dwarfs the human drama unfolding upon them.

At its core, the main theme of Grass is empathy—or the catastrophic lack thereof. Tepper explores how humanity's survival depends on our ability to listen to and respect the ecosystems we inhabit, rather than attempting to dominate and subjugate them. She contrasts the rigid, life-denying dogmas of human religion with the harmonious, evolutionary truths of the natural world. Through Marjorie’s eyes, the novel asks a profound question: what does it truly mean to be intelligent, and are humans the apex of creation or merely a destructive parasite?

Grass is a triumph of speculative fiction that challenges the reader to look beyond the surface of conquest and civilization. It is a work of intellectual rigor and emotional depth, proving that the most compelling science fiction is often that which holds up a mirror to our own terrestrial follies. Tepper has crafted a timeless masterpiece that remains as vital and urgent today as when it was first conceived.
