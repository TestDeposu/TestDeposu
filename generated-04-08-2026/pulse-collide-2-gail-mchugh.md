# Pulse (Collide, #2)

**Author:** Gail McHugh  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

In the high-stakes world of contemporary romance, the love triangle remains the ultimate proving ground for an author’s ability to manipulate tension, desire, and moral ambiguity. In Pulse, the second installment of Gail McHugh’s Collide series, the stakes are ratcheted up to a fever pitch. McHugh doesn’t just offer a choice between two men; she crafts a visceral collision between safety and obsession, forcing her protagonist, Emily Cooper, to navigate the wreckage of a life she thought she had mapped out.

The central theme of the novel is the deceptive nature of comfort versus the transformative power of intensity. Emily begins as a woman tethered to the familiar. Dillon Parker, her boyfriend, represents the "perfect" life—a roadmap of stability that, on the surface, is exactly what a grieving young woman should crave. However, McHugh’s brilliance lies in her slow, methodical peeling away of that domestic veneer. As the narrative progresses, the "perfect" boyfriend becomes a suffocating presence, and the "dark, mysterious stranger"—the notorious Gavin Blake—transforms from a seductive temptation into the only person who forces Emily to truly wake up.

McHugh’s writing style is unmistakably geared toward the reader who craves emotional volatility. She writes with a cinematic urgency that mimics the racing heartbeats of her characters. There is little room for quiet contemplation in the world of Pulse; instead, the prose is drenched in sensory details—the electricity of a touch, the suffocating atmosphere of New York City, and the raw, unvarnished ache of long-buried secrets. Her dialogue is sharp and often confrontational, mirroring the inner warfare Emily wages against her own intuition. McHugh understands that for a romance to feel "dangerous," the reader must feel the vertigo of the cliffside right along with the heroine.

What makes this entry in the series particularly compelling is the exploration of agency. Too often, heroines in this genre are passive recipients of affection or conflict. Emily, however, is constantly grappling with the consequences of her choices. McHugh forces the reader to sit with the discomfort of Emily’s betrayal and her desire. The author avoids the easy path of painting one suitor as a villain and the other as a saint; instead, she paints them both in shades of gray, leaving Emily—and the reader—to determine which flavor of chaos is worth the sacrifice.

If the first book set the stage for the attraction, Pulse is where McHugh burns the bridge. It is a story about the terrifying realization that the life you’ve built may not be the life you were meant to live. The pacing is relentless, reflecting the frantic, impulsive rhythm of early adult discovery. For those who enjoy romance that balances heat with the genuine, gut-wrenching pain of identity crisis, this book delivers. McHugh is a master at turning the page-turner into an emotional gauntlet, ensuring that by the time you reach the resolution, you are just as breathless and conflicted as the woman caught in the middle of it all.

Pulse is not merely a romance; it is a testament to the fact that sometimes you have to lose your heart to finally find your pulse.
