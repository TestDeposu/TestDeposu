# This Tender Land

**Author:** William Kent Krueger  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

Odysseus on the Mississippi: A Review of William Kent Krueger’s *This Tender Land*

In the canon of American storytelling, certain landscapes demand their own mythologies. William Kent Krueger’s 2019 bestseller, *This Tender Land*, stakes its claim in that rich literary soil where historical hardship meets timeless folklore. Set against the bruised backdrop of 1932 America, deep in the grip of the Great Depression, the novel is an enthralling, big-hearted epic that feels less like a contemporary release and more like a long-lost classic hauled up from the depths of the river it so vividly honors.

At its emotional core, the book follows four young outcasts fleeing the brutal confines of the Lincoln Indian Training School in Minnesota. Odie O’Banion, a defiant orphan with a penchant for trouble and a gift for storytelling, leads his older brother Albert, their mute best friend Mose, and a grieving little girl named Emmy down the Gilead River and into the vast, indifferent current of the Mississippi. What begins as a desperate escape quickly transforms into an odyssey of survival, self-discovery, and grace. 

Krueger’s greatest triumph in *This Tender Land* lies in his handling of theme. The novel operates as a masterclass in examining the resilience of the human spirit when stripped of safety, family, and home. The journey down the river is a physical manifestation of grief and healing. As the quartet drifts past struggling farmers, charismatic yet fraudulent faith healers, and Hoovervilles choked by despair, Krueger explores how trauma isolates us, but how shared empathy can ultimately stitch us back together. The America of 1932 is a scarred country, yet through the clear-eyed perspective of children, Krueger reveals a nation that is still capable of staggering beauty and unexpected redemption. 

Stylistically, Krueger writes with a lyrical reverence for the natural world. The American landscape is not merely a backdrop for the action; it is a breathing, active participant in the characters' lives. The river roars, whispers, threatens, and cradles. There is a palpable sense of place in every chapter—the smell of pine and damp earth, the oppressive heat of midsummer, and the heavy, star-studded expanse of the Midwestern night sky. Comparisons to literary touchstones like *Where the Crawdads Sing* and Mark Twain’s *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* are inevitable and well-earned, yet Krueger’s voice remains distinctively his own. He balances gritty realism with a touch of the mystical, ensuring that the harsh realities of the Depression era are softened by a lingering sense of wonder.

The pacing mirrors the rhythm of the river itself—moments of breathless, rapid danger give way to long, reflective stretches where the reader is invited to simply sit with the characters and absorb their internal landscapes. Odie’s narration is pitch-perfect: sharp, vulnerable, poetic, and utterly convincing. 

*This Tender Land* is a triumph of atmospheric fiction and emotional depth. It reminds us that no matter how far we drift from safety, we are all connected by the stories we carry and the landscapes that claim us. Krueger has crafted a magnificent journey that lingers in the heart and mind long after the final page is turned, cementing his status as a premier voice in modern American fiction.
