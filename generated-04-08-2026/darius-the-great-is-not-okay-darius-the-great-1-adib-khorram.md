# Darius the Great Is Not Okay (Darius The Great, #1)

**Author:** Adib Khorram  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2018

Darius Kellner’s world is a careful balance of half‑remembered Farsi phrases, relentless Klingon trivia, and the quiet ache of clinical depression that makes everyday interactions feel like navigating a foreign language without a translator. In Adib Khorram’s debut, Darius the Great Is Not Okay, that tension becomes the engine of a story that is as hilariously specific as it is universally resonant. The novel’s central theme—searching for belonging when you straddle two cultures and two selves—unfolds with a tenderness that never shies away from the messy realities of mental health, immigrant legacy, and adolescent yearning.

Khorram’s prose is immediate and intimate, rendered in a close third‑person voice that feels like Darius himself is whispering his thoughts directly into the reader’s ear. He balances sharp, self‑deprecating humor (“I speak better Klingon than Farsi”) with moments of stark honesty about medication, therapy, and the weight of expectations from both his American school and his Iranian grandparents. The humor never undercuts the emotional depth; instead, it highlights Darius’s coping mechanisms and makes his vulnerability all the more palpable. When Darius arrives in Iran, the setting becomes a character in its own right—bustling bazaars, the scent of saffron‑infused rice, the call to prayer echoing over rooftops—each detail rendered with sensory richness that grounds the internal landscape in something tangible.

The friendship with Sohrab, the boy next door who affectionately dubs him “Darioush,” is the novel’s beating heart. Their easy camaraderie—soccer matches, late‑night conversations on a secret rooftop, shared plates of faludeh—offers Darius a mirror in which he can see a version of himself unburdened by the hyphenated label “Fractional Persian.” Khorram captures the transformative power of being seen and accepted for who you truly are, not who you think you ought to be. Through Sohrab’s unwavering kindness, Darius begins to reconcile the fractured parts of his identity, realizing that adequacy isn’t a fixed point but a fluid, negotiated state that shifts with love, understanding, and self‑compassion.

Stylistically, Khorram employs a deft interweaving of Persian words and cultural references without italicizing or over‑explaining them, trusting the reader to infer meaning from context—a technique that immerses us in Darius’s bicultural experience while respecting the authenticity of his voice. The narrative pacing mirrors Darius’s internal rhythm: quick, witty exchanges punctuated by slower, introspective passages that allow the weight of his depression to settle before lifting again with a burst of humor or a simple, shared laugh. This ebb and flow keeps the reading experience dynamic, never allowing the heavier topics to dominate the tone, yet never letting the levity diminish their importance.

Ultimately, Darius the Great Is Not Okay is a celebration of the small, brave steps toward self‑acceptance. It reminds readers that feeling “not okay” is not a permanent state but a stepping stone toward finding the people and places that help us become more than okay—perhaps even extraordinary. Khorram’s debut is a shining example of contemporary YA that balances humor, heart, and cultural specificity, offering a story that will linger long after the final page is turned.
