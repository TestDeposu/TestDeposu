# The Hen Who Dreamed She Could Fly

**Author:** Sun-mi Hwang  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

In the grand tradition of literary fables that use the animal kingdom to hold a mirror up to the human experience, Sun-mi Hwang’s The Hen Who Dreamed She Could Fly stands as a quiet, devastating, and deeply beautiful masterpiece. Often marketed as the Korean Charlotte’s Web, the comparison feels both accurate and perhaps a touch reductive. While it shares the pastoral charm and emotional stakes of E.B. White’s classic, Hwang’s prose carries a stark, unflinching existential weight that is uniquely, hauntingly her own.

The story follows Sprout, a hen whose existence is defined by the repetitive, sterile labor of an industrial farm. Her life is a cycle of laying eggs that are promptly stolen, an existence that leaves her spirit brittle and yearning. When she finally breaks free of the coop, she discovers that freedom is not merely the absence of a cage; it is a precarious, dangerous landscape where the laws of nature are indifferent to her dreams. The central tension of the book—and its greatest triumph—is the intersection of the universal desire for self-actualization and the heavy, sacrificial burden of motherhood.

Hwang’s writing style is deceptively simple. She employs a spare, fable-like tone that masks the profound philosophical inquiries embedded in the narrative. There is no sentimentality here, even when the book leans into the heart-wrenching realities of its protagonist’s life. Hwang understands that life, particularly for a creature in the wild, is a series of trade-offs. Sprout’s journey toward independence is inextricably linked to her decision to adopt a duckling, a choice that forces her to confront the reality of her own limitations and the inevitable divergence of their paths.

The brilliance of the book lies in how it handles the concept of "the wild." Usually depicted as a place of romantic liberation, in Hwang’s hands, it is a place of biting cold, looming predators, and harsh hierarchies. Yet, within this unforgiving world, Sprout finds a sense of purpose that the coop could never provide. She is a heroine who refuses to be defined by her utility, rejecting the status of a "layer" to become a mother, a guardian, and an individual. Her rebellion is not one of loud grandstanding, but of quiet, persistent courage.

As a reader, you are struck by how quickly this barnyard drama evolves into a meditation on sacrifice. Hwang does not shy away from the biological imperatives that dictate the lives of her characters, nor does she soften the edges of the predator-prey relationship. By framing these harsh truths through the eyes of a hen, she invites the reader to empathize with the marginalized and the overlooked—those who, like Sprout, dream of lives they were not "designed" to lead.

The Hen Who Dreamed She Could Fly is a lean, punchy, and emotionally resonant work of literature. It is a book that can be read in a single sitting, yet it leaves an imprint that lasts for years. It is a testament to the idea that even if our wings are clipped, the capacity to dream of the sky is what makes us truly alive. Whether you are a fan of allegorical fiction or simply looking for a story that captures the raw, jagged beauty of the maternal spirit, this is an essential addition to your shelf.
