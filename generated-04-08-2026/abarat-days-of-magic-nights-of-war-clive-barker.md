# Abarat: Days of Magic, Nights of War

**Author:** Clive Barker  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

Clive Barker is an architect of the sublime, a writer who treats the boundary between the mundane and the monstrous as a thin, fraying veil. In the second volume of his sprawling Abarat cycle, Days of Magic, Nights of War, Barker cements his reputation as a master of dark, kaleidoscopic fantasy. While the series is categorized as young adult fiction, this is a label that feels woefully insufficient; Barker’s work possesses a grotesque, painterly weight that defies the cozy tropes of the genre.

The narrative continues to center on Candy Quackenbush, a girl who has been pulled from the gray, soul-crushing boredom of Chickentown, Minnesota, into the Abarat—a breathtaking archipelago where each of the twenty-five islands corresponds to an hour of the day. As the title suggests, the whimsy of the first installment has begun to ossify into something far more jagged and menacing. The war promised in the title is not merely a clash of swords and sorcery, but a collision of ontologies.

Barker’s prose is, as always, his greatest weapon. He writes with the sensory overload of a fever dream, populating his islands with creatures so strange they feel pulled from the margins of a Bosch painting. His world-building is expansive and uncompromising; the geography of the Abarat is not merely a stage for the plot, but a character in its own right, one that breathes, shifts, and bleeds. In Days of Magic, Nights of War, the tone shifts definitively toward the melancholic and the apocalyptic. The "Days of Magic" are rapidly being eclipsed by the "Nights of War," and Barker excels at capturing the specific dread of a world standing on the precipice of an irreversible change.

At the heart of the book is the encroaching shadow of Mater Motley, a villainess who stands among Barker’s most terrifying creations. She is the antithesis of Candy’s youthful, messy idealism. Through their conflict, Barker explores a recurring theme in his body of work: the necessity of the "monstrous" as a catalyst for growth. Candy is no longer the wide-eyed wanderer of the opening pages; she is being hardened by the friction of an archipelago that demands sacrifice. 

The brilliance of Barker’s style lies in his refusal to speak down to his audience. He trusts the reader to navigate the surreal, allowing the darkness to loom large without offering the comfort of easy resolutions. He balances visceral horror with moments of profound, aching beauty, reminding us that true fantasy is not about escape, but about the terrifying, exhilarating act of seeing the world anew.

If the first book was an invitation to the party, this second entry is the moment the lights go out and the doors are bolted shut. It is a darker, more ambitious, and deeply immersive experience. Whether you are a long-time devotee of Barker’s dark mythologies or a newcomer drawn to the promise of his singular vision, Days of Magic, Nights of War is a necessary, if chilling, descent. It is a reminder that in the Abarat, every hour holds a secret, and not all of them are meant to be kept in the light.
