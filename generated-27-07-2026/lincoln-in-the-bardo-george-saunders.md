# Lincoln in the Bardo

**Author:** George Saunders  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2017

George Saunders’s 2017 Booker Prize-winning masterpiece, Lincoln in the Bardo, is not merely a historical novel; it is a profound metaphysical exploration of grief, love, and the fragile boundary between existence and the unknown. Taking a fleeting historical footnote—reports that a grieving Abraham Lincoln visited the crypt of his eleven-year-old son, Willie, in February 1862—Saunders shatters the conventions of traditional storytelling. He launches the reader into a dazzling, spectral netherworld that redefines how we comprehend sorrow and memory.

At the heart of the novel lies the Tibetan Buddhist concept of the *bardo*, a transitional state between death and rebirth. Trapped in this haunting Georgetown cemetery are souls who refuse to accept their fate. Bound by earthly regrets, unfinished business, or sheer terror, these restless spirits—a chorus of the dead featuring historical figures and invented commoners alike—hover in a state of denial. They refer to their coffins as "sick-boxes," their corpses as "transition-ment," and their ghostly reality as a lingering dream from which they hope to wake. 

Into this ghostly waiting room stumbles young Willie Lincoln. Unaware that he has succumbed to typhoid fever, the boy waits for a father who promised to return. When the President himself breaches the cemetery gates, wrapping his arms around his dead son's cold body, the grief-stricken mourning of a shattered father intersects with the collective yearnings of the dead. This pivotal moment elevates the novel from a clever literary experiment into a devastatingly moving meditation on human suffering. 

What makes *Lincoln in the Bardo* an unforgettable triumph is Saunders’s breathtaking and radical stylistic innovation. Known previously as a master of the short story, Saunders constructs his debut novel as a polyphonic collage. The narrative is stitched together from hundreds of competing voices—fragments of historical texts, genuine and fabricated newspaper clippings, diary entries, and the overlapping, cacophonous chatter of the cemetery inhabitants. This kaleidoscopic approach might have felt chaotic in lesser hands, but under Saunders’s guidance, it becomes a brilliant orchestration. The chorus of ghosts provides comic relief, philosophical debate, and heartbreaking vulnerability, mirroring the chaotic nature of human consciousness itself.

Through this avant-garde structure, Saunders masterfully tackles his core theme: how we live and love when we know that everything we cherish must eventually end. The civil war raging outside the cemetery walls provides a macrocosm of national agony, perfectly mirrored by the microcosm of personal bereavement felt by Lincoln. The President is burdened not only by the loss of his son but by the mounting casualties of a fractured nation. Saunders humanizes an American icon by stripping away the marble mythology, leaving behind a grieving father whose immense sorrow mirrors the collective trauma of a country tearing itself apart.

Ultimately, *Lincoln in the Bardo* demonstrates that even the most unspeakable anguish can be bridged through empathy and a shared human voice. Saunders pushes the boundaries of fiction to offer a work that is formally daring, emotionally staggering, and utterly timeless. It is a literary seance that demands to be read, felt, and remembered.
