# Miss Lonelyhearts / The Day of the Locust

**Author:** Nathanael West  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2009

Nathanael West’s twin masterpieces, *Miss Lonelyhearts* and *The Day of the Locust*, packaged together in this essential 2009 edition, stand as the most terrifyingly prescient x-rays of the American Dream ever committed to print. Long before our modern era of manufactured despair and relentless media noise, West diagnosed the spiritual rot eating away at the twentieth-century psyche. Written during the Great Depression, these two short novels do not merely critique society; they eviscerate it, serving up a potent cocktail of dark comedy, apocalyptic dread, and stark, unyielding disillusionment. 

At the heart of both works lies the devastating theme of the broken promise. West explores what happens when human beings, fed on a steady diet of cheap mythologies and Hollywood fantasies, confront the cold, unyielding wall of reality. In *Miss Lonelyhearts*, the battlefield is the bustling, cynical landscape of New York City. The protagonist, a nameless male journalist trapped writing an advice column under a feminine pseudonym, is slowly crushed by the sheer, unmediated weight of human suffering. The letters he receives—desperate pleas from the crippled, the abused, and the utterly forsaken—become a tide of misery he cannot stem. Unable to offer genuine salvation, and tormented by his sadistic editor Shrike, Miss Lonelyhearts spirals into a Christ-like psychosis. Here, West dissects the failure of religion and modern empathy, showing how compassion, when stripped of true agency, becomes a form of torture.

If *Miss Lonelyhearts* is a scalpel cutting into the spiritual void of the East, *The Day of the Locust* is a stick of dynamite tossed into the false paradise of the West. Relocating his jaundiced eye to Hollywood, West turns his attention to the forgotten extras, the faded starlets, and the bitter drifters who migrated to California to die of boredom. Through the eyes of Tod Hackett, a painter sketching the fringe dwellers for a monumental canvas entitled *The Burning of Los Angeles*, West exposes the machinery of illusion. The characters—most notably the vapid, aspiring starlet Faye Greener and the tragic, twitching Homer Simpson—are trapped in a grotesque theater of unrealized desires. The American Dream is no longer a beacon of hope; it is a cruel joke that breeds a terrifying, dormant rage.

West’s literary style is as shocking today as it was in the 1930s—as the *Village Voice* aptly noted, like a glob of black bile vomited at a church social. He writes with a ruthless economy, blending jagged satire, surrealism, and grim naturalism. There is no room for sentimentality in West’s universe. He strips away the comforting illusions of narrative arc, favoring instead a relentless escalation toward catastrophe. His prose is stark, cinematic, and unflinching, operating with the precision of a nightmare. He captures the cacophony of the modern city and the hollow glare of the movie lot not with poetic flourish, but with brutal, jarring snapshots that leave the reader disoriented and raw.

To read Nathanael West is to look directly into the abyss of modern alienation. These novels remind us that beneath the glittering surface of civilization lies a volatile undercurrent of rage, emptiness, and despair. Unsparing, blasphemous, and fiercely brilliant, this volume is essential reading for anyone brave enough to confront the dark side of human ambition.
