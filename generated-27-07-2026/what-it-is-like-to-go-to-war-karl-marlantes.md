# What It is Like to Go to War

**Author:** Karl Marlantes  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2011

In "What It Is Like to Go to War," Karl Marlantes crafts a poignant and introspective memoir that delves into the unvarnished realities of war and its lasting impact on those who fight it. With unflinching candor, Marlantes recounts his experiences as a young lieutenant in the Vietnam War, where he was thrust into the midst of intense combat and forced to confront the harsh realities of leadership, mortality, and the human cost of conflict.

Through his narrative, Marlantes critiques the ways in which modern societies prepare their young soldiers for war, arguing that the ancient rituals, religious practices, and literary traditions that once helped warriors prepare for battle have given way to a more sanitized and technocratic approach to warfare. This, he contends, leaves young soldiers woefully unprepared for the psychological and spiritual challenges they will face on the battlefield.

Marlantes's writing is characterized by a lyrical prose that belies the brutality of the subject matter. His descriptions of combat are visceral and intense, conveying the chaos, fear, and adrenaline that accompany the experience of war. Yet, he also brings a philosopher's perspective to his narrative, drawing on a wide range of literary and cultural references to illuminate the timeless and universal aspects of the human experience.

One of the most striking aspects of Marlantes's memoir is his willingness to confront the darker aspects of his own experiences, including the killing of a young North Vietnamese soldier that continues to haunt him decades later. This episode serves as a powerful reminder of the enduring psychological toll of war, and the ways in which the memories of combat can continue to shape and define a person long after the fighting has stopped.

Throughout the book, Marlantes grapples with the complexities of war and its aftermath, seeking to understand how we might better prepare young soldiers for the challenges they will face, and how we might help them to heal and reintegrate into society upon their return. His conclusions are both sobering and thought-provoking, offering a nuanced and multifaceted exploration of the human cost of war.

Ultimately, "What It Is Like to Go to War" is a powerful and moving memoir that offers a searing indictment of the ways in which modern societies approach war, while also providing a testament to the enduring power of the human spirit. Marlantes's writing is both deeply personal and broadly resonant, making this a book that will appeal to anyone interested in the human experience of war, and the ways in which we might work to create a more just and compassionate world.
