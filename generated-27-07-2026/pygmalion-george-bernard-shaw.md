# Pygmalion

**Author:** George Bernard Shaw  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2012

Language, Class, and the Art of Reinvention: A Review of George Bernard Shaw’s Pygmalion

Long before it inspired the iconic melodies of My Fair Lady, George Bernard Shaw’s 1912 masterpiece Pygmalion stood as a razor-sharp theatrical indictment of Victorian and Edwardian British class obsession. At its core, the play is not merely a heartwarming tale of personal makeover, but a brilliant, biting critique of how society assigns human worth based entirely on the way a person speaks. Through the tumultuous relationship between phonetician Henry Higgins and flower girl Eliza Doolittle, Shaw exposes the profound artificiality of social stratification, proving that class distinctions are nothing more than constructed illusions sustained by speech and mannerism.

The plot hinges on a notorious wager: can Higgins transform a "draggle-tailed guttersnipe" into a duchess simply by teaching her to pronounce the English language with immaculate precision? Eliza, brilliantly abrasive and fiercely proud even in her destitution, undergoes this grueling linguistic metamorphosis. Yet, Shaw’s brilliance lies in refusing to offer a simple fairy-tale resolution. As Eliza sheds her Cockney accent, she discovers that her newly acquired refinement isolates her from her past without granting her true agency or belonging in the upper crust. She is trapped in a cruel liminal space, molded by a creator who views her as a scientific specimen rather than an autonomous human being. 

Shaw’s stylistic mastery is on full display throughout the text, driven by his trademark sparkling wit, relentless intellectual provocation, and unmatched facility for dialogue. Rather than relying on heavy-duty melodrama, Shaw uses sparkling repartee and philosophical friction to challenge his audience. He strips away romantic sentimentality, infusing his drama with social commentary that feels startlingly modern. Higgins is far from the idealized, dashing mentor; he is brilliantly infuriating, emotionally detached, and entirely oblivious to the human cost of his experiment. By subverting the classical myth of Pygmalion—where the sculptor falls hopelessly in love with his inanimate creation—Shaw constructs a complex dynamic where the "creation" ultimately demands her independence from a suffocatingly arrogant master.

This Washington Square Press Enriched Classics edition elevates the reading experience by contextualizing Shaw’s revolutionary ideas for contemporary audiences. Featuring insightful critical analysis by Eric Bentley, alongside historical background, illuminating notes, and period illustrations, this volume bridges the gap between Edwardian London and the modern reader. It reminds us that while accents and fashion trends may shift over the decades, society's persistent obsession with superficial markers of status remains remarkably unchanged. 

Pygmalion is a triumph of theatrical intellect that forces us to question the foundations of our own social biases. It asks a timeless, unsettling question: does changing a person's voice truly change who they are, or does it merely alter how a judgmental world chooses to receive them? Sharp, funny, and profoundly perceptive, Shaw’s classic remains essential reading for anyone interested in the complex intersections of language, power, and human identity.
