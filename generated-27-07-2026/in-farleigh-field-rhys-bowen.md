# In Farleigh Field

**Author:** Rhys Bowen  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2013

Domestic bliss, as it turns out, can be a terribly dull affair for a woman accustomed to the shadows of the criminal underworld. In Rhys Bowen’s captivating short piece, the acclaimed author temporarily co-opts the universe of her famous *In Farleigh Field* milieu to deliver a sharp, charming, and atmospheric vignette that proves once again why she remains a master of historical cozy mysteries. 

The narrative centers on Molly Sullivan—formerly the fiercely independent private investigator Molly Murphy—who has recently settled into matrimony with her beloved Daniel. Yet, the transition from sleuth to housewife leaves much to be desired. The thrill of the chase has been replaced by the monotony of sweeping, dusting, and domestic management. Bowen brilliantly captures the quiet desperation of a sharp mind suffocating in the rigid gender expectations of early twentieth-century domesticity. It is a subtle yet powerful theme that runs through much of Bowen's work: the tension between societal roles and personal identity. 

The turning point arrives with a dash of clandestine rebellion. When Molly secretly attends a suffragist meeting alongside her loyal and eccentric friends Sid and Gus, the narrative shifts from a domestic portrait into something altogether more haunting. There, she encounters a shy, visibly distressed woman with a bizarre and chilling claim: she lives in a haunted house. What begins as a momentary distraction quickly spirals into an intriguing puzzle, offering Molly the very adrenaline rush she has been secretly craving. 

Bowen’s literary style is on full display here. Even within the compact format of a short story, she effortlessly resurrects a bygone era with sensory-rich prose and pitch-perfect historical detail. The atmosphere is thick with the scent of damp London pavements, the fervor of the suffrage movement, and the eerie, psychological chill of the unknown. Her pacing is brisk, ensuring that readers are hooked from the moment Molly’s boredom is disrupted to the final, satisfying reveal. 

While technically serving as a delightful bridge to tide fans over between major releases, "The Face in the Mirror" stands firmly on its own as a masterclass in miniature mystery writing. It explores the themes of female empowerment, friendship, and the irrepressible nature of curiosity. Bowen reminds us that true detectives never truly hang up their hats, no matter what marriage certificate they happen to sign. 

For both longtime devotees of Molly Murphy and newcomers looking for a quick, atmospheric literary escape, this short story is an absolute treat. It is witty, suspenseful, and ultimately a celebration of women who refuse to be confined to the parlor.
