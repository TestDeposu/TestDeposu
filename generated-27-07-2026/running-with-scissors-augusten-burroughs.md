# Running with Scissors

**Author:** Augusten Burroughs  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2010

"Running with Scissors" is not just a memoir; it's a wild, unapologetic ride into the mind of Augusten Burroughs, a boy who navigates the labyrinthine world of his mother's psychiatrist with a resilience that is as astonishing as it is heartbreaking. Burroughs' prose is a whirlwind of wit, sarcasm, and raw honesty, a style that is as addictive as it is unsettling.

The main theme of "Running with Scissors" is the search for a family, a home, and a sense of belonging. Burroughs' mother, a poet with delusions of grandeur, gives him away to her psychiatrist, Dr. Finch, and his family. The Finches are a motley crew of misfits, living in a dilapidated Victorian house that is as chaotic as it is comforting. Despite the squalor and the bizarre behavior of its inhabitants, the house becomes a sanctuary for Burroughs, a place where he can be himself, even if that self is a confused, hurting child.

Burroughs' style is a unique blend of dark humor and stark honesty. He doesn't shy away from the grotesque or the absurd, instead embracing it with a wry wit that is both disarming and disturbing. He paints a vivid picture of his childhood, not with rose-tinted glasses, but with a stark, unflinching gaze that is both courageous and compelling.

"Running with Scissors" is not an easy read. It's a book that will make you laugh out loud one moment and want to cry the next. It's a book that will challenge you, make you uncomfortable, and force you to confront the darker side of human nature. But it's also a book that will stay with you, long after you've turned the last page. It's a testament to the human spirit, a story of survival and resilience in the face of adversity.

In the end, "Running with Scissors" is a book that defies categorization. It's a memoir, a coming-of-age story, a dark comedy, and a tragedy all rolled into one. It's a book that will make you question, make you think, and make you feel. And isn't that what the best books are supposed to do?
