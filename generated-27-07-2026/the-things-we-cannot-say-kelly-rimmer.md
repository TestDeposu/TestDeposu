# The Things We Cannot Say

**Author:** Kelly Rimmer  
**Page Count:** Unknown  
**Publication Date:** 2019

Title: Echoes Across Decades: A Review of Kelly Rimmer’s The Things We Cannot Say

In the crowded landscape of historical fiction, novels set against the backdrop of World War II often struggle to carve out a distinct emotional footprint. Yet, with her 2019 bestseller The Things We Cannot Say, Kelly Rimmer achieves precisely that, delivering a poignant exploration of memory, silence, and the resilience of the human spirit. Rimmer crafts a dual-narrative masterpiece that bridges the frantic, modern pace of contemporary life with the agonizing, slow-motion devastation of Nazi-occupied Poland, proving once again why she is a master of emotional storytelling.

At the heart of Rimmer’s narrative style is the delicate interplay between what is spoken and what is deliberately concealed. The novel’s central theme is anchored in its title: the profound weight of secrets, trauma, and the protective yet destructive nature of silence. Rimmer examines how suppressed truths can ripple across generations, quietly dictating the fears, decisions, and identities of descendants who know nothing of the original wounds. 

In the historical timeline, we are introduced to fifteen-year-old Alina Dziak in 1942. Rimmer’s prose is strikingly visceral as she transports the reader from the innocent, idyllic dreams of a young girl eagerly awaiting her childhood sweetheart, Tomasz, to the crushing, claustrophobic reality of the Nazi occupation. The author does not shy away from the brutal injustices that dismantled Polish villages, yet she manages to keep the narrative intensely personal. Instead of focusing solely on the grand, macro-level horrors of the war, Rimmer hones in on the micro-tragedies: the agonizing spaces between hope and despair, the deafening silence of a missing loved one, and the daily compromises made simply to survive. Alina’s voice is remarkably authentic—stubbornly hopeful, fiercely loyal, and ultimately unforgettable.

Alternating with Alina’s harrowing past is a modern-day storyline that grounds the historical tragedy in contemporary stakes. This structural choice prevents the novel from becoming a purely historical exercise, reminding readers that the echoes of 1942 still reverberate loudly in the present. Rimmer handles the transition between eras with grace, maintaining narrative momentum and emotional resonance in both timelines. As the modern protagonist embarks on an urgent search for family answers, the reader is taken on a dual journey of discovery that ultimately uncovers hard-won truths about self-acceptance and resilience.

Rimmer’s writing style is characterized by its emotional accessibility and finely wrought character development. She possesses an innate ability to make historical suffering deeply relatable, forcing us to confront a haunting question: how long does it take to find our voice after a lifetime of enforced silence, and how do we finally learn to trust it? 

Fans of sweeping historical dramas in the vein of Kristin Hannah and Anthony Doerr will find much to admire here, but Rimmer’s unique voice ensures this novel stands firmly on its own merits. The Things We Cannot Say is an unshakable, tear-jerking reminder of the devastation caused when truth is silenced, and a triumphant testament to the power of love that refuses to be forgotten. It is a compelling, beautifully paced read that lingers long after the final page is turned.
